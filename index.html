EKG Acquisition

In an outpatient cardiac rehabilitation program, a technician checks a student's setup before a resting 12-lead. The student counted correctly to the fourth intercostal space but placed V1 and V2 in line with the nipples, several centimeters out from each side of the sternum. Which correction should the technician make?

  1. Move both onto the center of the sternum, one above the other
  2. Keep them in place, since the fourth space is correct
  3. Move them down to the fifth space at the same distance
  4. Slide each electrode inward to the edge of the sternum

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The center of the sternum is neither the V1 nor the V2 site, and stacking both electrodes there makes the two leads record nearly the same view.
  • B. The fourth space sets the height, but the sternal edge sets the side-to-side position; electrodes this far out look at the heart from sites closer to V3 and V4.
  • C. The fifth space is the V4 level, so lowering the electrodes adds a second error to the first instead of correcting it.
  • D. Correct. V1 and V2 sit in the fourth intercostal space at the right and left sternal edges, so the electrodes keep their height and move inward until the edge of the sternum is felt.

V1 and V2 are defined by two landmarks: the fourth intercostal space for height and the sternal edge for the side-to-side position. Recording guidance describes counting down to the fourth space, then sliding the finger toward the sternum until its edge is felt, which is where the center of the electrode goes. The nipple is not a landmark because its position varies with build, age and sex. Septal electrodes that drift outward view the heart from the wrong place, which can produce misleading changes in the septal leads.

Administering Cardiac Tests

In a cardiology clinic, a technician checks a resting 12-lead before passing it to the provider. The calibration mark at the start of each row is 20 mm tall, and the tallest R wave in lead V5 measures 24 mm. What is the true amplitude of that R wave?

  1. 2.4 mV
  2. 0.12 mV
  3. 4.8 mV
  4. 1.2 mV

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. This reading assumes the standard gain of 10 mm/mV, where each millimeter is 0.1 mV; the doubled calibration mark shows the complexes are printed at twice their standard size.
  • B. This slips a decimal place: at 20 mm/mV each millimeter is 0.05 mV, not 0.005 mV.
  • C. This doubles the measured height instead of halving it, correcting for the gain in the wrong direction.
  • D. Correct. A 20 mm mark for the 1 mV test pulse means the gain is 20 mm/mV, so each millimeter is 0.05 mV and 24 mm is 1.2 mV.

The calibration (standardization) mark shows how tall a 1 mV signal prints, so it is the ruler for every amplitude on that tracing. At the standard gain of 10 mm/mV a 1 mV pulse is 10 mm tall and each small box is 0.1 mV. Here the mark is 20 mm, so the machine is at double gain and every deflection prints twice its standard height: 24 mm ÷ 20 mm per mV = 1.2 mV, which would have measured 12 mm at standard gain. Voltage criteria such as those for hypertrophy assume 10 mm/mV, so the technician resets the gain to standard for routine tracings, or makes sure a nonstandard gain is printed and pointed out to the reader.

Abnormal Rhythms

At a telemetry central station, a monitor technician reviews the strip of a 72-year-old patient with second-degree AV block. Sinus P waves march regularly at 80/min, and the conducted beats have a PR of 0.24 s and a narrow QRS. After one P wave fails to conduct, a QRS identical to the conducted beats appears 1.34 s after the previous QRS, 0.08 s after the next P wave. Which label BEST fits this beat?

  1. Junctional escape beat
  2. Premature junctional complex
  3. Conducted sinus beat
  4. Ventricular escape beat

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. It ends a pause, comes about 1.34 s after the last beat (an escape rate near 45/min), and matches the narrow conducted QRS. A P wave only 0.08 s before it is too close to have conducted in a patient whose conducted beats need 0.24 s.
  • B. The narrow QRS without a conducted P wave fits the junction, but a premature beat comes before the next beat is due. This one comes late, at the end of a pause.
  • C. The P wave just in front of it makes this tempting, but an AV node that needs 0.24 s on every conducted beat cannot pass an impulse in 0.08 s. The P wave and the QRS only happen to land together.
  • D. A ventricular escape also ends pauses, but it starts below the bundle of His and gives a wide QRS of a new shape. This QRS matches the conducted beats.

Escape beats rescue the heart when conducted beats fail to arrive, and LITFL lists high-grade second-degree and third-degree AV block among the causes of junctional escape. Two checks sort a beat like this one. Timing: a beat that ends a pause is an escape, while one that comes early is premature. PR: a PR far shorter than the patient's own conducted PR means the P wave did not conduct, even though it sits just in front of the QRS. A narrow QRS that matches the conducted beats places the escape in the AV junction, which Merck says fires at about 35 to 50/min. The technician reports the block and the escape beats to the nurse.

Stress Testing

At a cardiology office, a 63-year-old scheduled for a treadmill test next week says, "I haven't jogged in 20 years. Will I have to run the whole time?" Which explanation is MOST accurate?

  1. You keep going for the full 60 minutes, however tired you feel
  2. You run at one steady speed for a set time
  3. The belt keeps one speed while the slope rises
  4. You start at a walk, and about every 3 minutes the pace and slope increase

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. About 60 minutes is the length of the whole visit, including preparation and recovery, not the exercise. Exercise stops at the target heart rate, or earlier for symptoms, exhaustion or worrying changes.
  • B. Many patients picture a jog at one pace. The protocol is staged instead, starting slowly and building, so the heart's response can be seen at rising workloads.
  • C. The slope does rise, but the speed rises too. Telling the patient that just the slope changes would leave him surprised when the belt speeds up.
  • D. Correct. The test begins at a walk, and about every 3 minutes the speed and incline increase. It ends at the target heart rate, or earlier if symptoms or worrying changes appear.

A clear, honest picture of the test lowers anxiety and helps the patient prepare. On a treadmill test the patient starts walking, and about every 3 minutes the pace and incline increase. Exercise ends at the target heart rate, or sooner for chest pain, a worrying blood-pressure or ECG change, leg pain or exhaustion. Monitoring continues for 10 to 15 minutes afterward, or until the heart rate returns to baseline. Advise comfortable walking shoes and loose clothing.

The exams

EKG Tech Basics covers NHA CET, CCI CRAT, CCI CCT. Switch exams any time; mock exams follow each exam's length, time limit and topic proportions.

NHA

Certified EKG Technician (CET)

500+ practice questions · 120-question mock exams, 2 h limit

The NHA Certified EKG Technician (CET) exam has 120 questions (100 scored and 20 unscored pretest questions) with a 2-hour limit. Topic proportions follow the NHA CET test plan now in use (2017 job analysis). NHA has published a revised plan that takes over in late 2027 or winter 2027-28 (NHA has not named a date); its weights differ by a few points.

CCI

Certified Rhythm Analysis Technician (CRAT)

350+ practice questions · 130-question mock exams, 1 h 50 min limit

The CCI Certified Rhythm Analysis Technician (CRAT) exam has 130 questions (110 scored and 20 unscored) with 1 hour 50 minutes of testing time. Topic proportions follow the CCI CRAT examination matrix. The real exam mixes four-option multiple choice with multiple-response, hot-spot and drag-and-place questions; this mock uses multiple choice only.

CCI

Certified Cardiographic Technician (CCT)

The real exam mixes four-option multiple choice with multiple-response, hot-spot and drag-and-place questions; this mock uses multiple choice only.
