Question 1

In a cardiology clinic, a technician records a follow-up 12-lead ECG on a 63-year-old with no symptoms. The QRS is 0.13 s, with an rsR′ complex in V1 and a wide, slurred S wave in leads I and V6. The T waves are inverted in V1 and V2, and the tracing matches one from 6 months ago. Which statement about the T waves in V1 and V2 is correct?

  1. They mark acute anterior wall ischemia
  2. They are an expected secondary change
  3. They show a digoxin effect on repolarization
  4. They point to a low serum potassium

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. T inversion in V1 and V2 can come from ischemia, which makes this tempting, but here it follows a right bundle branch block QRS and is unchanged from 6 months ago in a patient with no symptoms.
  • B. Correct. In right bundle branch block the delayed right ventricular activation alters repolarization, so ST depression and T inversion in V1 to V3 are appropriate discordance.
  • C. Digoxin causes sagging, scooped ST depression with flattened T waves, mostly in the lateral leads, and no medication history here suggests it.
  • D. Low potassium flattens T waves, adds prominent U waves and can depress the ST segment across many leads, rather than inverting the T waves in V1 and V2 alone.

A QRS over 120 ms with an rsR′ in V1 and a wide, slurred S wave in I and V6 is complete right bundle branch block. Because the right ventricle depolarizes late and abnormally, it also repolarizes abnormally, and LITFL lists ST depression or T-wave inversion in the right precordial leads (V1 to V3) as the expected secondary change, called appropriate discordance. The technician still passes the tracing on for reading, but these T waves do not by themselves suggest new ischemia, particularly when they match the prior tracing; a new change or symptoms would be reported promptly.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 2

At a cardiology clinic, a technician screens a 62-year-old woman's 12-lead ECG, recorded at 10 mm/mV, for left ventricular hypertrophy. Data are: R wave in aVL 8 mm; S wave in V3 10 mm; QRS duration 100 ms. Which conclusion about the two Cornell criteria is correct?

  1. Neither applies with a QRS under 120 ms
  2. Neither met, with a product of 1,800 mm·ms
  3. Product met at 2,600 mm·ms; voltage not met
  4. Both met, at 26 mm and 2,600 mm·ms

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A wide QRS is not a requirement. The product uses QRS duration as one of its terms, so a normal-width QRS of 100 ms simply enters the multiplication.
  • B. This leaves out the 8 mm added for women in the product: 18 × 100 = 1,800 mm·ms. With the correction the product reaches 2,600 mm·ms.
  • C. Correct. Voltage is 8 + 10 = 18 mm, under the 20 mm cutoff for women. The product adds 8 mm for women: (8 + 10 + 8) × 100 = 2,600 mm·ms, above 2,440.
  • D. The 8 mm correction belongs to the product, not to the voltage criterion. Voltage stays at 18 mm, which does not reach the 20 mm limit for women.

Cornell voltage adds the R wave in aVL to the S wave in V3, with cutoffs above 28 mm in men and 20 mm in women. The Cornell voltage-duration product multiplies that sum by the QRS duration in milliseconds, after adding 8 mm for women, and is met above 2,440 mm·ms. Because hypertrophy often widens the QRS slightly, the product can pick up cases that voltage alone misses. The technician reports which criterion is met, measured at standard gain, so the reader can weigh it.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 3

In a sports cardiology clinic, a technician records a resting 12-lead for a 30-year-old marathon runner with no symptoms. The rhythm is sinus at 52/min. In V2 and V3, each upright T wave 5 mm tall is followed by a separate upright hump 0.5 mm tall; the next P wave arrives on time and no pause follows. Which interpretation fits the hump?

  1. A blocked premature atrial beat
  2. A prominent U wave from low potassium
  3. A notched T wave from QT prolongation
  4. A normal U wave

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A nonconducted PAC comes early, deforms the T wave and is followed by a pause; this hump repeats after every beat and the next P wave is on time.
  • B. A prominent U wave is taller than 1 to 2 mm or more than 25% of the T-wave height, often with flattened T waves; this one is 0.5 mm, a tenth of the T wave.
  • C. The hump is separate from the T wave rather than fused into it, so the T wave itself ends normally and the QT is measured to its end.
  • D. Correct. A small upright wave after an upright T, best seen in V2 and V3 and easier to see at slow rates, is a normal U wave.

A normal U wave is a small deflection right after the T wave, pointing the same way and usually well under the 1 to 2 mm upper normal limit. It is best seen in V2 and V3, and it grows as the heart rate slows, so it usually becomes visible below about 65/min, as in this trained runner at 52/min. It turns abnormal when it is prominent (more than 1 to 2 mm or 25% of the T-wave height, as with severe hypokalemia or some drugs) or inverted in a lead with an upright T wave, which is a specific sign of heart disease or ischemia.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 4

In a pre-operative testing clinic, a 74-year-old man with diabetes and high blood pressure has a 12-lead ECG before knee surgery. He has no chest pain. The machine statement reads 'early repolarization.' The tracing shows 1 mm of concave ST elevation in V2 to V4, a notched J point in V4, upright T waves that follow the QRS, and no reciprocal ST depression. Which detail MOST argues against accepting the machine statement without review?

  1. The notched J point in V4
  2. His age and cardiac risk factors
  3. T waves concordant with the QRS
  4. The absence of reciprocal ST depression

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A notched or slurred fish-hook J point, best seen in V4, is a hallmark of early repolarization. It supports the machine statement rather than arguing against it.
  • B. Correct. Benign early repolarization is a pattern of people under 50 and is particularly rare over 70; LITFL advises against making the diagnosis after 50, especially with risk factors for ischemic heart disease.
  • C. Prominent T waves that point the same way as the QRS are part of the early repolarization pattern, so this detail fits the machine statement.
  • D. Reciprocal depression would point toward infarction; its absence is what early repolarization shows. It does not count against the statement, although it cannot exclude ischemia by itself.

LITFL describes benign early repolarization as concave ST elevation most marked in V2 to V5, a fish-hook J point in V4, prominent concordant T waves and no reciprocal depression, and says it is common in healthy people under 50, less common after 50 and particularly rare over 70. Its advice is to avoid the diagnosis in patients over 50, especially those with ischemic risk factors. A 74-year-old with diabetes and hypertension therefore needs the tracing flagged for the reader and compared with earlier ECGs, because the same morphology can be ischemia.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 5

In a pre-operative clinic, a technician records a 12-lead ECG on a 74-year-old before hernia repair. The QRS is 0.13 s with an rsR′ in V1 and a wide, slurred S wave in V6. Lead I has an upright P wave and an rS complex, leads II, III and aVF show qR complexes, the axis is +110°, and the PR interval is 0.16 s. As the electrodes come off, the patient mentions nearly passing out twice this week. Which action should the technician take FIRST?

  1. Send it to be read with the pre-op packet
  2. Attach a Holter monitor before the patient leaves
  3. Report tracing and symptoms to the provider now
  4. Repeat it after checking the arm electrode positions

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. The patient is calm and seated, which makes routine handling tempting, but bifascicular block with near-fainting needs a provider's attention before the patient leaves.
  • B. Ambulatory monitoring might be ordered later, but a Holter needs a provider's order, and starting one does not get the symptom history in front of the provider today.
  • C. Correct. Right bundle branch block with right axis deviation fits bifascicular block, and with near-syncope it carries a high risk of progressing to complete heart block, so the provider needs both facts at once.
  • D. A negative lead I can come from swapped arm wires, but the upright P wave in lead I argues against a reversal, and repeating the tracing delays the report.

A QRS over 120 ms with an rsR′ in V1 and a wide, slurred S wave in V6 is right bundle branch block, which by itself leaves the axis normal. A right axis of +110° with rS in lead I and qR in the inferior leads adds a left posterior fascicular block, once other causes such as right ventricular hypertrophy are excluded: that is bifascicular block, with the left anterior fascicle carrying conduction alone. LITFL gives a progression rate to complete heart block of about 1% a year without symptoms but about 17% a year after syncope, and treats syncope or presyncope with bifascicular block as a reason for admission and monitoring. The technician's role is to put the tracing and the history in front of the provider promptly.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 6

In a cardiology clinic, a technician reviews a resting 12-lead ECG for a 38-year-old patient before a routine visit. The rhythm is sinus at 74/min with a narrow QRS. T waves are upright in I, II, aVF and V2 to V6 and inverted in aVR and V1, and the P waves and QRS complexes in aVR are negative as well. Which statement about the T waves is accurate?

  1. Abnormal in aVR, suggesting arm lead reversal
  2. Normal for these leads
  3. Abnormal in V1, suggesting anterior ischemia
  4. Abnormal in both, suggesting delayed repolarization

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Arm reversal makes aVR look upright and lead I inverted. Here aVR is negative throughout, as expected, so the inverted T wave in aVR is the normal state, not a reversal sign.
  • B. Correct. A normal T wave is upright in most leads but inverted in aVR, and it may be inverted in V1. This tracing matches that pattern.
  • C. T-wave inversion across the precordial leads can mean ischemia, which makes this tempting, but an inverted T wave in V1 alone is a normal finding. Ischemic inversion would spread into V2 to V4.
  • D. Delayed repolarization lengthens the QT interval; it does not explain T waves that point down only in the two leads that normally show them that way.

The T wave records ventricular repolarization and usually points the same way as the main QRS deflection. Lead aVR looks at the heart from the right shoulder, so its P wave, QRS and T wave are normally all negative. V1 sits over the right side of the heart and often shows an inverted T wave in healthy adults, and lead III may also be inverted as a normal variant. A technician should flag new inversion in leads that are normally upright, such as V2 to V6 or I and II, not inversion in aVR or V1.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 7

At an internal medicine clinic, a technician measures a 58-year-old man's 12-lead ECG at standard gain. The R wave in aVL is 10 mm and the S wave in V3 is 14 mm. The S wave in V1 is 16 mm, the R wave in V5 is 22 mm, and the R wave in V6 is 19 mm. Which conclusion about left ventricular hypertrophy voltage is correct?

  1. Both Cornell and Sokolow-Lyon criteria met
  2. Neither met; Sokolow-Lyon needs 40 mm
  3. Sokolow-Lyon met; Cornell not met
  4. Cornell met; Sokolow-Lyon not met

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Sokolow-Lyon is met, but 24 mm passes Cornell only under the over-20-mm threshold for women. For a man the threshold is over 28 mm.
  • B. Cornell is not met, but the Sokolow-Lyon threshold is 35 mm, not 40 mm, so 38 mm meets it.
  • C. Correct. Sokolow-Lyon is 16 + 22 = 38 mm, at least 35 mm, while Cornell is 10 + 14 = 24 mm, short of the over-28-mm threshold for men.
  • D. This reverses both results: Cornell falls short at 24 mm in a man, and Sokolow-Lyon reaches 38 mm against a 35 mm threshold.

The Cornell voltage criterion adds the R wave in aVL to the S wave in V3, and LITFL sets the threshold at over 28 mm in men and over 20 mm in women, so the patient's sex has to be known before the sum is judged; 24 mm falls short in this man. Sokolow-Lyon adds the S wave in V1 to the larger R wave in V5 or V6 and needs 35 mm or more, so 16 + 22 = 38 mm meets it. Different criteria often disagree because each is insensitive, which is why the technician reports the measurements accurately and the reading physician makes the hypertrophy call with the full tracing and history.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 8

In a hospital stress lab, a 70-year-old woman's resting 12-lead shows sinus rhythm at 72/min with a QRS of 0.09 s. Late in stage 2, at a sinus rate of 128/min, the QRS abruptly widens to 0.14 s with a QS complex in V1 and broad, notched R waves without q waves in I, V5 and V6, each beat still preceded by a P wave at the same PR interval. In recovery the QRS narrows again once the rate falls below 105/min. Which interpretation BEST fits?

  1. Rate-related left bundle branch block
  2. Accelerated idioventricular rhythm
  3. Ventricular tachycardia starting at peak exercise
  4. Intermittent pre-excitation over an accessory pathway

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The wide complexes meet LBBB morphology, every one follows a sinus P wave at a fixed PR, and the block appears above a critical rate and clears when the rate slows, which defines a rate-related block.
  • B. AIVR is a ventricular rhythm of about 50 to 110/min that runs at its own rate without a fixed relation to P waves; these wide beats are sinus beats at 128/min.
  • C. VT is a wide rhythm from the ventricles that usually dissociates from the P waves; here each wide beat follows a sinus P wave at the same PR, and the rate did not jump when the QRS widened.
  • D. Pre-excitation also widens the QRS, but through a short PR and a slurred delta wave at the start of the QRS; the PR here is unchanged and the shape is a typical LBBB.

A rate-related, or rate-dependent, bundle branch block appears when the heart rate passes a critical value at which one bundle can no longer recover in time, and it clears when the rate slows. A published case describes LBBB appearing with faster rates during exercise and the QRS narrowing again at lower rates. The criteria are those of a fixed LBBB: a QRS of 0.12 s or more, a dominant S or QS in V1, and broad lateral R waves without q waves. While the block is present the ST segments cannot be read for ischemia by the usual rules, so the technician tells the supervising physician and records the rates at which the block began and ended.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 9

A 57-year-old woman at a pre-operative testing appointment for knee surgery mentions "indigestion" since this morning. Her 12-lead ECG shows ST elevation of 3 mm in V1 through V4. Which action should the technician take FIRST?

  1. Stay with her and have a provider read it right now
  2. Re-prep the chest sites and repeat the tracing
  3. Send it with her pre-op packet for the surgeon
  4. Add right-sided leads before anyone reads it

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The tracing shows an acute anterior injury pattern in a patient with a possible ischemic symptom, so a provider needs it immediately while she stays under watch.
  • B. Tempting because poor skin contact can distort the baseline, but matching ST elevation across four contiguous chest leads is a pattern, not noise, and repeating the tracing first delays the read.
  • C. A pre-op tracing normally goes in the packet, but this one shows an acute injury pattern and cannot wait for a routine review.
  • D. Right-sided leads such as V4R look for right ventricular involvement in inferior infarction; they do not fit this pattern and they delay the read.

ST elevation in V1 to V4 is the anteroseptal pattern of an acute anterior infarction, usually from the left anterior descending artery. Three millimeters exceeds the STEMI threshold in V2 and V3 for a woman (1.5 mm) and appears in more than two contiguous leads, and her "indigestion" may be her ischemic symptom. The technician's role is to recognize that this tracing cannot wait in the routine stack: keep her where she is, get a provider to read it immediately, and follow the facility's emergency process. If she becomes unresponsive and pulseless, staff start CPR and call the code team.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library: Anterior Myocardial Infarction; Fifth Universal Definition of MI (2026)

Question 10

In a cardiology clinic, a technician reviews a 12-lead on a 76-year-old patient referred for dizzy spells. The QRS is 0.13 s with an rsR′ in V1 and wide S waves in I and V6. The frontal axis is −60°, with qR complexes in I and aVL and rS complexes in II, III and aVF. Which conduction pattern do these findings BEST fit?

  1. Left bundle branch block with left axis deviation
  2. RBBB with left anterior fascicular block
  3. RBBB with left posterior fascicular block
  4. Isolated right bundle branch block

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. LBBB can come with a left axis, but it shows a dominant S wave in V1 and broad monophasic R waves in I and V6, not an rsR′ and wide lateral S waves.
  • B. Correct. The rsR′ in V1 with wide lateral S waves is right bundle branch block, and an axis of −60° with qR in I and aVL and rS in the inferior leads adds left anterior fascicular block: a bifascicular block.
  • C. This is also a bifascicular block, but left posterior fascicular block shifts the axis to the right, not to −60°.
  • D. The right bundle pattern is correct, but isolated RBBB keeps a normal axis; an axis of −60° means a second fascicle is involved.

Clinically, bifascicular block means right bundle branch block plus a block of one left fascicle: the anterior fascicle (left axis deviation) or the posterior fascicle (right axis deviation). LITFL defines left anterior fascicular block by an axis of −45° to −90° with qR in I and aVL and rS in II, III and aVF. LITFL puts progression to complete heart block at about 1% a year in patients without symptoms and about 17% a year in those who present with syncope, so the technician reports the pattern together with the dizzy spells to the physician.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library
