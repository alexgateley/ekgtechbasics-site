Question 1

At check-in for a treadmill stress test in a cardiology office, a technician reviews the preparation instructions with a 58-year-old man. Which statement from the patient should the technician report to the supervising provider before the test begins?

  1. I wore my walking shoes and loose shorts today.
  2. I took sildenafil last night.
  3. I had toast and juice about six hours ago.
  4. I took my usual pills this morning, as my cardiologist said to.

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Comfortable shoes and loose clothing are exactly what the preparation instructions ask for, so this is good preparation, not a concern.
  • B. Correct. Patients are asked to tell the provider about sildenafil, tadalafil or vardenafil taken in the past 24 to 48 hours, because these drugs interact dangerously with nitrates that may be needed for chest pain during or after the test.
  • C. A light meal six hours earlier fits the usual instruction of nothing to eat for at least 3 hours, and even the longer fasts some labs ask for, so this does not need to be reported.
  • D. Patients are told to ask their provider whether to take their regular medicines and not to stop any on their own; he followed his cardiologist's instructions.

Stress test preparation usually means nothing to eat, smoke or drink with caffeine or alcohol for at least 3 hours, often no caffeine for 24 hours, comfortable shoes and loose clothes, and taking regular medicines only as the provider directs. Erectile dysfunction drugs taken within 24 to 48 hours must be reported, because nitroglycerin given for chest pain during the test can then drop the blood pressure severely. The technician does not decide whether the test proceeds; the supervising provider does, once informed.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; MedlinePlus Drug Information: Sildenafil (2023)

Question 2

At a community hospital stress lab, a 63-year-old patient in stage 3 of a Bruce protocol has reached 78% of his target heart rate when he says he has to stop because his legs are exhausted. He has no chest pain, and the ECG shows no new changes. A coworker says the test will not be diagnostic unless he reaches the target. Which action is BEST?

  1. Ask him to rate his exertion and go on if it is under 17
  2. Urge him to finish the stage so he reaches his target
  3. Honor his request and alert the supervising provider
  4. Lower the incline and keep him walking

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. The Borg scale helps judge effort, and a rating above 17 marks a maximal effort, but no rating overrides a patient's request to stop.
  • B. Tempting because a test stopped below 85% of predicted maximum is less conclusive, but the request to stop outranks the target, and pushing an exhausted patient risks a fall.
  • C. Correct. A patient's request to stop is an absolute indication to end an exercise test, whatever the heart rate; the provider then decides how to report a submaximal test and whether another kind of stress test is needed.
  • D. Changing the incline breaks the Bruce protocol and still overrides his request to stop; the cool-down that follows the test is a separate step.

Exercise test guidance lists the patient's request to stop among the absolute indications for ending the test, alongside central nervous system symptoms such as dizziness or near-fainting, signs of poor perfusion, and technical trouble monitoring the ECG or blood pressure. Fatigue and leg cramps are listed as relative reasons, but the patient's wish settles the matter because a test cannot continue without consent. The target heart rate, often 85% of the age-predicted maximum, is a goal, not a requirement. The technician stops the exercise as the lab's procedure directs, keeps monitoring through recovery, and tells the provider, who interprets the submaximal result.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3

In a hospital nuclear cardiology lab, a 68-year-old man received a regadenoson injection for a SPECT perfusion scan 3 minutes ago and reports chest tightness. His resting tracing had flat, normal ST segments. Data are: BP 122/74 mm Hg, down from 134/80; heart rate 92/min, sinus rhythm; ST segment in V4 to V6 now 1.5 mm below the PR baseline, horizontal, read 80 ms after the J point. What should the technician do NEXT?

  1. Report the ST depression to the physician at once
  2. Report it once the perfusion images are done
  3. Recheck the ST level once his chest tightness eases
  4. Log it as a common regadenoson effect and keep watching

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Horizontal ST depression of 1 mm or more at 80 ms after the J point is the usual ischemic criterion, and with chest tightness after a vasodilator it can signal ischemia the label warns about, so the physician needs to know now.
  • B. The images do show where blood flow is reduced, which makes waiting tempting, but they are read later; an ischemic ECG change with symptoms during stress is reported in real time.
  • C. Waiting for the symptom to pass seems reasonable because many reactions fade within minutes, but delaying the report leaves the physician unaware of possible ischemia while it is happening.
  • D. Chest discomfort and a small blood pressure fall are common after regadenoson, which makes this tempting, but new horizontal ST depression is an ischemic change, not a routine side effect, and it is reported rather than only logged.

The usual criterion for pathological ST depression during stress testing is horizontal or downsloping depression of at least 1 mm, read 80 ms after the J point, in consecutive beats; here it measures 1.5 mm in three lateral leads. The Lexiscan label lists chest discomfort, dyspnea, flushing and headache as common reactions, but it also lists angina or ST-segment depression and warns that myocardial infarction, ventricular arrhythmias and cardiac arrest have followed the injection, so resuscitation equipment and trained staff must be ready. Merck notes that prognosis worsens with deeper ST depression. The technician measures the change, reports it at once and keeps recording the 12-lead and blood pressure for the physician.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 4

In a cardiology clinic stress lab, a 60-year-old man who takes no rate-slowing medicines walks on a Bruce protocol until he stops for exhaustion with a Borg rating of 19. Data are: resting heart rate 70/min; peak heart rate 120/min; no ST changes or arrhythmias. The lab predicts maximum heart rate as 220 minus age. Which finding should the technician flag for the reading physician?

  1. Chronotropic incompetence
  2. Submaximal effort by the patient
  3. A normal heart rate response
  4. Beta-blocker effect on the heart rate

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. His predicted maximum is 160/min, and 120/min is 75% of it, below the 80% cutoff; his chronotropic index, (120 − 70) ÷ (160 − 70), is about 56%, also below 80%.
  • B. A low peak heart rate often comes from stopping early, which makes this tempting, but a Borg rating of 19, near the top of the 6 to 20 scale, shows he worked to exhaustion.
  • C. His heart rate did rise with exercise, but it peaked well short of the expected range; 85% of 160 would be 136/min, so the rise was inadequate.
  • D. Beta-blockers are a common reason a heart rate fails to climb, but this patient takes no rate-slowing medicines, so a drug effect does not explain it.

Chronotropic incompetence is the heart's failure to raise its rate enough to meet the body's demand during exercise. Exercise testing reviews define it as failing to reach at least 80% of the age-predicted maximum heart rate, or a chronotropic index (the heart rate reserve used divided by the reserve predicted) under 80%. Here 220 − 60 = 160, and a peak of 120/min reached at true exhaustion falls short on both measures. It predicts cardiovascular events and death and can reflect sinus node disease, autonomic dysfunction, heart failure or a drug effect, so the technician flags it for the physician to interpret.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 5

During stage 3 of a Bruce protocol treadmill stress test, a 67-year-old patient reports new chest pressure. Data are: systolic BP at rest 136 mm Hg, at the end of stage 2 164 mm Hg, now 148 mm Hg by manual cuff; heart rate 128/min, below his target heart rate. Which action should the technician take FIRST?

  1. Keep him walking until he reaches his target heart rate
  2. Recheck the BP with the automated cuff at the end of the stage
  3. Have him rate the pressure on the 0 to 10 scale and continue
  4. Alert the supervising provider to the BP drop and chest pressure now

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Reaching the target heart rate is a goal of the test, not a requirement that outranks warning signs, so symptoms and a falling pressure end the test earlier.
  • B. Confirming a reading is reasonable, but the manual reading already shows the drop, automated cuffs are less reliable during treadmill motion, and waiting for the end of the stage delays action.
  • C. Symptom scales are used during stress testing, which makes this tempting, but rating the pain while the pressure falls lets a stop criterion pass without action.
  • D. Correct. A systolic fall of more than 10 mm Hg as the workload rises, together with chest pressure, is a reason to stop the test, so the provider is told at once while the technician stays with the patient.

Systolic pressure normally rises with each Bruce stage, because the treadmill's speed and incline increase about every 3 minutes. A fall of more than 10 mm Hg despite a higher workload, with chest pressure or other signs of ischemia, is a standard reason to stop the test, and here the pressure fell 16 mm Hg. The test ends on findings like this whether or not the target heart rate was reached. The technician alerts the provider at once, keeps the ECG and pressure monitoring going and stays with the patient through recovery.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 6

In a hospital stress lab, a 61-year-old man referred for suspected coronary disease is in stage 3 of a Bruce protocol. He has no symptoms, and his systolic BP has risen with each stage. Leads V5 and V6 show the J point 2.5 mm below the PR baseline, and the segment stays flat at that level to 80 ms past the J point, in more than three consecutive beats. Which classification is MOST accurate?

  1. Downsloping depression: an absolute reason to stop
  2. Horizontal 2.5 mm depression: significant if angina develops
  3. Horizontal 2.5 mm depression: a relative reason to stop
  4. Upsloping depression: an expected exercise change

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Downsloping depression is the more worrying pattern, but this segment stays level from the J point to 80 ms, so it is horizontal. Even marked downsloping depression sits on the relative list; ST elevation is the ST change on the absolute list.
  • B. Pain adds weight, but horizontal or downsloping depression of 1 mm or more in three consecutive beats is abnormal by itself, and over 2 mm is a relative indication with or without symptoms.
  • C. Correct. Measured 80 ms after the J point the segment is 2.5 mm down and runs flat, so it is horizontal depression over 2 mm. In a patient with suspected ischemia that is a relative indication to end the test, and the physician needs the finding now.
  • D. J-point depression that slopes quickly back up is common during exercise, which makes this tempting, but an upsloping segment rises toward baseline by 80 ms. This one is still 2.5 mm down at 80 ms.

Exercise ST depression is measured from the PR baseline at a point after the J point, usually 80 ms, and described by depth and slope. Horizontal or downsloping depression of 1 mm or more in at least three consecutive beats is the abnormal, ischemic pattern. Carluccio's 2026 review lists marked horizontal or downsloping depression over 2 mm, in a patient with suspected ischemia, as a relative indication to end the test, while ST elevation over 1 mm in leads without Q waves is absolute. A relative finding is reported to the supervising physician at once, who decides whether exercise continues.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 7

In a stress lab, a technician is preparing a patient for a treadmill test. The patient asks, "The monitor watches my heart the whole time, so do I need to tell you how I feel?" Which instruction should the technician give?

  1. Speak up if the discomfort lasts several minutes, since brief symptoms pass
  2. Signal for leg pain, since the heart is already being watched by the machine
  3. Slow down by yourself and wait for staff to ask how you feel
  4. Report chest discomfort, dizziness, palpitations or breathlessness right away

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because patients try to finish the test, but waiting lets a worsening problem continue on the treadmill. Staff decide whether a symptom is serious, so the patient reports early.
  • B. Leg pain can stop the test, but the tracing does not measure chest tightness, light-headedness or breathing difficulty. Assuming the machine covers the heart leaves the most important symptoms unreported.
  • C. Patients should not manage this alone. The test follows a set protocol, and a patient who stays silent may keep going while symptomatic because staff cannot see what he feels.
  • D. Correct. The monitor shows the electrical tracing but not what the patient feels. Symptoms are a reason to stop the test, so the patient must say them when they begin.

A stress test stops when the target heart rate is reached, when the patient develops chest pain or a worrying blood-pressure change, when the ECG suggests the heart muscle is short of oxygen, or when fatigue or symptoms such as leg pain prevent going on. The technician records rhythm and blood pressure, but only the patient can report feelings like dizziness or pressure. Before the test, also tell the patient to wear comfortable shoes and loose clothing, and to follow the provider's instructions on food, caffeine and medicines.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; NHA CET Test Plan (2017 job analysis)

Question 8

In a stress lab, a technician monitors a treadmill test ordered for risk assessment in a 24-year-old patient with a WPW pattern and no symptoms. At rest the PR is 0.10 s with a delta wave. During stage 2, as the sinus rate climbs past 140/min, the delta wave disappears abruptly from one beat to the next, the PR becomes 0.16 s and the QRS narrows. What does this finding suggest about the accessory pathway?

  1. The pathway conducts faster with exertion, a higher-risk sign
  2. Exercise has blocked the AV node, unmasking the pathway
  3. The pathway now conducts backward, as in a concealed pathway
  4. The pathway fails at faster rates, a lower-risk sign

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Sympathetic tone can speed pathway conduction, which makes this tempting, but that would keep or increase the delta wave. Here the delta wave vanished.
  • B. AV nodal slowing makes pre-excitation more obvious, not less, and exercise speeds AV node conduction. The normal PR and narrow QRS show conduction moved back to the AV node.
  • C. A concealed pathway conducts only from ventricle to atrium and shows no delta wave at any time, but this pathway showed one at rest and lost it suddenly at a higher rate, which is a conduction limit.
  • D. Correct. A sudden loss of pre-excitation as the heart rate rises shows the pathway cannot keep conducting at short cycle lengths, which is generally associated with a lower risk of sudden death.

Pre-excitation shows when part of the ventricle is activated early over the accessory pathway, giving a short PR and a slurred QRS upstroke. When the rate rises and the pathway reaches its refractory limit, every impulse travels through the AV node instead, so the PR lengthens to normal and the QRS narrows in a single beat. On exercise testing, this sudden loss of pre-excitation is the expected response and suggests a lower-risk pathway, while pre-excitation that persists or grows at high rates suggests a more dangerous one. The technician marks the stage and heart rate at which the delta wave disappeared.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 9

During a cardiac rehabilitation intake exercise test with a metabolic cart, a 52-year-old man reaches a peak oxygen uptake of 31.5 mL/kg/min before he stops for fatigue. Which peak workload should the technician record on the report?

  1. 9 METs
  2. 31.5 METs
  3. 10.5 METs
  4. 3.5 METs

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. One MET is the resting oxygen uptake of 3.5 mL/kg/min, so 31.5 divided by 3.5 gives 9 METs.
  • B. This copies the oxygen uptake without converting it. METs are multiples of resting uptake, so the number has to be divided by 3.5.
  • C. This comes from dividing by 3 instead of 3.5, a rounding slip that overstates his capacity by more than one MET.
  • D. 3.5 mL/kg/min is the uptake that defines one MET, not this patient's workload. Recording it would describe a resting level of effort.

A metabolic equivalent, or MET, is the oxygen a person uses at rest: 3.5 mL of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute. Functional capacity is reported in METs, the number of times resting uptake the patient reached, so 31.5 ÷ 3.5 = 9 METs. Stepped treadmill protocols such as the Bruce raise the work rate by about 1 to 2.5 METs per stage, and the workload can also be estimated from speed and grade. Exercise capacity in METs is a strong predictor of cardiovascular events and death, which is why it appears on the stress report.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 10

At 07:30 in a hospital outpatient stress lab, a technician sets up for the first treadmill test of the day. The crash cart with the defibrillator is parked just outside the door, and its daily check log has no entry for today. The first patient is changed and waiting. Which action is BEST?

  1. Move the cart inside so it is closer to the treadmill
  2. Start, since the cart passed yesterday's check
  3. Start, and check the cart during the first recovery
  4. Have the daily cart check done before the first test

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Having the cart close is good, but a cart directly outside the room meets the standard, and moving it does not show that the defibrillator works or that the cart is stocked.
  • B. Yesterday's entry does not show that the defibrillator works today or that supplies used since then were replaced, and the risk of collapse is present from the first test.
  • C. This gets the check done early, but an arrest can happen during exercise, before recovery begins, when an unchecked cart might fail.
  • D. Correct. A full resuscitation cart with a defibrillator must be ready in or just outside the room, and the daily check is what shows it is ready, so the first test waits until the check is done by whoever is assigned.

Exercise testing carries a small risk of acute myocardial infarction, collapse and death, so a full resuscitation cart with a defibrillator must be in the testing room or directly outside it, with at least two trained staff present. Departments assign people to check the cart daily and weekly, and the log is the evidence that the defibrillator works and the supplies are complete. A missing entry means the check has not been done, so the technician has it done, or reports the gap to the assigned person, before the first patient exercises.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version
