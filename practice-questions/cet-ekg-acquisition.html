Question 1

On a medical unit, a technician records a 12-lead ECG on a 66-year-old patient. Leads I, II, III and aVF all show inverted P waves and QRS complexes, aVR is upright, and aVL looks like an ordinary lead. Which pair of electrodes was MOST likely reversed?

  1. Right arm and left leg
  2. Right arm and right leg
  3. Left arm and left leg
  4. Right arm and left arm

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Swapping the right arm and left leg electrodes inverts leads I, II, III and aVF, turns aVR upright and leaves aVL unchanged.
  • B. Moving the right arm electrode onto the neutral right leg site flattens lead II into a near-straight line rather than inverting it.
  • C. This swap inverts lead III and makes the P wave larger in lead I than in lead II, but leads I and II stay upright and aVR is unchanged.
  • D. Arm reversal also makes aVR upright, which makes it tempting, but it inverts lead I and leaves aVF unchanged, so it does not explain inverted leads II, III and aVF.

Each limb reversal leaves a fingerprint. Right arm and left leg reversal inverts I, II, III and aVF, makes aVR upright, and swaps leads I with III and aVR with aVF, while aVL is unaffected. Arm reversal inverts lead I alone, left arm and left leg reversal inverts lead III, and any swap with the right leg neutral electrode flattens one lead. Knowing which lead is unaffected points to which electrode pair to check, so the technician corrects those wires and repeats the tracing.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 2

In a hospital stress lab, a 58-year-old man is in stage 2 of a Bruce protocol treadmill test. His resting 12-lead showed sinus rhythm with no Q waves. Which finding on the monitor should the technician report to the supervising provider at once?

  1. Sinus tachycardia that has risen to 138/min
  2. Systolic BP up from 134 to 172 mm Hg
  3. Isolated PVCs that fade as the workload rises
  4. ST elevation of 2 mm in V2 through V4

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The heart rate is supposed to climb toward the target during a stress test, so a sinus rate of 138/min in stage 2 is an expected finding, not an endpoint.
  • B. A rise in systolic pressure with workload is the expected response to exercise. A fall in systolic pressure as the workload increases, or a rise past about 230 to 250 mm Hg, would be the concern.
  • C. PVCs can look worrying, but isolated, uniform PVCs that are suppressed as the rate climbs are the benign pattern. PVCs that increase in number or complexity with workload are the red flag.
  • D. Correct. New ST elevation of 1 mm or more in leads without Q waves (other than aVR, aVL or V1) is an endpoint that ends the test, so the provider needs to know immediately.

Exercise normally raises the heart rate and systolic pressure, so a climbing sinus rate and a systolic rise of about 40 mm Hg are expected responses that the technician records and keeps watching. New ST elevation of at least 1 mm in leads without Q waves from an old infarct is a test endpoint, because it suggests acute injury, so the technician tells the supervising provider immediately and stays with the patient. Isolated PVCs that fall away as the rate rises are reassuring; PVCs that grow more frequent or complex with workload are reported.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3

In a vascular surgery clinic, a patient with a right below-elbow amputation needs a follow-up resting 12-lead. His tracing from last month is annotated "arm electrodes on both upper arms, mid-biceps level." Where should the technician place the arm electrodes today?

  1. On the residual right forearm and the left forearm near the wrist
  2. On the right shoulder and the left wrist, as near standard as each arm allows
  3. Repeat last month's sites and annotate this tracing
  4. Repeat last month's sites without a new note

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. This looks closer to standard, but the two arm electrodes sit at different levels and differ from last month's setup, so a change could come from placement.
  • B. Each site may seem as close to standard as possible, but the pair is badly asymmetric and the shoulder site approaches the torso, which alters wave amplitudes.
  • C. Correct. Repeating last month's symmetric sites keeps the limb leads comparable, and annotating this tracing tells the reader the limb electrodes were moved.
  • D. The sites are right, but each tracing has to carry its own note; a reader of this tracing alone would not know the limb electrodes were moved.

Limb electrodes normally go just above the wrists and ankles. Recording guidance allows moving them up the limbs when a limb has been amputated, and reviews of alternative placements add that proximal sites should be placed symmetrically. Because moving electrodes up the limbs can change the ECG, the same configuration is used for every serial recording and each tracing documents the sites. Matching last month's mid-biceps sites means a difference between the two tracings is more likely to be real. For a resting ECG, limb electrodes are not placed on the torso.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 4

In a long-term care facility, a resident reaching for her call light knocks a cup of water across the ECG cart's keyboard and top panel just before her tracing. The cart, plugged into the wall, still shows a normal screen. A second cart is on the next unit. Which action is BEST?

  1. Dry it with towels, then record if it still works
  2. Leave it off for an hour to dry, then go on
  3. Unplug it and record this one tracing on battery
  4. Turn it off, unplug it and send it for inspection

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Drying the outside is a sensible first thought, but water may have reached the circuits under the keys, and a normal screen does not show that the cart is safe.
  • B. Waiting lets the surface dry, but drying time is not an inspection, and moisture inside the cart can still cause a fault.
  • C. Running on battery removes the wall current, which makes this the tempting near-miss, but the wet electronics are still powered, and the resident would be connected to them.
  • D. Correct. Electrical equipment that has gotten wet is inspected by a qualified person before it is powered again, so the cart comes out of use and the second cart is used for her tracing.

Water inside electrical equipment can create a shock hazard or a fault that cannot be seen from outside, and a working screen does not rule it out. Equipment that has gotten wet is inspected by a qualified person, such as biomedical engineering, before it is energized again, and only qualified and authorized people repair electrical equipment. The technician switches the cart off, unplugs it by the plug with dry hands, tags it out of use, and records the resident's tracing on the second cart, which keeps the delay short.

Sources: OSHA Quick Card: Electrical Safety

Question 5

At a neurology clinic, a technician records a 12-lead for a 68-year-old patient with Parkinson disease whose hands shake at rest. The preview shows a fast, irregular baseline in the limb leads. She is reclined with her arms hanging at her sides. Which adjustment should the technician try FIRST?

  1. Move the limb electrodes onto her shoulders and lower abdomen
  2. Rest her arms on the table and tuck her hands under her thighs
  3. Turn on the machine's muscle filter and record the tracing
  4. Ask her to grip the side rails firmly to hold her hands still

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Torso sites alter wave amplitudes and limit the tracing's diagnostic use. With a significant tremor, electrodes may move up the limbs, but only after positioning fails, and the tracing is labeled.
  • B. Correct. Supporting the limbs and tucking the hands under the legs relaxes the muscles and steadies the tremor, which is the first step before changing settings or sites.
  • C. The filter reduces interference but distorts the waveform, so it is used only after positioning and relaxation have failed, and its use is marked on the tracing.
  • D. A firm grip tenses the arm muscles, which adds the same somatic artifact the technician is trying to remove.

Tremor and tense muscles add somatic potentials that make a fast, irregular baseline. The first fix is positioning: limbs supported on the bed or table, hands gently tucked under the legs, and the patient comfortable and warm. If artifact persists, the next options are moving the limb electrodes up the limbs (still on the limbs, labeled) and then switching on the filter, which is marked on the tracing because it distorts the waveforms. A suboptimal tracing is annotated with the reason.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 6

A technician in a cardiology office phones a 54-year-old man the day before his treadmill stress test to review the preparation. He says, 'I take metoprolol every morning for my blood pressure. Should I skip it tomorrow?' Which reply is BEST?

  1. "I'll ask your ordering provider and call you back."
  2. "Yes, skip it for 24 hours so your heart rate can rise."
  3. "Take it tonight instead of tomorrow morning."
  4. "Take it as usual, because it won't affect the test."

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Whether to hold a heart medicine before a stress test is the ordering provider's decision, so the technician gets the answer from the provider rather than guessing; the patient keeps his usual routine until told otherwise.
  • B. Beta-blockers do blunt the heart rate response, and some providers hold them for about 24 hours, which makes this tempting, but telling a patient to stop a medicine is outside the technician's role.
  • C. Moving the dose sounds like a compromise, but it is still a change to his medicine that only the provider can make.
  • D. Metoprolol slows the heart rate response to exercise and can keep him from reaching his target heart rate, so the claim is wrong, and the decision belongs to the provider.

Many medicines change how the heart responds to exercise. Beta-blockers such as metoprolol slow the heart rate and blunt its rise, which can keep a patient from reaching the target heart rate and make the test less able to show ischemia. When the goal is to test without medication, the ordering provider may ask for them to be held, often for about 24 hours. At other times the provider wants the test done on the usual medicines. Because the choice depends on why the test was ordered, the technician does not advise the patient. The technician asks the provider and passes on the provider's instructions.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 7

In an outpatient internal medicine clinic, a patient needs a routine 12-lead. A red, scaly rash covers the inner right wrist where the technician usually places the right arm electrode, and the forearm skin above it is intact. Where is the BEST site for the right arm electrode?

  1. A few centimeters higher on the same forearm
  2. Below the right clavicle, on the upper chest
  3. The right upper arm, with the left arm electrode kept at the wrist
  4. On the rash, after cleaning it well with an alcohol wipe

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Electrodes are kept off inflamed skin, and a site a little higher on the same forearm is still on the distal limb, so the limb leads stay standard.
  • B. The chest is clear of the rash, but torso placement changes the limb-lead pattern and is kept for exercise testing, emergencies or limbs that cannot be used.
  • C. Moving up the arm avoids the rash, but the move is larger than needed and leaves the arm electrodes at uneven levels, which shifts limb-lead voltages.
  • D. Cleaning seems to meet the skin-prep need, but alcohol and adhesive irritate inflamed skin further, and a scaly surface gives poor, unstable contact.

Electrodes should not go on skin with redness, lesions or wounds: gel and adhesive can irritate it further, and inflamed or scaly skin gives poor, unstable contact. Limb electrodes do not have to sit exactly at the wrist. The long-standing standard allows any site on the arms and legs away from the shoulders and hips, and signals change little along the distal limb, but sites near the trunk, such as the upper arm or the torso, alter limb-lead voltages. Moving the electrode a short distance up the same forearm onto intact skin avoids the rash and keeps the tracing equivalent to a standard one.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Green, 12-Lead EKG Confidence, 3rd ed.

Question 8

A technician scanning a 48-hour Holter recording in a hospital cardiology department finds a 3.8-second stretch with no complexes on channel 1 at 02:14. Channel 2 shows QRS complexes continuing at a steady rate of 64 through the same seconds, and the diary says the patient was asleep. How should the technician mark the event for the reading provider?

  1. Sinus pause longer than 3 seconds during sleep
  2. Intermittent AV block with dropped ventricular beats
  3. Artifact from signal loss on channel 1
  4. Recorder failure that makes the night's data unreadable

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A pause of this length would be flagged, but a real pause stops the complexes in every channel at once, and channel 2 shows beats continuing.
  • B. A blocked beat drops the QRS in every recorded lead. Complexes marching on in channel 2 show the ventricles kept beating.
  • C. Correct. The heart cannot pause in one channel while beating in another, so the gap comes from lost electrode contact or a lead problem on that channel.
  • D. Channel 2 recorded well through the same seconds, so the night is still readable; one channel lost signal briefly.

Loose electrodes, broken lead wires and older recorder faults can create pseudo-pauses that mimic sinus arrest or AV block, while motion and muscle noise can mimic flutter, fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia. The check that settles most of these is the simultaneous channel: a true event appears in every lead at the same moment. Calling artifact a pause risks an unneeded pacemaker, while calling a real pause artifact misses it.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 9

In a hospital echocardiography lab, a 66-year-old patient has just finished a transthoracic echocardiogram, and the cardiologist adds a resting 12-lead ECG. Smears of ultrasound gel remain across the left chest, and the V3 to V5 electrodes slide and lift as soon as they are applied. What should the technician do NEXT?

  1. Move V3 to V5 onto clean skin just below the gel
  2. Leave the gel on, since it helps carry the signal to the skin
  3. Press the same electrodes back on and tape their edges
  4. Wipe off the gel, clean and dry the skin, then use new electrodes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Clean skin is the goal, but the standard chest positions are used whenever they can be reached; wiping the gel away makes them usable, while lower sites can distort the anterior and lateral leads.
  • B. Ultrasound gel looks and feels like electrode gel, which makes this tempting, but each electrode already carries its own conductive gel, and the slippery film keeps the adhesive from sealing, so the electrodes keep lifting.
  • C. Tape seems quick, but the electrodes and the skin under them are still coated with gel, so contact stays poor and the baseline drifts.
  • D. Correct. The film of ultrasound gel sits under the adhesive ring as well as the gel center, so the skin is cleaned and dried and fresh electrodes are applied at the standard sites.

Electrodes need clean, dry skin under both the gel center and the adhesive ring. A European consensus on ECG signal quality says the electrode's ability to stick must be checked first and that moist skin is washed and dried before electrodes go on, and the UK recording guideline lists mild soap and water or an alcohol wipe for cleansing. Leftover ultrasound gel is a wet, slippery film, so electrodes placed over it lift and give a drifting baseline. Because chest electrodes belong at their standard landmarks whenever those can be reached, the gel is removed rather than worked around.

Sources: Dee et al., Alarm Management Program (2022); LITFL ECG Library

Question 10

A 63-year-old patient arrives in the emergency department by ambulance with chest pain. The paramedics transmitted a 12-lead ECG and left their chest electrodes in place, still dry and well stuck. The provider wants a hospital 12-lead to compare with the paramedic tracing. What should the technician do FIRST?

  1. Record at once with the electrodes in place
  2. Ask the paramedics which landmarks they used to place them
  3. Remove the electrodes and apply a fresh set at standard sites
  4. Check each chest electrode against the landmarks

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Speed matters with chest pain, but recording through unchecked sites can copy a placement error into the hospital tracing, and the check takes moments.
  • B. Their account helps, but recollection is less reliable than looking, and checking at the bedside is quicker than finding the crew.
  • C. A fresh set gives standard sites, but removing the electrodes first loses the chance to see where the paramedic tracing was recorded from, which matters for the comparison.
  • D. Correct. Checking the electrodes left in place shows whether the paramedic tracing came from standard sites; correct ones can be reused for a closely matched comparison and wrong ones moved, which tells the provider whether differences come from placement.

Studies of chest electrode placement by paramedics and other clinicians show wide variation, most often V1 and V2 too high and V4 to V6 too low, and such errors can create patterns such as anterior T-wave inversion, septal Q waves or ST elevation. Researchers suggested leaving prehospital electrodes in place where the manufacturer permits, so hospital staff can judge their accuracy and either use the same positions for comparison or disregard the earlier findings. The technician then records promptly and tells the provider about any electrode that had to be moved.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library
