Question 1

On a medical telemetry unit, the nurse asks a technician to repeat a 12-lead ECG on a 66-year-old patient whose potassium has just been reported high. The first tracing, 40 minutes ago, showed sinus rhythm at 84/min with tall, tented T waves, a PR of 0.18 s and a QRS of 0.09 s. Which change on the repeat tracing marks the MOST advanced stage and must be reported at once?

  1. Sinus rate slowed from 84/min to 76/min
  2. P waves flatter and wider, PR now 0.22 s
  3. QRS now 0.16 s with a bizarre shape
  4. T waves taller and narrower in V2 to V4

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Bradyarrhythmias belong to the hyperkalemia sequence, which makes this tempting, but a sinus rate of 76/min is not a bradyarrhythmia, and a slower sinus rate alone is a less advanced change than a widening QRS.
  • B. P-wave flattening and PR prolongation are the second step in the sequence. They are worth reporting, but they come before QRS widening.
  • C. Correct. QRS widening with bizarre morphology comes late in the hyperkalemia sequence, after the T-wave and P-wave changes, and it precedes the sine-wave pattern and arrest rhythms.
  • D. Taller, more tented T waves show the potassium effect is still present, but peaked T waves are the first change to appear, so this is the earliest stage among the options.

LITFL describes ECG changes from about 6.0 mmol/L upward in a rough order: peaked T waves first, then P-wave widening and flattening with PR prolongation, then bradyarrhythmias and conduction blocks, then QRS widening with bizarre shapes, and above about 9.0 mmol/L a sine-wave pattern, ventricular fibrillation, PEA or asystole. It also warns that the level and the tracing correlate poorly and that arrest can occur with a fairly normal ECG. Any new change goes to the nurse, but a widening QRS is reported immediately, in person, while the technician stays with the patient.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 2

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician places electronic calipers on two consecutive R waves during a regular stretch of a 72-year-old patient's recording, displayed at 25 mm/s, and the software reports an R-R interval of 1,250 ms. Which box count and heart rate match this interval?

  1. About 31 small boxes, 48/min
  2. 62.5 small boxes, 24/min
  3. 12.5 small boxes, 120/min
  4. 25 small boxes, 60/min

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. At 25 mm/s a small box is 0.04 s, so 1.25 s ÷ 0.04 ≈ 31 boxes, and 1,500 ÷ 31.25 = 48/min, the same as 60 ÷ 1.25 s.
  • B. This uses 0.02 s per box, the value at 50 mm/s. The recording is displayed at 25 mm/s, so the box size and the rate from it are off by a factor of two.
  • C. This treats each small box as 0.10 s, mixing up small and large boxes. A large box is 0.20 s and a small box 0.04 s, so this count is far too low and the rate far too high.
  • D. This uses 0.05 s per small box. That value is wrong at 25 mm/s, where 1 mm is 0.04 s, so the box count and the rate both come out wrong.

Heart rate from an interval is 60 divided by the R-R in seconds, so 1,250 ms (1.25 s) gives 48/min. On paper at 25 mm/s each small box is 0.04 s and each large box 0.20 s, so the same interval spans about 31 small boxes or 6.25 large boxes, and 1,500 ÷ 31.25 or 300 ÷ 6.25 both give 48/min. Knowing that the box methods and the caliper reading are the same calculation lets a scanner check the software against the strip and catch a speed setting that has been changed.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 3

In the emergency department, a technician records a 12-lead ECG on a 46-year-old with sudden shortness of breath after a long flight. The QRS is 0.09 s, lead I is mainly negative, and leads III and aVF are mainly positive. A tracing from a year ago had an axis of +50°. Which statement about today's axis is BEST?

  1. New right axis deviation, as seen with right heart strain
  2. New left axis deviation from a horizontal heart
  3. Right axis deviation expected from a tall, thin build
  4. Normal axis, since right deviation starts beyond +110°

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Lead I is mainly negative and leads III and aVF are upright, which puts the axis beyond +90°. Against last year's +50°, the shift is new, and acute right heart strain such as a pulmonary embolism is a listed cause.
  • B. A horizontal heart in a short, stocky build does shift the axis left, but left deviation needs an upright lead I and a negative aVF, the reverse of this tracing.
  • C. A vertical heart in a tall, thin person is a cause of right axis deviation, but body build has not changed since last year, so it does not explain a new shift.
  • D. +110° is the axis threshold in the right ventricular hypertrophy criteria, which makes this tempting, but right axis deviation itself begins beyond +90°.

With lead I negative and aVF positive, the axis lies between +90° and +180°, which is right axis deviation. LITFL lists its causes as right ventricular hypertrophy, acute right ventricular strain such as pulmonary embolism, lateral infarction, chronic lung disease, hyperkalemia, sodium-channel blocking drugs, pre-excitation, dextrocardia, ventricular ectopy, left posterior fascicular block and a vertical heart. A change from a prior tracing matters more than the number alone, because a stable cause such as body build would already have shown last year. The technician gives today's tracing and the old one to the physician promptly, since sudden dyspnea after a long flight raises concern for a clot.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 4

In the emergency department, a provider asks the technician to print a lead II strip at 50 mm/s to look closely at the P waves of a 55-year-old patient with palpitations. On that printout the QRS complex spans 4 small boxes, and the R waves are 40 small boxes apart. Which values should the technician write on the strip?

  1. QRS 0.08 s, rate 38/min
  2. QRS 0.08 s, rate 75/min
  3. QRS 0.16 s, rate 75/min
  4. QRS 0.16 s, rate 38/min

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The QRS was converted correctly, but 1,500 ÷ 40 works only at 25 mm/s. At 50 mm/s the rate is 3,000 ÷ 40, or 75/min.
  • B. Correct. At 50 mm/s each small box is 0.02 s: 4 × 0.02 = 0.08 s for the QRS, and 40 × 0.02 = 0.80 s between R waves, which is 60 ÷ 0.80 = 75/min.
  • C. The rate is right, but the QRS was timed at 0.04 s per small box. At 50 mm/s each small box is 0.02 s, so the QRS is 0.08 s.
  • D. This uses the 25 mm/s value of 0.04 s per small box for both measurements, which doubles the QRS and halves the rate.

Doubling the paper speed spreads every waveform over twice as many boxes, so at 50 mm/s a small box is 0.02 s and a large box is 0.1 s instead of 0.04 s and 0.2 s. A QRS of 4 small boxes is therefore 0.08 s, which is a normal narrow complex, and an R-R interval of 40 small boxes is 0.80 s, a rate of 75/min. The faster speed can make waves such as flutter waves easier to see, which is why a provider may ask for it. The technician labels the printout with its speed, because anyone reading it later at the 25 mm/s values would misread both numbers.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 5

At the central telemetry station of a renal unit, a monitor technician watches the lead II rhythm of a 68-year-old with end-stage kidney disease whose dialysis session is a day overdue. An hour ago the strip showed sinus rhythm at 78/min with tall, narrow, peaked T waves. Now it shows a regular rhythm at 42/min with QRS complexes 0.20 s wide, no visible P waves and the same tall T waves. Which interpretation BEST fits the change for the report to the nurse?

  1. Third-degree AV block with ventricular escape
  2. Junctional escape with bundle branch block
  3. Wide slow rhythm, likely hyperkalemia
  4. Accelerated idioventricular rhythm

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. The slow, wide escape-like complexes are tempting, but complete block needs P waves marching on at their own rate; here no P waves are seen at all, so AV dissociation cannot be shown.
  • B. A junctional escape conducted with a bundle branch block is wide with no upright P wave, but it does not explain the stepwise loss of P waves, widening QRS and peaked T waves in a patient with kidney failure.
  • C. Correct. A tracing that has moved from peaked T waves to lost P waves and a very wide, slow QRS follows the hyperkalemia sequence to a late, dangerous stage, and naming the likely cause tells the nurse why it is urgent.
  • D. The complexes are wide with no P waves, which makes this tempting, but 42/min is below the range of about 50 to 110/min, and this rhythm would not explain the tall peaked T waves that came first.

High potassium changes the tracing in a sequence: tall, symmetric, peaked T waves first, then a longer PR, then disappearance of the P waves with a widening QRS and nodal or ventricular escape rhythms, then a sine-wave pattern before ventricular fibrillation or asystole. A slow, very wide rhythm with no P waves in a patient with kidney failure whose earlier strip showed peaked T waves is that late stage, not a simple escape rhythm. The technician reports it to the nurse at once with both strips, and calls for help if the rhythm degenerates; testing and treatment are the team's decision.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Hyperkalemia (2025); LITFL ECG Library

Question 6

A monitor technician at a telemetry central station is asked to watch lead II on a 64-year-old patient admitted with chest pain and to tell the nurse if the ST segment moves up or down from its usual level. Within each beat on the strip, which part of the tracing should the technician use as the baseline when judging whether the ST segment is raised or lowered?

  1. The J point
  2. The point where the T wave begins
  3. The PR or TP segment
  4. The lowest point of the S wave

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. The J point, at the end of the QRS, is where the ST level is read, which makes it tempting, but it is the point being measured, not the reference it is measured against.
  • B. The start of the T wave is the end of the ST segment itself, so it moves up or down with the ST segment and cannot show whether the segment has shifted.
  • C. Correct. The flat stretches between waves, from the end of the P wave to the QRS and from the end of the T wave to the next P wave, mark the isoelectric line the ST segment is compared with.
  • D. The S wave belongs to ventricular depolarization and dips below the baseline by design; using its bottom as zero would make a normal ST segment look elevated.

ST deviation is measured at the J point, where the QRS ends, and compared with the isoelectric baseline of the PR segment or TP segment of the same beat. A normal ST segment lies flat along that baseline or slightly off it. Using a point inside the QRS or the T wave as zero builds the shift into the reference, so a real change can be missed or a normal one exaggerated. Once the baseline is set, the technician compares each strip with the earlier ones and reports a new rise or fall to the nurse.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; LITFL ECG Library

Question 7

In a Holter scanning lab, the software flags a 2.4-second gap between QRS complexes in a 66-year-old patient with dizziness. Before classifying it, the technician marches out the atrial activity through the gap with calipers. Which finding inside the gap shows it comes from AV block rather than from sinus node failure or a premature beat?

  1. No P wave or other atrial activity in the gap
  2. A narrow QRS without a P wave ending the gap
  3. A sinus P wave on time with no QRS following it
  4. An early P wave of a new shape on the T wave

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. An empty gap means the atria were not activated at all, which points to the sinus node (sinus pause, arrest or exit block), not to AV block.
  • B. A junctional escape beat can end a pause from either sinus node failure or AV block, so it tells where the rescue came from, not where the failure was.
  • C. Correct. A P wave that arrives on schedule but is not followed by a QRS means the sinus node fired and the atria depolarized, so the impulse stopped at the AV node or below.
  • D. This is a nonconducted premature atrial complex, the commonest cause of a pause. It is a premature beat meeting a refractory AV node, not a conduction block of on-time sinus beats.

A pause in the ventricular rhythm can come from three levels. If the sinus node fails to fire or its impulse cannot leave the node, no P wave appears. If a premature atrial beat arrives too early, an abnormal P wave sits on the preceding T wave and no QRS follows. If the AV conduction system fails, a normal P wave arrives on time and is not conducted. Marching the P-P interval through the gap therefore places the block. Holter reports name the level because a sinus-level pause and an AV-level block are different findings for the physician.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 8

On a surgical unit, a technician records a follow-up 12-lead EKG for a 70-year-old patient on the second day after hip surgery and compares it with the pre-operative tracing. Which T-wave finding should the technician point out to the nurse?

  1. Inverted T wave in aVR on both tracings
  2. Inverted T wave in V1 on both tracings
  3. New deep, symmetric inversion in V2 to V4
  4. Shallow inversion in lead III on both

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. The T wave is normally inverted in aVR, because that lead looks at the heart from the right shoulder. Seen on both tracings, it is expected.
  • B. An inverted T wave in V1 is a normal finding in adults, and it has not changed since the pre-operative tracing.
  • C. Correct. T waves are normally upright in V2 to V4, and deep, symmetric inversion that was not on the earlier tracing is abnormal and worth reporting promptly.
  • D. Tempting because lead III looks at the inferior wall, but an isolated, shallow inverted T wave in lead III is a normal variant, and it is unchanged.

The T wave shows ventricular repolarization and is normally upright in every lead except aVR and V1; an inverted T wave in lead III alone is a normal variant. A new inversion compared with a prior tracing is abnormal, and pathological inversion is usually symmetric and deeper than 3 mm. Causes include myocardial ischemia and pulmonary embolism, which is a real concern a few days after hip surgery. Comparing with the earlier tracing is what separates expected inversions from a change, so the technician points out the new finding to the nurse promptly and leaves the interpretation to the provider.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 9

In a central telemetry room, a monitor technician reviews a lead II strip for an 80-year-old patient on a medical unit. Data are: regular R-R intervals at 44/min; narrow QRS complexes; an upright P wave before each QRS with a constant PR interval of 0.16 s; a small extra hump on each T wave. Which finding would show that this rhythm is NOT sinus bradycardia?

  1. R-R intervals that stay equal across the strip
  2. T-wave humps that march with the P waves
  3. A QT interval longer than half the R-R interval
  4. A PR interval that stays at 0.16 s

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Equal R-R intervals are expected in both rhythms, because 2:1 conduction drops beats in a fixed ratio. Regular spacing is what makes a hidden P wave easy to miss.
  • B. Correct. If each hump falls exactly where the next P wave is due, it is a hidden P wave. The atria are then firing at about 88/min and only every second P wave reaches the ventricles.
  • C. Tempting because a hump on the T wave changes how the T wave looks, but a long QT is a repolarization finding. It does not show whether extra atrial beats are present.
  • D. A constant, normal PR on the conducted beats fits sinus bradycardia and 2:1 conduction equally well, so it cannot separate them.

In a slow rhythm with P waves present, the key question is whether every P wave is followed by a QRS. A P wave can hide in the T wave of the beat before, so the technician sets calipers on one P-P interval and walks it across the strip. If the T-wave humps land exactly on schedule, the atrial rate is double the ventricular rate, a fixed 2:1 AV block, which is an AV-node conduction problem rather than a slow sinus node. Counting only the visible P waves would label it sinus bradycardia and understate the problem. The technician reports the atrial and ventricular rates separately to the nurse.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 10

At a long-term care facility, a technician records a routine 12-lead EKG on an 83-year-old resident who has no complaints. The lead II rhythm strip is slightly irregular at 78/min with a narrow QRS. A P wave comes before each QRS, but the technician counts three different P-wave shapes, and the PR interval changes from beat to beat. Which interpretation fits this strip?

  1. Atrial fibrillation with a controlled rate
  2. Sinus arrhythmia
  3. Multifocal atrial tachycardia
  4. Wandering atrial pacemaker

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The rhythm is irregular at a controlled rate, but atrial fibrillation has no distinct P waves. A separate P wave before each QRS rules it out.
  • B. Sinus arrhythmia is irregular too, but every P wave comes from the sinus node, so the P shape and the PR interval stay the same from beat to beat.
  • C. The P-wave findings are the same, which makes this the closest miss. But multifocal atrial tachycardia requires a rate over 100/min, and this strip runs at 78/min.
  • D. Correct. Three or more P-wave shapes with a varying PR interval and a rate of 100/min or less define a wandering atrial pacemaker, in which the pacing site shifts between atrial foci.

Wandering atrial pacemaker and multifocal atrial tachycardia share the same picture: at least three distinct P-wave morphologies, a PR interval that varies, and an irregular rhythm, because impulses come from several atrial sites. The rate separates them, so the technician must count it: 100/min or less is a wandering atrial pacemaker, over 100/min is multifocal atrial tachycardia. Visible, discrete P waves separate both from atrial fibrillation, and changing P shapes separate them from sinus arrhythmia.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; LITFL ECG Library
