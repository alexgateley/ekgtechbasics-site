CET Safety and Patient Care practice questions

Question 1

At a cardiology office, a 63-year-old scheduled for a treadmill test next week says, "I haven't jogged in 20 years. Will I have to run the whole time?" Which explanation is MOST accurate?

  1. You keep going for the full 60 minutes, however tired you feel
  2. You run at one steady speed for a set time
  3. The belt keeps one speed while the slope rises
  4. You start at a walk, and about every 3 minutes the pace and slope increase

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. About 60 minutes is the length of the whole visit, including preparation and recovery, not the exercise. Exercise stops at the target heart rate, or earlier for symptoms, exhaustion or worrying changes.
  • B. Many patients picture a jog at one pace. The protocol is staged instead, starting slowly and building, so the heart's response can be seen at rising workloads.
  • C. The slope does rise, but the speed rises too. Telling the patient that just the slope changes would leave him surprised when the belt speeds up.
  • D. Correct. The test begins at a walk, and about every 3 minutes the speed and incline increase. It ends at the target heart rate, or earlier if symptoms or worrying changes appear.

A clear, honest picture of the test lowers anxiety and helps the patient prepare. On a treadmill test the patient starts walking, and about every 3 minutes the pace and incline increase. Exercise ends at the target heart rate, or sooner for chest pain, a worrying blood-pressure or ECG change, leg pain or exhaustion. Monitoring continues for 10 to 15 minutes afterward, or until the heart rate returns to baseline. Advise comfortable walking shoes and loose clothing.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; NHA CET Test Plan (2017 job analysis)

Question 2

A technician finishes a 12-lead EKG on a medical-surgical unit. The cart screen still shows the tracing with the patient's name and birth date, and the technician must step into the next room for a moment to fetch a missing cable. Visitors and staff are passing in the hallway. Which action BEST protects the patient's information?

  1. Leave the screen on, since the hallway is inside a staffed hospital unit
  2. Turn the cart to face the wall so the hallway cannot see the screen
  3. Lock the screen and close the open tracing before stepping into the next room
  4. Ask a visitor who is waiting nearby to keep an eye on the cart until she returns

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Incidental exposure is tolerated only when reasonable safeguards are in place; a patient name on an open screen in a public corridor, with visitors passing, is not a reasonable safeguard.
  • B. Turning the cart may reduce what a passer-by sees, but anyone can step around it or touch the unlocked device; it is a weaker safeguard than securing the display.
  • C. Correct. Locking or closing the display is a technical safeguard that stops a passer-by from reading the name and tracing while the technician is out of sight.
  • D. This hands responsibility for protected information to someone outside the workforce, which creates a new exposure instead of removing the old one.

The privacy rule expects every workforce member to use reasonable administrative, technical and physical safeguards so that incidental exposure stays limited. A screen showing a name, birth date and rhythm is protected information, and walking away from it, even briefly, in a corridor is the kind of lapse that safeguards are meant to prevent. Securing the display before leaving is quick, controlled by the technician, and works whether or not anyone is watching.

Sources: HIPAA Privacy and Breach Rules, 45 CFR 164

Question 3

At an outpatient cardiology office, a patient returning a 14-day patch monitor thanks the technician for her help and asks for her personal cell number, 'so I can text you if the flutters come back.' Which response is BEST?

  1. Offer to connect on social media instead of sharing her phone number
  2. Share her number, since texting could get the patient help sooner
  3. Decline kindly and give the office's number for symptoms
  4. Share her number but ask the patient to text her during office hours

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Social media contact is still a personal relationship with a patient, and it blurs professional lines and risks privacy breaches.
  • B. It sounds caring, but a text to one person's phone can sit unread, bypasses the team who can act, and starts a personal relationship outside the care setting.
  • C. Correct. It keeps the relationship professional and sends symptom reports to the office, where staff who can act on them will see them.
  • D. Limiting the hours does not change that symptoms go to a personal device instead of the care team, and the relationship has stepped outside the clinic.

Professional boundaries separate the caregiver's personal life from the patient's care, and the professional, not the patient, is responsible for keeping them. Red flags include meeting or contacting a patient outside the care setting and becoming the one person who can help. Symptom reports belong with the office, where the provider and nurse can review them, so the technician thanks the patient warmly, declines, and gives the clinic's contact route.

Sources: NCSBN: A Nurse's Guide to Professional Boundaries

Question 4

On an inpatient rehabilitation unit, a technician needs to ask a patient about chest pain, medicines and recent falls before recording an ECG in a two-bed room. The roommate is awake and the curtain is open. Which approach BEST protects the patient's privacy?

  1. Move the patient to the hallway so the room stays quiet
  2. Skip the history questions and record the ECG without asking
  3. Draw the curtain, speak quietly and ask what the ECG requires
  4. Ask the roommate to leave the room until the questions are done

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A hallway is more exposed than a curtained bed, and moving the patient adds a fall risk and a longer wait, so privacy is worse, not better.
  • B. Symptoms like chest pain matter for the test and must be reported to the nurse, so omitting them to avoid being overheard trades safety for privacy.
  • C. Correct. Reasonable safeguards such as a curtain and a lowered voice, with questions kept to what the test needs, limit what a roommate might overhear.
  • D. Sending another patient out seems thorough, but the roommate may be unable to move, and HIPAA expects reasonable safeguards, not emptying a shared room.

The Privacy Rule does not demand soundproof rooms. Incidental disclosures, such as a roommate catching a few words, are tolerated when staff have used reasonable safeguards and limited the information to the minimum needed. Pulling the curtain, lowering the voice and asking only the questions that matter for the ECG meet that standard, and any symptom the patient describes is still reported to the nurse. Asking about symptoms is part of safe care, not a privacy risk.

Sources: HHS: HIPAA Incidental Uses and Disclosures; HIPAA Privacy and Breach Rules, 45 CFR 164

Question 5

In a stress lab, a patient recovering from a stroke, with weakness of his left arm and leg, is ready to move from his wheelchair to a recovery chair after a pharmacologic stress test. How should the technician set up the two chairs?

  1. Recovery chair on his right, wheelchair angled to it, brakes locked
  2. Recovery chair on his left, so his weaker side leads the move
  3. Recovery chair on his right, wheelchair brakes off so it can adjust
  4. Recovery chair directly behind him, so he can back into the seat

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. His strong right side can push off and lead the pivot, and an angled, braked wheelchair gives a short, stable turn. The technician guards the weak side.
  • B. The weak side cannot push or support him, so a move toward it has no support. The new seat goes on the side he can use.
  • C. The side is right, but an unlocked wheelchair rolls when he shifts his weight off it. The brakes stay on during a transfer.
  • D. A seat behind him forces a long backward turn without his eyes or his strong side leading the movement, which raises the risk of a fall.

In a transfer, the new seat goes on the patient's strong side so he can push off with the stronger arm and leg and pivot toward it. For a patient with left-sided weakness, that is the right side. The wheelchair is angled close to the other seat, the brakes are locked, the footrests are moved aside, and the technician guards the weak side with the gait belt while the patient stands and turns.

Sources: Open RN Nursing Fundamentals, 2e (2024); MedlinePlus: Moving a Patient from Bed to a Wheelchair (2025)

Question 6

In a busy emergency department, a technician wearing gloves has just applied five-lead monitor electrodes to a patient on a stretcher when a nurse calls from the next bay for a STAT 12-lead ECG on a patient with chest pain. The gloves are intact and look clean. Which action is BEST?

  1. Remove the gloves, clean her hands, and put on new gloves
  2. Take off the gloves and put on a fresh pair without cleaning her hands first
  3. Rub sanitizer over the gloved hands and keep wearing the same pair
  4. Keep the same gloves and go now, since the 12-lead is STAT and they touched intact skin

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Gloves are changed between patients, and hands are cleaned after removing them and before putting on a new pair.
  • B. New gloves alone skip the step that protects the second patient. Hands are cleaned after glove removal and before the next pair goes on.
  • C. Tempting because it looks like hand hygiene and saves time. Single-use gloves are not washed or decontaminated for reuse, and gloves are not a substitute for hand hygiene.
  • D. Tempting because the request is urgent and intact skin lowers the risk. Moving from one patient to another is a standard reason to change gloves, whatever they look like, and the change takes seconds.

CDC lists moving between patients as a reason to change gloves and says hands are cleaned before putting gloves on and after taking them off, because gloves can be contaminated and do not replace hand hygiene. OSHA's bloodborne pathogens standard adds that single-use gloves are replaced when contaminated or torn and are not washed for reuse. A STAT request does not change the sequence: it takes seconds, and it keeps organisms from the first patient from reaching the patient with chest pain.

Sources: CDC Hand Hygiene Guideline (2002); OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1030: Bloodborne Pathogens Standard

Question 7

In a busy emergency department, a nurse says to a technician who has just recorded a chest-pain ECG, 'You drew blood at your last job. Draw the troponin on this patient so we move faster, and I'll sign for it.' Phlebotomy is not in the technician's job description here. Which response is BEST?

  1. Decline, since it is outside your job description, and offer to help with ECGs
  2. Draw it, because the nurse is licensed and accepts responsibility for the order
  3. Ask the patient for verbal permission and draw it once she agrees
  4. Draw it if the nurse stands by to watch the needle stick

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A task outside the job description and the facility's written policies is not accepted, however busy the department is.
  • B. Tempting because delegation is a nurse's decision. A nurse can delegate only tasks within the delegatee's job description or the facility's written policies, and the nurse's signature does not widen the technician's role.
  • C. A patient's agreement addresses consent, not scope. It does not make a task part of the technician's role.
  • D. Supervision sounds like a safeguard, but it does not give the technician the credentialing or the written authorization the facility requires for the task.

NCSBN and ANA delegation guidance lists the right task as one within the delegatee's job description or the facility's written policies, with competency training provided. A delegatee should accept only responsibilities he or she is trained and educated to perform, and should tell leadership about gaps in training. Past experience at another employer does not authorize a task here, because the job description and written policy set the scope. Declining politely, and offering the work that is within the role, keeps the patient safe and the care team moving.

Sources: NCSBN/ANA Delegation Guidelines (2019)

Question 8

In a cardiology clinic, a technician lowered a 76-year-old man to the floor when his knees gave way during a transfer from the exam table. He sits up on the floor, says he is fine and wants to be helped up so they can finish, and shows no visible injury. What is the BEST next step?

  1. Help him to a chair and skip the fall report because he has no injury
  2. Help him back onto the table and finish the ECG before reporting the slip
  3. Stay with him, ask the nurse to assess him, and complete a fall report
  4. Ask a coworker to lift him with you, then let him rest in the waiting room

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because no one is hurt, but staff often leave out falls without injury, and each fall needs a report to find and prevent the next one.
  • B. Tempting because he asks to continue, but moving him before a clinician checks him risks making an unseen injury worse, and the report would wait.
  • C. Correct. A clinician needs to check him, and a report records the fall whether or not he is hurt.
  • D. Tempting as a quick fix, but a two-person manual lift of a patient who has just fallen is unsafe, and he leaves without a clinician's check.

A patient who falls can have an injury that is not visible, such as a hip, spine or head injury, so the technician does not move him on his own word or his wish to continue. The technician stays with him and asks the nurse to assess him before anyone lifts him. The fall is then reported and written up factually, even when no one is hurt: staff who think a no-injury fall is not worth a report cause under-reporting, and falls that are reviewed in a quick huddle are linked to fewer repeat falls.

Sources: MedlinePlus: Moving a Patient from Bed to a Wheelchair (2025); Haines et al., Documentation of In-Hospital Falls on Incident Reports (2008); Jones et al., Post-Fall Huddles (BMC Health Serv Res 2019)

Question 9

In a long-term care dining room, a technician is giving back blows and abdominal thrusts to a resident who is choking on food. The resident goes limp, and the technician guides her to the floor while a nurse calls the code team and brings the AED. Which action should the technician take NEXT?

  1. Roll her onto her side and continue the back blows
  2. Keep giving abdominal thrusts with her lying on her back
  3. Begin CPR, looking in her mouth before giving breaths
  4. Sweep a finger deep in her throat to find the food

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Back blows are for a responsive patient who can stand or lean forward. Lying unresponsive on the floor, she needs CPR, starting with compressions.
  • B. Carrying on with the same maneuver feels logical, but the sequence for an unresponsive choking patient is CPR. Compressions both push on the obstruction and circulate blood if her heart stops.
  • C. Correct. Once a choking patient is unresponsive, CPR starts with compressions, which can also force the food out. The mouth is checked before breaths, and a loose, visible object is removed.
  • D. A blind sweep can push the food farther down and injure the throat. Food is removed from the mouth when it can be seen and is loose.

When a choking adult loses consciousness, the rescuer guides her to the floor, makes sure help and an AED are on the way, and begins CPR starting with chest compressions. Each time the airway is opened for breaths, the rescuer looks in the mouth and removes an object if it is loose and visible. Compressions raise the pressure inside the chest, which may expel the food. When the code team arrives, the technician follows the leader's direction and reports what happened and when.

Sources: Korean CPR Guidelines 2025 Part 3: Adult BLS; Lim et al.: Singapore BCLS and AED Guidelines (2026)

Question 10

In a long-term care facility, a technician arrives to record an ECG for a resident named on the order. The resident has dementia and cannot state a name, and there is no wristband on the resident's arm. A passing aide says, "That's the resident from the room on your order." What should the technician do FIRST?

  1. Record the tracing now and label it once the nurse confirms identity later
  2. Ask the charge nurse to verify the resident and replace the band
  3. Read the name from the order aloud and proceed if the resident responds
  4. Accept the aide's word and the room on the order as the two identifiers

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Confirming later seems to save time, but labeling after the fact, away from the patient, risks the tracing going to the wrong record.
  • B. Correct. A nurse can verify identity against the record and restore the band before anything is recorded.
  • C. A response seems like confirmation, but a resident with dementia may answer to any name, and the technician supplied the answer.
  • D. This feels efficient, but a room and a passing remark do not verify who the resident is, and residents move or swap rooms.

Patients without wristbands are the most frequent identification problem, and in the College of American Pathologists monitoring program cited by AHRQ PSNet, refusing to proceed until the band was replaced cut errors the most. A resident who cannot state a name cannot confirm identity, and a passing remark about a room does not stand in for two verified identifiers. The charge nurse can verify against the record and replace the band, and the tracing is labeled with the resident present, not afterward.

Sources: AHRQ PSNet WebM&M: Right Patient, Wrong Sample (2006); Cornes et al., Blood Sampling Guidelines: Patient Safety and Identification (2019)
