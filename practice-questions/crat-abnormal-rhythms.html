Question 1

At a pre-operative testing clinic, a technician measures a 12-lead on a 67-year-old patient before cataract surgery. Data are: sinus rhythm 64/min; PR 0.16 s; QRS 0.10 s; qR complexes in I and aVL; rS complexes in II, III and aVF, with lead II mainly negative. Which conduction finding should the technician flag for the reading physician?

  1. Left bundle branch block
  2. Left anterior fascicular block
  3. Left posterior fascicular block
  4. Old inferior myocardial infarction

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. LBBB often brings left axis deviation, but it needs a QRS of 120 ms or more with broad R waves and no Q waves laterally. A QRS of 0.09 s rules it out.
  • B. Correct. Lead I upright with lead II mainly negative places the axis beyond −30°, and qR in I and aVL with rS in II, III and aVF on a narrow QRS is the pattern of LAFB.
  • C. LPFB gives the opposite picture: right axis deviation with rS in I and aVL and qR in the inferior leads. This tracing has lead I upright and the inferior leads negative.
  • D. An old inferior infarct leaves Q or QS waves in II, III and aVF. Here each inferior complex begins with a small r wave before the deep S, which is the fascicular pattern.

LITFL's criteria for left anterior fascicular block are left axis deviation (usually −45° to −90°), qR complexes in I and aVL, rS complexes in II, III and aVF, and a prolonged R-wave peak time in aVL, with a QRS that stays normal or only slightly wide (80 to 110 ms). The left ventricle is activated through the posterior fascicle, so the first forces point down toward the inferior leads (small r waves) and the main forces swing up and left. Because LAFB is a fascicular block rather than a full bundle branch block, the QRS does not reach 120 ms.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 2

In a cardiology clinic, a technician reviews a 12-lead EKG from a 57-year-old patient. The rhythm is regular at 70/min with a narrow QRS, and a small P wave sits just before each QRS with a PR interval of 0.08 sec. The provider asks the technician to check whether these P waves come from the AV junction. In which leads should junctional P waves appear inverted?

  1. Leads I and aVL
  2. Leads II, III and aVF
  3. Leads V5 and V6
  4. Leads V1 and V2

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Leads I and aVL view the heart from the left side, so they are a natural place to check P waves. But they look across the bottom-to-top path of retrograde atrial activation rather than along it, and the junctional criterion is a negative P wave in the inferior leads.
  • B. Correct. A junctional impulse spreads upward through the atria, away from the inferior leads, so II, III and aVF record the P wave as negative.
  • C. V5 and V6 face the lateral left ventricle and are used for lateral ST changes and left bundle branch patterns. They do not reliably show the bottom-to-top atrial activation of a junctional beat.
  • D. V1 is a good lead for spotting flutter waves and bundle branch patterns, which makes it tempting. The junctional P-wave criterion, however, is defined by polarity in the inferior leads.

A sinus P wave travels from the top of the right atrium downward and to the left, so it is upright in lead II. A junctional impulse starts low, near the AV node, and activates the atria from the bottom up, the opposite direction, so the P wave is negative in the inferior leads II, III and aVF. Combined with a PR interval under 0.12 sec, an inverted inferior P wave supports a junctional origin. A rate of 70/min places this rhythm in the accelerated junctional range.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a 48-hour recording from a 63-year-old patient. At 06:40 both channels go perfectly flat for 41 seconds, starting and ending with a large, sharp baseline jump. No P waves, QRS complexes or escape beats appear, and sinus rhythm resumes afterward at the same rate as before. The diary reads '06:38 getting dressed, felt fine.' Which classification BEST fits the 41-second segment?

  1. Paroxysmal AV block with ventricular standstill
  2. Sinus arrest with no escape rhythm
  3. A vasovagal pause on standing up to dress
  4. Signal loss from disconnected electrodes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. In AV block the sinus node keeps firing, so P waves would continue across the pause; their absence here points away from a conduction block.
  • B. Sinus arrest removes P waves too, but a 41-second stop with no escape beat would be expected to cause fainting, and it would not begin and end with sharp baseline jumps.
  • C. A vasovagal pause slows the sinus rate first and usually ends with escape beats or symptoms; it would not produce a silent, perfectly flat 41-second segment in a patient who felt fine.
  • D. Correct. A perfectly flat line in every channel, framed by sharp jumps, with no symptoms during a shower, fits electrodes or the cable pulled loose while dressing and then reseated, rather than a pause in the heart.

Loose or disconnected electrodes are a classic cause of false pauses and false asystole. The pattern here points to signal loss: every channel is perfectly flat at the same time, sharp jumps mark the moments the electrodes came off and went back on, no P waves appear even though the sinus node would keep firing in AV block, and a 41-second cardiac standstill would be expected to cause collapse in a patient who instead wrote 'felt fine' in the diary. The technician reports it as artifact with the diary entry, so the physician is not handed a false asystole that could lead to an unneeded pacemaker.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 4

In a device clinic, a technician records a 12-lead ECG on a 74-year-old the day after a dual-chamber pacemaker was placed for high-grade AV block. The ventricular pacing spikes are tall and alike, and each one captures. Once, a spike appears with no QRS after it, and the next P wave and paced beat arrive on time. Which additional finding would MOST support that this spike is artifact rather than a pacing output that failed to capture?

  1. It recurs at the same point in several cycles
  2. It is a fraction of the other spikes' height
  3. It falls 0.16 s after a P wave, at the AV delay
  4. It appears the first day after the implant

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A failure that repeats at the same point in the timing cycle behaves like an intermittent device or lead problem, not random outside noise, so it argues for real loss of capture.
  • B. Correct. The same lead at the same settings draws output spikes of about the same size, so one spike far smaller than the device's usual spikes points to an outside signal rather than a missed output.
  • C. A spike at the programmed AV delay is exactly when the device would fire, so it fits a real output that failed to capture; in the published case the artifact also fell there, so timing alone cannot clear it.
  • D. Lead dislodgement is most likely soon after implant, so the timing raises concern for a real capture problem instead of ruling it out.

Loss of capture is a pacing spike with no paced complex after it, and early after implant it can mean a dislodged lead, so it is reported to the device team. Outside electrical sources can draw small spikes that look like a missed output. Clues to artifact are a spike far smaller than the device's usual spikes and no pacing annotation from the recorder for that spike, while the next beats stay on schedule; a spike that lands at the AV delay does not rule artifact out. The technician still flags the event and attaches the strip, because confirming device function is the provider's call.

Sources: CanadiEM: Interpreting a Pacemaker ECG (2016); LITFL ECG Library

Question 5

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a 24-hour recording from a 27-year-old patient referred for near-constant palpitations. The referral notes normal thyroid tests and hemoglobin, no fever and no stimulant use. The rate averages 108/min while seated, climbs from 105 to 148/min over about a minute when the diary reads 'walked to the mailbox', and stays above 90/min during sleep. Every P wave is upright in lead II with one shape throughout, and no rate change occurs within a single beat. Which rhythm pattern do these findings BEST fit?

  1. Focal atrial tachycardia
  2. Atypical AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia
  3. Inappropriate sinus tachycardia
  4. Sinus node re-entrant tachycardia

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. An ectopic atrial focus gives a P wave whose shape or axis differs from sinus and usually switches on suddenly. Here one upright P shape persists through sleep, rest and effort.
  • B. LITFL notes the slow-slow form can be mistaken for sinus tachycardia, but it starts and stops abruptly and its P waves are retrograde, inverted in the inferior leads rather than upright in lead II.
  • C. Correct. A sinus rate persistently above 100/min with no physiological stressor, an exaggerated rise on minimal effort and P waves identical to sinus fits this pattern.
  • D. Its P waves look sinus too, which makes it the closest miss, but it comes in runs that start and stop within one beat at 100 to 150/min, not as a raised rate that drifts all day.

LITFL describes inappropriate sinus tachycardia as a sinus rate persistently above 100/min in the absence of a physiological stressor, with an exaggerated rate response to minimal exercise, typically in young healthy adults; its ECG cannot be told apart from sinus tachycardia. A Holter separates it from re-entry: the rate drifts up and down over many beats, never switches within one beat, and the P waves keep the sinus shape day and night. The technician reports the rate trend and the diary matches, and the physician makes the diagnosis after other causes are excluded.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 6

In an outpatient cardiology clinic, a technician records a 12-lead for a 79-year-old with Parkinson disease and a visible resting hand tremor. The limb leads show a fine, rapid, irregular wobble of the baseline between QRS complexes, and the computer reading suggests atrial fibrillation. The ventricular rate is 74/min and the QRS is narrow. Which finding MOST strongly suggests that the wobble is tremor artifact instead?

  1. The ventricular rate is under 100/min
  2. The QRS complexes are under 0.12 sec
  3. The R-R intervals stay regular across the strip
  4. The wobble is less than 0.5 mm tall

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Atrial fibrillation can run under 100/min when AV conduction is slow or rate-slowing medication is taken, so a normal rate does not exclude it.
  • B. Atrial fibrillation usually has a narrow QRS too, since conduction below the AV node is normal; QRS width does not separate the two.
  • C. Correct. Atrial fibrillation conducts irregularly to the ventricles; tremor adds noise to the baseline but leaves the underlying R-R rhythm regular.
  • D. Fine fibrillatory waves are defined as under 0.5 mm, so a low-amplitude wobble fits atrial fibrillation as well as artifact.

Muscle tremor and electrical interference can imitate fibrillatory waves, and computer interpretations are easily fooled by them. The deciding check is the ventricular rhythm: marching out the R waves with calipers or a paper edge shows that true atrial fibrillation is irregularly irregular, while a tremor-contaminated sinus rhythm stays regular, and P waves can often be found in the leads the tremor disturbs least. The technician then works to reduce the artifact, repeats the tracing and notes the tremor for the reader, rather than accepting the computer statement.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Atrial Fibrillation (2024); LITFL ECG Library

Question 7

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a 24-hour recording from a 27-year-old competitive cyclist. At 03:10 the sinus rate is 46/min, an upright P wave comes before each narrow QRS, and the PR interval measures 6 small boxes on each beat at 25 mm/s. No P waves are dropped. In the afternoon the PR was 0.18 s. Which interpretation fits the 03:10 segment?

  1. Sinus bradycardia with a PR within normal limits
  2. Junctional escape rhythm at 46/min
  3. Sinus bradycardia with Mobitz type I AV block
  4. Sinus bradycardia with first-degree AV block

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Six boxes can look short at a glance, but at 25 mm/s each small box is 0.04 s, so 6 boxes is 0.24 s, above the 0.20 s upper limit.
  • B. The rate fits a junctional escape, but a junctional rhythm has an inverted or absent P wave close to the QRS, not an upright P with a long PR.
  • C. Mobitz I needs a PR that lengthens beat by beat until a P wave fails to conduct; here the PR is steady and no P wave is dropped.
  • D. Correct. Six small boxes is 0.24 s, longer than 0.20 s, and every P wave still conducts at a constant PR, which is first-degree AV block on a slow sinus rhythm.

Each small box at 25 mm/s is 0.04 s, so 6 × 0.04 = 0.24 s. First-degree AV block is a PR over 0.20 s with every P wave conducted. In young people with high vagal tone and in trained athletes it can be physiologic, and vagal tone peaks in sleep, which is why the PR here was normal in the afternoon. The technician reports the PR, the time and the rate in the Holter summary; a PR beyond 0.30 s, dropped beats or symptoms in the diary would raise the concern.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; LITFL ECG Library

Question 8

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a daytime segment from a 74-year-old patient who takes a beta blocker. The P-QRS complexes come in repeating groups of four. Within each group the P-P interval shortens from 0.96 s to 0.88 s to 0.82 s. Each group ends with a 1.40 s gap that has no P wave, and the PR stays 0.18 s throughout. Which rhythm BEST fits?

  1. Second-degree SA exit block, type I
  2. Second-degree SA exit block, type II
  3. Sinus arrhythmia
  4. Second-degree AV block, type I

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Progressive shortening of the P-P interval, grouped beating and a dropped P wave, with a pause shorter than twice the shortest P-P, is the Wenckebach pattern at the sinus node.
  • B. Type II exit block also drops P waves, but the P-P stays constant and the pause is an exact multiple of it. A shortening P-P with a 1.40 s gap does not fit.
  • C. LITFL notes this pattern is easily mistaken for sinus arrhythmia, but sinus arrhythmia varies smoothly with breathing and does not end each cycle with an abrupt dropped P wave.
  • D. AV Wenckebach also groups beats, but the PR lengthens until a P wave fails to conduct, and that blocked P wave is visible in the pause. Here the PR is fixed and the gap holds no P wave.

In SA exit block the sinus node fires normally but some impulses fail to leave it. In type I, the delay between impulse and atrial depolarization grows beat by beat. That growth pushes successive P waves closer together until one impulse is blocked, so the P-P shortens before each dropped P wave. The surface ECG shows only P waves, so the diagnosis rests on P-P timing with a constant PR. Beta blockers, digoxin, vagal tone and inferior MI are listed causes, so the finding goes in the report with the patient's medicines.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 9

At a telemetry central station, a monitor technician reviews the strip of a 72-year-old patient with second-degree AV block. Sinus P waves march regularly at 80/min, and the conducted beats have a PR of 0.24 s and a narrow QRS. After one P wave fails to conduct, a QRS identical to the conducted beats appears 1.34 s after the previous QRS, 0.08 s after the next P wave. Which label BEST fits this beat?

  1. Junctional escape beat
  2. Premature junctional complex
  3. Conducted sinus beat
  4. Ventricular escape beat

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. It ends a pause, comes about 1.34 s after the last beat (an escape rate near 45/min), and matches the narrow conducted QRS. A P wave only 0.08 s before it is too close to have conducted in a patient whose conducted beats need 0.24 s.
  • B. The narrow QRS without a conducted P wave fits the junction, but a premature beat comes before the next beat is due. This one comes late, at the end of a pause.
  • C. The P wave just in front of it makes this tempting, but an AV node that needs 0.24 s on every conducted beat cannot pass an impulse in 0.08 s. The P wave and the QRS only happen to land together.
  • D. A ventricular escape also ends pauses, but it starts below the bundle of His and gives a wide QRS of a new shape. This QRS matches the conducted beats.

Escape beats rescue the heart when conducted beats fail to arrive, and LITFL lists high-grade second-degree and third-degree AV block among the causes of junctional escape. Two checks sort a beat like this one. Timing: a beat that ends a pause is an escape, while one that comes early is premature. PR: a PR far shorter than the patient's own conducted PR means the P wave did not conduct, even though it sits just in front of the QRS. A narrow QRS that matches the conducted beats places the escape in the AV junction, which Merck says fires at about 35 to 50/min. The technician reports the block and the escape beats to the nurse.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 10

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a recording from a 76-year-old patient with a single-chamber VVI pacemaker set at 60/min. At rest every beat is a captured paced beat at 60/min. During two diary entries marked 'vacuuming, lightheaded,' the tracing shows pauses of up to 2.6 s with no pacing spikes and no QRS complexes, and fine, fast, irregular noise rides on the baseline during each pause. Which explanation BEST fits the pauses?

  1. Failure to capture from a dislodged lead
  2. Oversensing of muscle signals
  3. Battery depletion lowering the paced rate
  4. Rate hysteresis waiting for a native beat

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Loss of capture shows pacing spikes with no QRS after them; these pauses have no spikes at all, so the device is not firing.
  • B. Correct. The pacemaker senses noise from working chest and arm muscles as heartbeats and withholds its output, so pauses without spikes appear during effort, matching the diary.
  • C. A depleting battery slows the paced rate in a steady way across the whole recording; it does not cause sudden pauses with no spikes that come only with arm effort.
  • D. Hysteresis lets the rate fall to a programmed level a little below the lower rate; a 2.6-s pause (about 23/min) is far below any such setting and comes with baseline noise.

When a pacemaker oversenses, it reads signals that are not heartbeats, such as T waves, outside interference or myopotentials from skeletal muscle, as the patient's own activity and inhibits its output. The clues fit together: the pauses come only during arm work, noise from muscle activity is visible on the baseline, and no spikes appear even though the rate falls far below the lower rate. Failure to capture would show spikes with nothing after them, and failure to pace from a generator or battery problem would not follow the diary activity. Symptomatic pauses go to the physician so the device can be interrogated.

Sources: CanadiEM: Interpreting a Pacemaker ECG (2016)
