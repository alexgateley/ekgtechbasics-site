Question 1 · Pre-Procedural Activities

In a pre-operative testing clinic, an ECG is finished, and a patient who can bear weight with help must move from a power exam table to his wheelchair. He has been sitting on the table edge for several minutes and denies dizziness, but his feet dangle well above the floor. The brakes are locked and a gait belt is on. What should the technician do BEFORE the pivot?

  1. Have him slide forward off the edge until his feet reach the floor
  2. Lower the table until his feet rest flat on the floor
  3. Raise the table a little so he can slide down into the chair
  4. Turn the wheelchair to face the table squarely, head-on

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because it gets his feet down without adjusting anything, but sliding off a high edge drops his weight suddenly onto his legs and can start a fall.
  • B. Correct. With his feet flat on the floor he can bear weight and push off during the stand, and the technician can use the legs rather than catch him.
  • C. Tempting because gravity seems to help, but a sliding drop into the chair is uncontrolled, and a pivot transfer depends on his standing on his feet first.
  • D. Tempting because it seems to shorten the distance, but a chair facing the table head-on forces a wider turn; it goes beside the table, parallel or at a slight angle.

A stand-pivot transfer starts with the patient sitting and his feet flat on the floor, so the surface is lowered until they touch. The wheelchair sits beside the table, parallel or at a slight angle, with the brakes locked and the footrests out of the way. The patient rests a moment sitting in case of dizziness, which has already happened here. Then the technician stands close, grips the gait belt, bends the knees with the back straight, counts to three, and pivots with the feet while the patient pushes off and bears as much weight as he can.

Sources: MedlinePlus: Moving a Patient from Bed to a Wheelchair (2025)

Question 2 · Pre-Procedural Activities

At a pediatric office, a technician is asked to record a pre-sports ECG on a 14-year-old girl. She came with her grandmother, who says the parents are at work and agreed by phone. The chart lists no authorization for the grandmother to consent to care. What should the technician do?

  1. Proceed, since an adult relative is present and the girl agrees to the test
  2. Send the family home to reschedule with a parent present
  3. Have the girl sign the consent form herself, since she is old enough to understand
  4. Hold the ECG and ask the nurse to verify who may consent for her

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A relative's presence is tempting, but a grandparent is not automatically authorized to consent for a minor, and the girl's own agreement does not replace a parent's consent.
  • B. A parent may be reachable by phone, so turning the family away is premature and delays care. The nurse should decide that after checking, not the technician.
  • C. Understanding the test is helpful but does not give a 14-year-old legal authority to consent, except for an emancipated minor, which the chart does not show.
  • D. Correct. Consent for a minor comes from a parent or legally authorized representative, so the nurse checks the chart and, if needed, reaches a parent under office policy.

Minors generally cannot consent for themselves. A parent or legally authorized representative consents, and only an emancipated minor with the capacity to understand may consent alone. Authorization for another adult usually must be on file. The technician cannot judge legal authority, so she pauses before placing electrodes and asks the nurse, who can check the record or phone a parent. Explaining the ECG to the girl and her grandmother in simple terms is still appropriate while waiting.

Sources: AORN: Elements of Informed Consent and Key Surgical Consent Guidelines (2024)

Question 3 · Resting ECG

At a thoracic surgery clinic, a 70-year-old patient who had lung surgery 10 days ago cannot lie flat because of incision pain, so the technician will record her 12-lead while she sits in a chair. Which setup is BEST?

  1. Leaning forward on the over-bed table to keep pressure off her back
  2. Chest leads moved up one interspace to allow for the upright heart
  3. Seated with back supported, feet on floor, chest leads at standard sites
  4. Flat on the exam table for the 10 seconds, since supine is the standard position

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Leaning forward may ease the incision, but holding the trunk up tenses the muscles and adds artifact, and the posture is farther from the standard.
  • B. Some assume the heart shifts enough when upright to need new landmarks, but moving the chest leads changes the views and makes the tracing harder to compare.
  • C. Correct. A seated ECG keeps V1 to V6 at their usual landmarks, and a supported back with feet on the floor keeps muscles relaxed so the tracing stays free of motion artifact.
  • D. Supine is the standard position, which makes this tempting, but making a patient in pain lie flat is unkind and unnecessary when a seated recording is accepted.

A seated 12-lead is an accepted adaptation for patients who cannot tolerate lying flat because of pain, dyspnea or recent surgery. The precordial electrodes stay at their anatomical sites, and limb electrodes go on the wrists and ankles or symmetrically on the upper arms and thighs. Sitting upright can raise the heart rate a little and slightly change intervals and T waves, so the technician writes 'seated' on the tracing header, which lets the provider compare it fairly with earlier supine ECGs.

Question 4 · Resting ECG

At an occupational health clinic, a technician reviews a routine 12-lead ECG on a 45-year-old before sending it. Lead III shows small P waves, a small QRS that is about half up and half down, and small T waves. Leads I and II differ in shape, aVL and aVF differ, and aVR is negative. The technician has already traced each wire to the correct limb. What should the technician do NEXT?

  1. Swap the left arm and right leg wires and repeat
  2. Replace the left leg electrode and repeat it
  3. Raise the gain to 20 mm/mV and repeat the tracing
  4. Send the tracing as recorded

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. That reversal makes lead III an almost perfectly flat line with lead I matching lead II and aVL matching aVF. None of that is present, and swapping correct wires would create the error.
  • B. Lead III does use the left leg electrode, but a poor electrode causes noise or wander. Clean, small complexes are not a contact problem.
  • C. Tempting because the complexes are small, but gain enlarges every lead and can push the tall leads off the grid. A small lead III does not need a bigger setting.
  • D. Correct. Lead III has all its waves, the wires are verified and no leads match in pairs, so this is a valid tracing with a small lead III, not a wiring error.

A limb lead whose QRS is equally up and down sits at right angles to the heart's electrical axis. Lead III looks from +120 degrees, so a normal axis near +30 degrees makes it small and biphasic, with P waves and T waves still visible. A neutral-electrode reversal looks different: the affected lead is a nearly flat line with no waves, and two pairs of leads match. Knowing the normal look of a small lead avoids an unneeded repeat, while a true flat line with matching leads still calls for a wire check.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 5 · 12-Lead ECG Analysis

In a cardiology clinic, a technician fills in the axis on a 48-year-old's 12-lead ECG before an echocardiogram. Lead III shows an R wave and an S wave of equal size. Leads I and II have tall, nearly equal R waves, and lead aVR is the most negative limb lead. At about what angle does the QRS axis lie?

  1. About +120°
  2. About +60°
  3. About −150°
  4. About +30°

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. +120° is the angle of lead III itself. An axis pointing along a lead makes that lead the tallest, not equiphasic.
  • B. +60° would make lead II clearly the tallest limb lead and lead III upright rather than equiphasic. Leads I and II are about equal here, which puts the axis halfway between them.
  • C. −150° is also at right angles to lead III, but an axis there would make leads I and II negative, and here both are tall and upright.
  • D. Correct. Lead III sits at +120°, so an equiphasic lead III puts the axis at right angles to it, at +30° or −150°; upright leads I and II pick +30°.

The isoelectric-lead method finds the limb lead whose QRS nets to zero; the axis runs at right angles to it. Each lead has two perpendicular directions, so the second step is to pick the one that points toward the upright leads. Lead III's perpendiculars are +30° and −150°. Tall, matched R waves in I (0°) and II (+60°) and a deeply negative aVR (−150°, the exact opposite of +30°) all confirm +30°, a normal axis inside the −30° to +90° range.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 6 · 12-Lead ECG Analysis

In an outpatient stress lab, a 49-year-old arrives for a treadmill test ordered after an episode of chest pressure yesterday that went away on its own. He is pain-free now. On the resting 12-lead EKG, leads V2 and V3 show deep, symmetrical T-wave inversion with no ST elevation, a change not present on his tracing from last year. What should the technician do BEFORE starting the test?

  1. Start the protocol, since he is pain-free and the ST segments are flat
  2. Replace the V2 and V3 electrodes and repeat the tracing to clear the inversion
  3. Hold the test and show the tracing to the supervising provider
  4. Note a nonspecific T-wave change and send the tracing for routine reading

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because there is no ST elevation and no pain, but this pattern is typically recorded while pain-free, and stressing the heart can trigger a large infarction.
  • B. Poor contact causes noise and baseline wander, which makes rechecking tempting, but a clean tracing with deep, symmetrical inversion in two neighboring leads reflects the heart, and repeating it only delays the report.
  • C. Correct. New deep, symmetrical T-wave inversion in V2 and V3 after recent chest pain is a high-risk pattern, and exercise could provoke infarction, so the provider must see it first.
  • D. T-wave changes are sometimes nonspecific, but a new, deep, symmetrical pattern confined to V2 and V3 after chest pain is not, and routine reading would come after the treadmill.

Deep, symmetrical T-wave inversion in V2 and V3 that is new and recorded while the patient is pain-free after recent chest pain fits the Wellens pattern. LITFL describes it as highly specific for a critical narrowing of the proximal left anterior descending artery, with a high risk of an extensive anterior infarction within days to weeks, even when cardiac enzymes are normal or barely raised. Because stress testing these patients can lead to infarction or cardiac arrest, the technician does not start the treadmill: the patient stays at rest, and the provider sees the new tracing beside last year's. Being pain-free is the typical setting for this pattern rather than reassurance, since during pain the T waves can look falsely normal.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 7 · 12-Lead ECG Analysis

In a stress lab, a technician measures the resting 12-lead ECG of a 64-year-old referred for exertional fatigue. At 25 mm/s the QRS spans 2.75 small boxes. V1 shows an rS complex with a tiny r wave, leads I, V5 and V6 show broad monophasic R waves with no q waves, and the axis is −10°. Which conduction finding do these measurements fit?

  1. Incomplete right bundle branch block
  2. Incomplete left bundle branch block
  3. Left anterior fascicular block
  4. Complete left bundle branch block

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Incomplete right bundle block also keeps the QRS under 120 ms, but it gives an rSR′ in V1 and slurred lateral S waves, not an rS in V1 with broad monophasic lateral R waves.
  • B. Correct. At 0.04 s per small box the QRS is 0.11 s, and typical left bundle branch block morphology with a QRS under 120 ms is incomplete left bundle branch block.
  • C. This block keeps the QRS near normal, but it needs left axis deviation of about −45° to −90° with qR complexes in I and aVL. An axis of −10° with no lateral q waves does not fit.
  • D. The shape fits, but this misreads the width: 2.75 small boxes is 0.11 s, and a complete block needs a QRS of 120 ms or more.

At the standard paper speed of 25 mm/s each small box is 0.04 s, so a QRS spanning 2.75 boxes lasts 0.11 s. LITFL's criteria for left bundle branch block are a QRS of 120 ms or more with a dominant S wave in V1, broad monophasic lateral R waves, absent lateral q waves and a prolonged R-wave peak time in V5 and V6. When that typical morphology appears with a QRS under 120 ms, LITFL calls it incomplete left bundle branch block. The missing lateral q waves come from reversed septal activation, which runs right to left when the left bundle conducts poorly.

Question 8 · Stress Testing

During a treadmill stress test, the technician watches the 12-lead monitor of a 61-year-old who reports chest tightness with exertion. Which ST-segment change, if it appears, MOST suggests myocardial ischemia?

  1. Horizontal ST depression of 1.5 mm in V5 and V6
  2. Upsloping ST depression of 1 mm at peak heart rate
  3. T-wave inversion in aVR at rest and at peak
  4. Downsloping ST depression of 0.5 mm in lead III alone

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. New horizontal depression of 1 mm or more, measured just after the J point, is fairly specific for ischemia, and here it appears in two neighboring lateral leads.
  • B. Tempting, because it is ST depression during exercise, but upsloping depression counts as ischemic only when it is deeper, about 1.5 mm or more with a slow upslope.
  • C. An inverted T wave in aVR is expected; it is a normal finding there, as it can be in III and V1.
  • D. Downsloping is a worrying shape, but 0.5 mm falls short of the 1 mm exercise threshold, and a change confined to lead III is less reliable than one seen in neighboring leads.

On an exercise ECG, ST depression is measured from the PR (PQ) junction to a point 80 ms after the J point, or 60 ms after it when the heart rate is above 130. New horizontal or downsloping depression of 1 mm or more, seen in three consecutive beats on a stable baseline, is fairly specific for ischemia; upsloping depression counts only when it is deeper, about 1.5 mm or more with a slow upslope. An inverted T wave in aVR is normal. The technician reports new ST depression to the supervising provider at once, notes the time and stage on the record, and keeps watching the patient and the monitor; whether to stop the test is the provider's decision.

Question 9 · Ambulatory Monitoring

A 58-year-old man is on day 9 of a 30-day looping event monitor. At an electrode check in a cardiology office, he says that yesterday his heart fluttered for about 20 seconds, and he pressed the button right after the fluttering stopped rather than during it. He thinks the episode was lost. Which reply is MOST accurate?

  1. Pressing it saves a stretch of rhythm from just before the press
  2. A spell under a minute is too brief for the monitor to keep
  3. It saves the rhythm after a press, so press sooner next time
  4. The episode is saved once he phones it in to the monitoring center

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A looping monitor records all the time and keeps a set window of rhythm from before each press, so a press soon after a short spell can still capture it.
  • B. Loop recorders save strips of seconds to minutes around a press, and brief spells are a common reason they are ordered, so a 20-second episode is not too short.
  • C. That is how a symptom (non-looping) event recorder works: it is applied or held during symptoms and records from that point on. A looping device also keeps the stretch before the press.
  • D. Sending the recording to the center matters, but the strip is saved at the moment he presses the button; phoning in delivers it rather than creating it.

A looping event monitor records continuously and keeps overwriting its memory. When the patient presses the button, it locks in a preset window of rhythm from before the press along with a period after it, which is why pressing just after a short spell still captures it. A symptom (post-event) recorder lacks this memory and records from when it is applied. The technician can reassure him, remind him to write the time and what he was doing in his diary, send the recording as instructed and press as soon as he can next time.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; NHA CET Test Plan (2026 job analysis)

Question 10 · Rhythm Analysis

At a Holter clinic, a technician scans a 24-hour recording from a 46-year-old patient whose diary reads "heart racing while mowing the lawn." At that time the rate climbs over about 3 minutes from 88 to 134/min, then drifts back down over several minutes after a diary entry reading "stopped to rest." The P waves keep the same upright shape as at rest. Which interpretation BEST fits this section?

  1. Atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction
  2. Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia
  3. Sinus tachycardia
  4. Junctional tachycardia

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Flutter at 2:1 runs at a fixed rate near 150/min with sawtooth flutter waves in place of normal P waves. This rate changes gradually and the P waves look like the resting ones.
  • B. Reentrant supraventricular tachycardia starts and stops abruptly, often in a single beat, and the P waves are usually hidden or different. A rate that ramps up over minutes does not fit.
  • C. Correct. A rate above 100/min that rises and falls gradually with exertion and rest, with P waves unchanged from the resting sinus beats, fits sinus tachycardia.
  • D. A junctional rhythm has an inverted P wave just before, within or after the QRS in lead II, or no visible P wave. These P waves are upright and match the resting sinus P waves.

How a fast rhythm starts and stops is a clue to its mechanism: an abrupt onset and end suggests a reentry circuit, while a gradual rise and fall suggests increased automaticity, as in sinus tachycardia. Sinus tachycardia is a sinus rhythm above 100/min and is a normal response to exercise, pain, fever, anxiety or low blood volume. Matching the recording to the diary entry and comparing the P waves with the resting beats is how the technician supports this reading. The scanned event, with its times and diary notes, then goes to the provider, who interprets the whole recording.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 11 · Rhythm Analysis

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a three-channel recording from a 72-year-old patient with palpitations. In the V1-type channel, discrete upright atrial waves appear every 0.20 s with a flat-looking stretch between them, and every third wave is followed by a narrow QRS, R-R 0.60 s. The software labels the segment 'atrial tachycardia with 3:1 block.' Which step BEST tests that label?

  1. Remeasure the V1-type channel at 50 mm/s paper speed
  2. Look for sawtooth waves in an inferior-lead channel
  3. Confirm each R-R equals three atrial cycles
  4. Confirm the PR of each conducted beat is fixed

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A faster speed spreads the waves out, but V1 is the lead where flutter waves can look like discrete P waves, so a closer look at the same lead can still mislead.
  • B. Correct. In typical flutter the inferior leads show inverted sawtooth waves with no flat baseline, while V1 often shows upright waves that resemble P waves. A continuous sawtooth there points to flutter, whereas atrial tachycardia keeps an isoelectric baseline between its P waves.
  • C. The arithmetic confirms a fixed 3:1 ratio, but flutter and atrial tachycardia with block both give R-R intervals that are whole multiples of the atrial cycle, so it cannot separate them.
  • D. A fixed PR on the conducted beats shows a stable conduction ratio, which both rhythms can have. It does not show whether the atrial waves are flutter waves.

LITFL describes typical flutter as a sawtooth of inverted flutter waves in II, III and aVF and notes that upright flutter waves in V1 may resemble P waves, so a single V1-type channel can make flutter look like an atrial tachycardia with block. Atrial tachycardia has discrete P waves separated by a flat isoelectric baseline, while flutter is continuous atrial activity. The measured cycle of 0.20 s, an atrial rate of 300/min, is the classic flutter rate, and LITFL gives 3:1 as a fixed ratio near 100/min. Checking the other channels before accepting a software label is part of reviewing any Holter segment.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 12 · Rhythm Analysis

At a skilled nursing facility, a technician records a 12-lead for an 86-year-old resident with long-standing atrial fibrillation who takes digoxin. Earlier tracings showed an irregular rhythm at 70 to 90/min. Today the baseline still shows fibrillatory waves, but the narrow QRS complexes are evenly spaced at 46/min. The resident is alert and says she feels tired. What should the technician do with this tracing?

  1. Report it to the nurse now as a change
  2. File it as the resident's known atrial fibrillation
  3. Label it atrial flutter with 4:1 block for routine reading
  4. Repeat it after warming the room to settle the baseline

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Atrial fibrillation that turns regular and slow suggests the atrial impulses are no longer conducting and a junctional escape is driving the ventricles, so the nurse needs to know now.
  • B. The fibrillatory baseline is the resident's known rhythm, but the ventricular response has changed from irregular to regular, which is a new finding.
  • C. Flutter with 4:1 block is regular, but it shows uniform sawtooth waves at about 300/min, not a chaotic fibrillatory baseline, and it would not be routine here either.
  • D. Cold and shivering cause artifact, but fibrillatory waves are the resident's own atrial activity; repeating the tracing delays a change that needs reporting.

In atrial fibrillation the AV node passes impulses at random, so the ventricular rhythm is irregularly irregular. When the QRS complexes of a patient in atrial fibrillation become regular and slow, the atrial impulses are probably no longer reaching the ventricles (complete heart block) and a junctional escape rhythm has taken over; this regularized atrial fibrillation is a classic sign of digoxin toxicity. The technician does not diagnose the cause but recognizes the change from prior tracings, reports it to the nurse promptly with the old and new tracings, and stays alert for worsening symptoms.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Merck Manual Professional: Atrial Fibrillation (2024)

Question 13 · Rhythm Analysis

At a Holter clinic, a technician scans a 24-hour recording from a 74-year-old patient. One lead II section is irregular at about 80/min, and the scanning software has labeled it atrial fibrillation. On close review, identical atrial waves appear without a flat baseline between them, one every 0.22 sec, and every R-R interval measures either 0.66 or 0.88 sec. Which interpretation BEST fits this section?

  1. Atrial flutter with variable conduction
  2. Atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction
  3. Atrial fibrillation with a controlled rate
  4. Multifocal atrial tachycardia

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Regular atrial waves at about 270/min with R-R intervals of exactly three or four wave intervals mean the AV node is passing every third or fourth flutter wave, which makes the ventricular rhythm irregular.
  • B. The atrial waves do fit flutter, but 2:1 conduction would give a regular ventricular rate near 136/min with R-R intervals of 0.44 sec. This section is irregular and slower.
  • C. This is the software's label, and the rhythm is irregular at a controlled rate. But atrial fibrillation has chaotic, varying fibrillatory waves and R-R intervals with no fixed pattern, not identical waves and two set intervals.
  • D. Multifocal atrial tachycardia is irregular, which makes it tempting, but it needs a rate over 100/min and at least three different P-wave shapes with flat baseline between them. Identical waves at about 270/min do not fit.

Atrial flutter with variable block is a common reason scanning software calls atrial fibrillation. The check is arithmetic: in flutter the R-R intervals are multiples of the flutter-wave interval, here 3 x 0.22 = 0.66 sec and 4 x 0.22 = 0.88 sec, because the AV node conducts every third or fourth wave. Atrial fibrillation shows no such pattern, because its atrial activity is disorganized. The technician corrects or flags the label so the provider sees the right rhythm, since the interpretation feeds into treatment and follow-up decisions.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 14 · Rhythm Analysis

On a medical unit, the nurse tells a technician that a 70-year-old patient's calcium level came back markedly high and asks for a 12-lead EKG. Which finding should the technician expect on the tracing?

  1. Prominent U waves with flattened T waves
  2. A long QT from a lengthened ST segment
  3. A short QT with little or no ST segment
  4. Tall, peaked T waves with a widening QRS

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. U waves with flat T waves point to low potassium; calcium leaves the T wave largely unchanged.
  • B. Calcium does act on the ST segment, which makes this tempting, but lengthening is the effect of low calcium, the opposite problem.
  • C. Correct. High calcium shortens the ST segment, and when it is marked the T wave starts right at the end of the QRS, so the QT is short.
  • D. These are the changes of high potassium, another electrolyte that alters the tracing, not of high calcium.

Calcium changes the length of the ST segment while leaving the QRS and the T wave largely unchanged, so the QT interval moves with it. A high ionized calcium shortens the ST segment, and when it is marked the ST segment disappears and the T wave begins right at the end of the QRS, giving a short QT. Low calcium does the opposite and lengthens the ST segment and QT, which raises the risk of dangerous arrhythmias once the QTc passes 500 ms. Peaked T waves with QRS widening point to high potassium, and U waves with flat T waves to low potassium, so knowing which part of the waveform each electrolyte moves helps the technician measure carefully and recognize a tracing that matches the nurse's concern.

Sources: Gardner et al., Ionized Calcium and the ECG (2014); LITFL ECG Library

Question 15 · Rhythm Analysis

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a recording from an 80-year-old patient with dizzy spells and a known left bundle branch block. Daytime sinus beats show an upright P wave, a PR of 0.18 s and a QRS of 0.14 s. From 01:50 to 02:15 the P waves disappear, and a regular rhythm at 44/min follows, with QRS complexes 0.14 s wide and identical in shape to the daytime beats. How should the technician label the overnight rhythm?

  1. Junctional escape rhythm
  2. Accelerated idioventricular rhythm
  3. Ventricular escape rhythm
  4. Sinus bradycardia with left bundle branch block

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The rate sits in the junctional escape range of 40 to 60/min, and a QRS identical to the conducted beats shows the impulses travel down the same bundle-branch pathway, so the width comes from the old block.
  • B. AIVR is also wide without P waves, but it runs at about 50 to 110/min and starts in the ventricles, so its QRS would differ from the conducted beats.
  • C. The wide QRS and missing P waves fit a ventricular escape, which makes this tempting, but a ventricular focus would change the QRS shape and usually fires at 20 to 40/min.
  • D. The QRS matches the patient's bundle branch block, but sinus bradycardia needs an upright P wave before each QRS, and none is seen in this stretch.

Width alone does not prove a ventricular origin. An impulse that starts in the AV junction travels down the bundle branches like a sinus beat, so in a patient with a bundle branch block it is drawn with the same wide QRS as the conducted daytime beats. A ventricular focus spreads from a new starting point, which changes the QRS shape, and it usually fires at 20 to 40/min. Comparing escape complexes with the patient's own conducted beats is therefore the key step. The technician labels the rhythm and reports the loss of sinus P waves with its times and the diary entries for the reading physician.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library
