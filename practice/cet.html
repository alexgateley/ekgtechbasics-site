Question 1 · Safety, Compliance and Coordinated Patient Care

In a hospital outpatient ECG lab, a technician finishes a 12-lead on a patient who is still seated in the room. While labeling the printout, she sees she picked the previous patient's name from the cart's worklist, so the tracing is filed in the wrong chart. Which action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Send the tracing to the provider, since the waveform itself is accurate
  2. Delete the file from the cart and record the patient again under the correct name
  3. Write the correct name over the printed name and keep the file as it is
  4. Report the error to her supervisor and follow the correction process

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The waveform is real, which makes this tempting, but a tracing under the wrong name can be read as another patient's result and drive the wrong care.
  • B. Deleting removes the evidence of the mistake and a copy may already be in the other chart, so the wrong-chart entry goes uncorrected and unreported.
  • C. Handwriting over the name looks like a quick fix, but it alters the record without a trace and leaves the wrong chart and the electronic file unchanged.
  • D. Correct. A wrong-patient entry needs an accurate, traceable fix under policy, so the tracing reaches the right chart and the other patient's record is cleaned up.

Wrong-patient errors usually come from a skipped check or a distraction rather than a wrong waveform, and the tracing is useless, or dangerous, once it sits in the wrong chart. The technician should not alter, delete or forward it on her own. Telling her supervisor lets the facility correct both charts, document the event and decide whether the patient must be recorded again, and reporting the slip helps the lab fix the worklist step that allowed it.

Sources: AHRQ PSNet WebM&M: Right Patient, Wrong Sample (2006); AHRQ PSNet Primer: Reporting Patient Safety Events

Question 2 · Safety, Compliance and Coordinated Patient Care

A stress-lab technician wants to plan tomorrow's treadmill tests at home tonight. The printed schedule lists each patient's name, birth date and the reason for the test. Which action BEST protects the patients' information?

  1. Photograph the schedule with her phone instead of carrying the paper
  2. Leave the printed schedule in the lab and do the planning there
  3. Take it home in a closed folder zipped inside her work bag
  4. Copy the names and test times onto a note, leaving off the reasons

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A photo avoids losing paper, but it copies names, birth dates and reasons onto a personal device the facility does not secure.
  • B. Correct. Keeping the printed list in the lab, where it can be secured, avoids carrying identifiable health information into a place with no facility safeguards.
  • C. A folder hides the page on the way, but the list still leaves the facility, where it can be lost, stolen or seen by others at home.
  • D. Dropping the reasons trims the detail, but names tied to a cardiac lab schedule are still protected information carried off site.

Covered entities must keep administrative, technical and physical safeguards that protect health information from deliberate or accidental disclosure. A printed schedule with names, birth dates and reasons for testing is protected information, and taking it home, copying it to a personal phone or rewriting part of it moves it outside those safeguards, because no locked room, secured device or facility policy follows it there. Planning in the lab, or through any secure remote access the facility provides, keeps the information where policy can protect it.

Sources: HIPAA Privacy and Breach Rules, 45 CFR 164

Question 3 · Safety, Compliance and Coordinated Patient Care

A patient wearing a 48-hour Holter monitor phones the cardiology clinic. He says he has had crushing chest pressure for 15 minutes and is sweaty and nauseated. His wife is at home with him. Which instruction should the technician give?

  1. Have your wife drive you here so the recording can be downloaded right away
  2. I will take your number and have the provider call you back after clinic
  3. Hang up and call 911 now; do not drive in
  4. Press the event button, write the time in the diary, and bring the monitor in tomorrow

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Being driven in delays emergency care, and a clinic is not equipped like an ambulance. NHLBI advises not driving or letting someone else drive during a suspected heart attack.
  • B. A callback hours later wastes the time when treatment helps most. Symptoms like these call for emergency services now, not a message.
  • C. Correct. Chest pressure with sweating and nausea can be a heart attack. Calling 911 brings an ambulance crew who can begin care on the way; driving delays that care and risks a collapse on the road.
  • D. Marking the event is useful for milder symptoms, but it does nothing for a possible heart attack happening now. The recording can be retrieved later.

Chest pain or pressure, sweating for no clear reason, nausea, shortness of breath and sudden dizziness are heart attack warning signs. NHLBI advises calling 9-1-1 right away even when unsure, and not driving or being driven, because emergency crews can start treatment on the way. The technician gives that instruction clearly, stays on the line or confirms the call is being made as clinic policy directs, then tells the nurse or provider and documents the call, including the time and the symptoms described.

Sources: AHA: Warning Signs of a Heart Attack; NHA CET Test Plan (2026 job analysis)

Question 4 · Safety, Compliance and Coordinated Patient Care

In a cardiology office, a nurse asks a technician to witness a patient's signature on the consent form for a stress echocardiogram. As the patient signs, he says, 'I'm not sure what happens during the treadmill part.' Which action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Describe the risks of the treadmill test herself, then witness the form
  2. Witness the signature, since a witness confirms who signed the form
  3. Witness the signature and mention his comment to the provider afterward
  4. Hold off on witnessing and tell the nurse he has questions

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Explaining risks and alternatives is the provider's responsibility. A technician giving a risk talk goes beyond her role and may leave out details.
  • B. A witness does confirm the signer, which makes this tempting, but witnessing a signature the patient has just said he does not understand weakens the consent.
  • C. This looks like a fair compromise, but the form is then witnessed and in the chart before his questions are answered, so the test could start without informed consent.
  • D. Correct. The provider must explain the test and confirm understanding, so questions are handed back to the nurse or provider before anyone witnesses the form.

Informed consent means the patient understands the test, its risks and alternatives before agreeing, and the licensed provider is responsible for that discussion, often confirmed by having the patient repeat it back. A witness confirms the patient's name, the signature and the procedure and provider named on the form, so signing as witness vouches for those details. A patient who voices doubt is not ready, and he may also still refuse or withdraw.

Sources: AORN: Elements of Informed Consent and Key Surgical Consent Guidelines (2024)

Question 5 · Safety, Compliance and Coordinated Patient Care

On a cardiac unit, a patient's 12-lead ECG shows ST elevation in leads II, III and aVF. The provider orders an added right-sided lead, V4R, and the patient asks the technician why an extra electrode is needed. Which explanation is correct?

  1. It views the back of the left ventricle, which the standard leads miss
  2. It repeats lead V4 on the right side to confirm the electrode was placed correctly
  3. It views the high side wall of the left ventricle, which the limb leads miss
  4. It views the right ventricle, which standard leads show poorly

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Posterior leads V7 to V9 are the extra leads for the back wall. The posterior wall is a tempting choice after an inferior pattern, but V4R sits on the right side of the chest.
  • B. A technician checks placement by landmarks, not by duplicating a lead. V4R is an ordered lead that records different tissue, so it is not a placement check.
  • C. The high lateral wall is already shown by leads I and aVL, so no extra right-sided electrode is needed for it. V4R looks the other way, toward the right ventricle.
  • D. Correct. Lead V1 is the only standard lead facing the right ventricle, so V4R is added to look at it directly when an inferior pattern raises the question of right ventricular injury.

The right coronary artery supplies the inferior wall, and it also feeds the right ventricle, so right ventricular infarction complicates up to about 40% of inferior ST-elevation infarcts. Standard leads view the right ventricle poorly. Lead V4R, placed in the fifth intercostal space on the right midclavicular line, is the most useful right-sided lead, and its ST elevation can fade within hours, so the technician records it promptly and labels it. Treatment choices belong to the provider.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 6 · EKG Acquisition

During orientation in a hospital ECG department, a technician uses a resting tracing to show a new hire how the limb leads relate. At the same instant in the QRS complex, lead I reads +0.6 mV and lead III reads +0.4 mV. What does lead II read at that instant?

  1. +0.5 mV
  2. +1.0 mV
  3. -0.2 mV
  4. +0.2 mV

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Averaging assumes lead II sits between the other two leads, but lead II is the sum of leads I and III, not their mean.
  • B. Correct. By Einthoven's law, lead II equals lead I plus lead III at every instant, so 0.6 + 0.4 = 1.0 mV.
  • C. This subtracts lead I from lead III, which reverses both the operation and the sign.
  • D. This subtracts lead III from lead I; the rule adds them, because lead II spans the right arm to the left leg, the path the other two leads cover together.

The three standard limb leads form Einthoven's triangle: lead I records between the right and left arms, lead II between the right arm and the left leg, and lead III between the left arm and the left leg. Going from the right arm to the left arm and then on to the left leg covers the same electrical path as going straight from the right arm to the left leg, so the voltages add: lead II equals lead I plus lead III at every instant. Swapping two limb electrodes, other than the neutral, rearranges which lead shows which pattern, which is why each wire must reach its own limb.

Sources: Ramadurai et al., Frequency of ECG Lead Reversal (2025); LITFL ECG Library

Question 7 · EKG Acquisition

In a veterans' outpatient clinic, a 69-year-old man with thick chest hair needs a resting ECG. When the technician explains that small areas may need shaving, he says he does not want any hair removed. What should the technician do?

  1. Shave just the V1 and V2 sites, which are hardest to place through hair
  2. Clip the hair short instead, since clipping differs from shaving
  3. Explain that the ECG cannot be recorded unless the hair is removed
  4. Part the hair, press the electrodes to the skin and check the tracing

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Shaving any site after he has said no overrides his decision, however small the area or important the lead.
  • B. Clipping is still hair removal, which he has declined; changing the method does not change his answer.
  • C. Declining a shave is not declining the test. Hair is removed when it largely interferes with the signal, and many patients with chest hair record well without it.
  • D. Correct. Hair removal needs his agreement. Parting the hair lets the gel reach the skin, and the tracing may be clean without shaving; if artifact remains, the technician notes it and tells the provider.

The UK recording guideline says chest hair may need removing for good skin contact, but verbal consent is obtained first, and a single-use razor is discarded in a sharps container afterward. A European consensus advises shaving only when hair is abundant and largely interferes with signal transmission. When a patient declines, the technician respects the choice, separates the hair so each electrode sits on skin, presses the adhesive edges firmly and checks the tracing. If quality is poor, the tracing is annotated and the provider told, so the reader knows why.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Dee et al., Alarm Management Program (2022)

Question 8 · EKG Acquisition

A technician at a remote cardiac monitoring center reviews a transmitted mobile telemetry event: a 7-second pause in a 74-year-old, which meets the ordering physician's emergency notification criteria. On the follow-up call the patient says she nearly fainted and still feels lightheaded. The physician and the on-call provider cannot be reached within the protocol's time limit. What should the technician do NEXT?

  1. Leave an urgent voicemail and keep calling the office
  2. Activate EMS to her location as the protocol directs
  3. Send the strip in the next morning's summary report
  4. Ask her to press the event button and send again

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Repeated calls are reasonable inside the protocol's time limit, but that limit has passed. Continuing to call leaves a symptomatic patient without help.
  • B. Correct. A symptomatic event that meets emergency criteria, with no physician reachable in time, is the situation in which a monitoring center's protocol directs staff to send emergency medical services.
  • C. A routine report suits non-urgent findings. A long pause with near-fainting that meets emergency criteria cannot wait until morning.
  • D. Another recording adds data but delays help for a patient who is still symptomatic after a long pause.

Mobile cardiac telemetry sends recordings to a monitoring center, where trained technicians review events and pass alarms to the patient's providers under agreed notification criteria. The center calls the patient to ask about symptoms, and if an event meets emergency criteria and the physician cannot be reached, the center activates emergency medical services. The technician does not decide on treatment. The job is to recognize that the event is urgent, follow the escalation steps in order, stay on the line with the patient if the protocol allows, and document every call and time.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 9 · EKG Acquisition

In a pre-operative testing clinic, a technician preparing a 72-year-old patient for a resting 12-lead ECG runs a finger down the sternum from the notch and meets a horizontal bony ridge a few centimeters lower. Which step should come NEXT to find the V1 site?

  1. Move to the lower tip of the sternum and count two spaces up from the xiphoid
  2. Place V1 at the right sternal edge in the space just below the ridge
  3. Slide down and to the right into the second space, then count to the fourth
  4. Place V1 at the right sternal edge level with the nipple

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Counting upward from the xiphoid has no fixed rib reference, because the lower costal cartilages crowd together; the standard method starts from the ridge where the second rib attaches.
  • B. Tempting because it uses the right landmark, but the space directly under the ridge is the second intercostal space, so V1 would sit two spaces too high.
  • C. Correct. The space just below the ridge on the patient's right is the second intercostal space; counting past the third reaches the fourth, where V1 sits at the right sternal edge.
  • D. Nipple height varies with sex, age, body build and breast tissue, so it is not a reliable marker of the fourth intercostal space.

The ridge is the manubriosternal joint, the angle of Louis, where the second rib joins the sternum. Sliding the finger down and to the patient's right lands in the second intercostal space; counting down through the third reaches the fourth, and the center of V1 goes there at the right sternal edge. The same count is repeated on the left for V2 rather than placing V2 straight across from V1, because the rib spaces on each side can be offset. Starting from a fixed bony landmark prevents the most common chest-lead error, V1 and V2 placed too high.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 10 · EKG Acquisition

At the end of a 48-hour Holter hookup, a 55-year-old man says the recorder is bulky and he plans to take it off in bed and put it back on in the morning. Which instruction should the technician give?

  1. Keep it on at night if he has had spells lying down, and remove it if not
  2. Take it off in bed if the electrodes stay on, since heart rhythms are quiet in sleep
  3. Take the whole set off at night and use fresh electrodes in the morning
  4. Keep it on through the night, in its pouch close to his body, and note bedtime and wake-up time

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. His history may not show spells that happen in sleep, which he cannot feel, so this rule would drop the very hours that need recording.
  • B. Rhythms are slower in sleep, but pauses and AV block can occur then, and a gap in the recording would hide them.
  • C. Fresh electrodes would not be applied or checked by the technician, and the recording would have a long gap.
  • D. Correct. The night stays in the recording, the pouch keeps the recorder close to the body as required, and the bedtime and wake-up note makes the overnight hours readable.

A Holter records continuously for its whole wear time, and the overnight hours are part of the record. Slow rates, pauses and paroxysmal AV block can appear during sleep, which the patient cannot feel or predict, so the order of 48 hours means 48 hours. Taking the recorder off leaves a gap that cannot be filled in later. Sleeping with a recorder is uncomfortable, so the technician offers practical help, such as wearing it in its pouch or on a belt close to the body, and asks him to note bedtime and wake-up time.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 11 · EKG Acquisition

At a long-term care facility, an 81-year-old resident is 6 hours into a 48-hour Holter recording. The charge nurse tells the technician who placed it that the resident has an MRI of his knee booked for 09:00 tomorrow, in the middle of the recording. Which action by the technician is BEST?

  1. End the recording early tonight so it finishes before the scan
  2. Report the clash to the provider who ordered the Holter recording
  3. Take the recorder off for the scan and reattach it with new electrodes after
  4. Leave the recorder on for the scan, since its case is plastic

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Ending early clears the way for the MRI, but it shortens a study the provider ordered for 48 hours and may miss the spells it was meant to catch.
  • B. Correct. The recorder cannot go into the scanner, and whether to move the MRI or change the Holter is a decision for the ordering provider, so the technician reports the clash and documents it.
  • C. This seems to keep both tests, but it leaves a gap in a recording ordered to run for 48 hours, and interrupting an ordered study is not the technician's decision to make.
  • D. A plastic case makes this sound safe, but the recorder holds a battery and electronics and the leads end in metal snaps, and Holter patients are told to stay away from magnets.

Patients wearing a Holter monitor are told to keep it close to the body for the whole recording and to avoid magnets, metal detectors, electric blankets and high-voltage areas. An MRI scanner is a very strong magnet, so the recorder and its electrodes cannot go in with him. The ordered 48 hours were chosen to catch his symptoms, and the technician does not decide which test gives way. Reporting the clash to the ordering provider, telling the nurse, and documenting the call lets the provider reschedule one test or change the order.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; NHA CET Test Plan (2026 job analysis)

Question 12 · EKG Acquisition

A Holter order for a 34-year-old runner gives the reason as 'palpitations while running' and adds 'usual activity.' At the hookup, the patient says she will skip her runs while she wears the recorder because the wires bounce and she does not want to ruin the recording. Which instruction should the technician give?

  1. Unplug the recorder during the run and plug it back in afterward
  2. Run as usual with the wires secured, and log the time and how she feels
  3. Run as usual, then write down the run's time and any symptoms the next morning
  4. Skip the run this week and walk briskly instead, so the wires stay put

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Unplugging protects the wires but removes the stretch of recording the order exists to capture.
  • B. Correct. The run is the activity that provokes her symptoms, so wearing the secured recorder through it and logging the time is how the reader will match any rhythm change to a feeling.
  • C. Running as ordered is right, but a diary written the next morning is less reliable; times and feelings are best logged when they occur.
  • D. Walking is gentler on the wires, but it is not the activity that brings on her spells, so the recording could look normal without answering the question.

Consensus guidance supports a 24 to 48 hour Holter for patients who can reliably reproduce their symptoms, such as exertional palpitations, with a diary that records the time. Here the recording is useful only if it includes the activity that provokes them. The technician helps by reducing artifact, through skin preparation, secured leads and a snug pouch or belt for the recorder, instead of changing her routine, because bouncing wires are a signal problem and not a reason to skip the test.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 13 · EKG Analysis and Interpretation

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician measures a gap on a 25 mm/s printout from an 81-year-old patient who logged near-fainting. Before the gap the P-P interval is 4 large boxes. The distance from the last P wave to the next one is 17 large boxes, with no P waves or other atrial activity in between. Which classification BEST fits?

  1. SA exit block of about 3.4 s
  2. Sinus arrest of about 6.8 s
  3. Sinus arrest of about 3.4 s
  4. Sinus pause of about 1.7 s

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. The duration is right, but exit block gives a pause that is an exact multiple of the P-P. 17 large boxes is 4.25 cycles of 4 boxes, not a whole number.
  • B. This counts each large box as 0.40 s. At 25 mm/s one large box is 5 small boxes of 0.04 s, which is 0.20 s.
  • C. Correct. 17 × 0.20 s = 3.4 s, longer than the 3-second threshold LITFL uses for sinus arrest, and 17 ÷ 4 = 4.25 P-P cycles is not a whole multiple.
  • D. This counts each large box as 0.10 s, which is the value at 50 mm/s. At 25 mm/s each large box is 0.20 s, so the gap is twice as long.

At 25 mm/s, one small box is 0.04 s and one large box is 0.20 s, so 17 large boxes equal 3.4 s and the P-P of 4 boxes is 0.80 s. A gap with no atrial activity that is not a whole multiple of the P-P comes from the sinus node failing to fire; LITFL calls a pause over 3 seconds sinus arrest and lists it under sinus node dysfunction. Pauses over 3 seconds appear on many labs' urgent-notification lists, so the technician follows the lab's protocol and sends this symptomatic pause to the physician promptly.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 14 · EKG Analysis and Interpretation

At a telemetry central station, a 70-year-old patient's rhythm changes from sinus rhythm at 72/min to a regular narrow rhythm at 70/min with an inverted P wave just after each QRS. The nurse reports that the patient now feels lightheaded and the blood pressure has fallen from 128/76 to 104/62 mm Hg. She asks why the pressure could fall when the rate barely changed. Which explanation BEST answers her?

  1. The junction starts weaker ventricular contractions
  2. The atria now contract after the ventricles, not before
  3. The ventricles now contract out of step with each other
  4. The rate fell too far to keep the pressure up

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Contraction strength depends on ventricular filling and the heart muscle, not on which pacemaker starts the beat, and the narrow QRS shows the ventricles still activate normally.
  • B. Correct. A retrograde P wave after the QRS means the atria contract while the ventricles are already contracting, so the atrial contribution to ventricular filling is lost and stroke volume falls.
  • C. Out-of-step ventricular contraction comes with a wide QRS, as in bundle branch block or ventricular pacing. This QRS is narrow.
  • D. A slower rate can lower output, but a change from 72 to 70/min is too small to explain this drop in pressure.

In sinus rhythm the atria contract just before the ventricles and top up ventricular filling at the end of diastole. In a junctional rhythm with a retrograde P wave after the QRS, atrial contraction comes too late to help fill the ventricles, so stroke volume and blood pressure can fall even when the rate is unchanged. Merck notes that losing atrial contraction can lower cardiac output by as much as 20% at a normal heart rate, a figure given for atrial fibrillation that reflects the same lost atrial contribution. The technician reports the rhythm change and its timing, and the bedside assessment belongs to the nurse.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Atrial Fibrillation (2024); LITFL ECG Library

Question 15 · EKG Analysis and Interpretation

At an urgent care center, a technician records a 12-lead EKG on a 29-year-old patient whose palpitations stopped before arrival. In lead II the PR interval spans 2½ small boxes at 25 mm/s, and the QRS begins with a slurred upstroke. Which description of these findings should the technician pass on to the nurse?

  1. Prolonged PR, as in first-degree block
  2. Short PR from a junctional rhythm
  3. Normal PR with a delta wave
  4. Short PR with a delta wave

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A prolonged PR is over 0.20 s, or 5 small boxes. This PR is shorter than normal, not longer.
  • B. A junctional rhythm can shorten the PR, which makes this tempting, but its P wave is inverted in lead II and the QRS starts sharply. A slurred upstroke points to early activation through an extra pathway.
  • C. The slurred upstroke is correctly named, but a PR of 0.10 s is below the normal range of 0.12 to 0.20 s.
  • D. Correct. 2½ small boxes × 0.04 s = 0.10 s, below the normal lower limit of 0.12 s, and a slurred upstroke at the start of the QRS is a delta wave.

The PR interval runs from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS, normally 0.12 to 0.20 s (3 to 5 small boxes at 25 mm/s), and it reflects conduction through the AV node. A short PR has two main causes: an accessory pathway that lets the impulse bypass the AV node, and a junctional rhythm. A slurred first part of the QRS, the delta wave, shows part of the ventricle being activated early through such a pathway; with a short PR and a slightly wide QRS this is the Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern. In a patient with recent palpitations, the technician reports it to the nurse for the provider's review.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library
