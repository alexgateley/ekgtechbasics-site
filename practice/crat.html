Question 1 · Starting Cardiac Monitoring

In a cardiology clinic, a technician preparing a 24-hour Holter hookup reads the history form of a 66-year-old man referred after fainting. He wrote that he has blacked out three times in 5 months and feels normal between episodes. The ordering provider is in the clinic. Which action is BEST before the technician applies the recorder?

  1. Have him press the marker button when he feels faint, so the spell is on the recording
  2. Apply it and extend the wear time to a week, since his spells are far apart
  3. Apply it as ordered and mention the spell spacing when the recorder is returned tomorrow
  4. Tell the provider the spells are weeks apart and ask if this recorder still fits

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A marker button helps with felt symptoms, but a faint can arrive without warning and a person who loses consciousness cannot press it, so it cannot be the plan for fainting.
  • B. Longer wear sounds logical for rare spells, but the technician cannot change the ordered test, and a standard wired Holter is not built for a week of recording.
  • C. Reporting afterward feels harmless, but the information reaches the provider after 24 hours of wear that was unlikely to catch a faint, and the patient may need a second visit.
  • D. Correct. The provider can keep, change or extend the recording once the spell frequency is known, and the patient has not yet spent a day wearing a device unlikely to catch an episode.

Symptom frequency decides which recorder can answer the question. A 24 to 48 hour Holter fits symptoms that happen daily, while spells weeks or months apart need a looping, patch or implanted recorder that records far longer; a consensus table estimates that a 24 to 48 hour recording explains syncope in only 1 to 5 percent of patients. The technician does not choose the device, so the useful step is to send the frequency to the person who ordered it before the patient wears a recorder that is unlikely to catch an episode.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 2 · Administering Cardiac Tests

In an occupational health clinic, a technician records a 12-lead ECG for a pre-employment physical using electrodes from a pouch that was opened about two months ago and left unsealed on the counter. The limb leads and V1 to V3, V5 and V6 are clean, but V4 shows a wandering, erratic baseline artifact. Which action BEST fixes the problem?

  1. Re-prep the V4 site and apply an electrode from a new pouch
  2. Replace the four limb electrodes, since they feed the chest leads
  3. Replace the V4 electrode with another from the same pouch
  4. Hold the V4 electrode down with a fingertip during recording

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. One noisy chest lead points to one poor electrode contact, and electrodes left in an opened, unsealed pouch dry out, so a fresh electrode on clean, dry skin restores the contact.
  • B. The limb electrodes do form the reference for the chest leads, which is why a limb problem shows up across V1 to V6; artifact in V4 alone with clean limb leads points to the V4 electrode instead.
  • C. Tempting because it targets the right site, but every electrode in that pouch has been drying for the same two months, well past the usual limit for an opened pouch, so the replacement may fail the same way.
  • D. Pressing on the electrode adds the technician's own movement and muscle signal to the lead, and it leaves the dried gel in place, so the artifact persists or changes form.

Artifact confined to one lead points to the electrode that makes that lead. Each chest lead compares its own electrode with a reference built from the limb electrodes, so a limb problem would disturb many leads at once. Electrode gel dries once a pouch is opened, which raises skin contact resistance and lets the baseline drift, so manufacturers ask that an opened pouch be folded shut and its electrodes used within a set time, commonly 30 days, and that dry electrodes be discarded. Fresh electrodes from a new pouch, on skin that is clean and dry, fix the contact; V4 belongs in the fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line, so the replacement goes back on that landmark.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 3 · Normal Rhythms

In a pediatric office, a technician records a 12-lead EKG on a calm 7-month-old as part of a heart murmur workup. The rhythm is regular at 25 mm/s, and consecutive R waves are 10 small boxes apart. Which rate and interpretation should the technician note?

  1. 30/min, a bradycardia to report to the provider
  2. 150/min, a tachycardia to report to the provider
  3. 150/min, within the normal range for an infant
  4. 75/min, a bradycardia to report to the provider

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. This divides 300 by the number of small boxes; the 300 rule uses large boxes, and 10 small boxes are 2 large boxes.
  • B. The rate is calculated correctly, but calling it tachycardia applies the adult limit of 100; infants normally run much faster.
  • C. Correct. 1,500 ÷ 10 small boxes = 150/min, and a resting rate of 80 to 160/min is normal for an infant 1 to 11 months old.
  • D. This is 1,500 ÷ 20, which measures from one R wave to the second R wave after it; the interval runs between consecutive R waves, so the true rate is twice as fast.

At 25 mm/s each small box is 0.04 seconds, so a minute holds 1,500 small boxes, and a regular rate is 1,500 divided by the small boxes between R waves: 1,500 ÷ 10 = 150/min. The large-box method agrees, because 10 small boxes make 2 large boxes and 300 ÷ 2 = 150. MedlinePlus lists 80 to 160 beats per minute as the normal resting heart rate for infants 1 to 11 months old, so this calm 7-month-old's rate is within range, even though the adult cutoff of 100 would label it fast. Judging the rate against the age-based range keeps the technician from reporting a normal infant as tachycardic, while a rate outside that range is reported.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Wong's Care of Infants & Children, 12th ed.

Question 4 · Normal Rhythms

In a pediatric office, a parent asks a technician where blood goes right after it returns from the lungs. Which answer is correct?

  1. Into the right ventricle, through the tricuspid valve
  2. Into the right atrium, through the venae cavae
  3. Into the left atrium, through the pulmonary veins
  4. Into the left ventricle, through the aortic valve

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. This is the next step for blood from the body after the right atrium, so it is tempting, but it happens before the lungs, not after.
  • B. The venae cavae do feed the right atrium, which makes this tempting, but they return oxygen-poor blood from the body, not blood from the lungs.
  • C. Correct. Oxygen-rich blood comes back from the lungs through the pulmonary veins and fills the left atrium.
  • D. The left ventricle is a later step and the aortic valve is its exit, so this skips the left atrium and the mitral valve.

Oxygen-poor blood enters the right atrium from the venae cavae, crosses the tricuspid valve to the right ventricle, and is pumped through the pulmonary valve to the lungs. After picking up oxygen, it returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, crosses the mitral valve into the left ventricle, and leaves through the aortic valve into the aorta. Each valve opens forward and closes to stop backflow, and the left ventricle makes the high pressure because it must supply the whole body.

Sources: NHLBI: Pacemakers (How They Work; After Getting a Pacemaker) (2022)

Question 5 · Normal Rhythms

In an outpatient surgery center, a technician records a pre-procedure 12-lead for a 24-year-old before a wisdom-tooth extraction. On the lead II strip, every P wave has the same upright shape and the PR stays at 0.14 sec. The P-P interval shortens gradually to 0.64 sec as she breathes in and lengthens to 1.08 sec as she breathes out. Which interpretation should the technician record?

  1. Sinus arrhythmia
  2. Wandering atrial pacemaker
  3. Sinus bradycardia
  4. Sinus rhythm with sinus pauses

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. One P-wave shape, a constant PR and a P-P interval that varies gradually with breathing by more than 0.12 sec (here 0.44 sec) define respiratory sinus arrhythmia, a normal finding in young people.
  • B. A wandering atrial pacemaker is also irregular, but it shows at least three P-wave shapes and a changing PR; here every P wave looks the same and the PR is fixed.
  • C. The slowest cycles (1.08 sec, 27 small boxes) give about 56/min, but the fastest give about 94/min; the rhythm averages near 70/min, and its defining feature is the cyclic change, not the slow phase.
  • D. A pause is a sudden gap in which an expected P wave is missing; here the cycles lengthen and shorten gradually with each breath, and no P wave is missing.

Respiratory sinus arrhythmia is the commonest irregular rhythm in healthy young people. Breathing in lowers vagal tone and speeds the sinus rate; breathing out restores it and slows the rate, so the P-P interval cycles gradually by more than 0.12 sec (3 small boxes). Every beat still starts in the sinus node, so the P waves keep one shape and the PR stays constant. The technician names the whole pattern rather than its slowest phase, and can confirm the link by watching the strip against the patient's breathing. Sinus arrhythmia that does not follow breathing is less common, is seen mostly in older adults and is more often abnormal.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 6 · Abnormal Rhythms

At an occupational health clinic, a technician reviews the lead II rhythm strip from a pre-employment ECG for a 40-year-old. The sinus rate is 75/min. The fourth QRS arrives earlier than expected, is narrow and looks like the others, and has no P wave before it; an inverted P wave appears just after it, and a pause follows. Which interpretation fits this beat?

  1. Premature ventricular complex
  2. Premature atrial complex
  3. Premature junctional complex
  4. Junctional escape beat

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A PVC also lacks a P wave before it and is followed by a pause, but its QRS is wide and bizarre; this early beat is narrow like the sinus beats.
  • B. A PAC is preceded by its own early P wave, shaped differently from the sinus P waves, with a PR of 0.12 sec or more; this beat has no P wave in front of it.
  • C. Correct. An early, narrow beat with no P wave before it and a retrograde (inverted) P wave after it comes from the AV junction.
  • D. An escape beat is junctional too, but it comes late, after a pause, when the sinus node fails; this beat comes early and is followed by a pause.

Ectopic beats are named by where they start and when they come: a premature beat arrives before the next sinus beat is due, and an escape beat arrives after a pause. A junctional focus activates the ventricles through the normal bundle branches, so the QRS looks like the sinus beats, and it activates the atria backward, so any P wave is inverted in lead II and falls just before the QRS (PR under 0.12 sec), inside it or after it. PJCs are much less common than PACs or PVCs, and the technician labels the beat on the strip so the reader can find it.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 7 · Abnormal Rhythms

In a Holter scanning lab, the software flags 40 beats as 'failure to capture' on the recording of an 81-year-old patient with a VVI pacemaker set at 60/min, whose own sinus rate drifts between 58 and 62/min. On each flagged beat, a pacing spike falls on the very start of a narrow QRS that looks identical to the patient's own conducted beats. No pause follows any of them, and spikes that occur away from native beats capture normally. Which classification BEST fits the flagged beats?

  1. Failure to capture needing a device check
  2. Fusion beats
  3. Pseudofusion beats
  4. Undersensing of the native QRS complexes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. True failure to capture shows a spike outside the refractory period with no depolarization after it; here a native beat was already starting, so the ventricle could not respond and nothing failed.
  • B. Fusion produces a hybrid QRS whose shape lies between the paced and the native beat; these complexes are unchanged from the patient's own beats, so the paced impulse added nothing.
  • C. Correct. The spike fires just as the patient's own beat begins, before the signal reaches the lead, so it lands on an unchanged native QRS; this is normal when the native and paced rates are close.
  • D. Undersensing shows spikes well after native beats or marching through them; a spike at the very onset of a QRS fired before the device could sense it, which is expected timing.

When the patient's own rate hovers around the programmed rate, the pacemaker's timer can run out at the same moment a native beat starts. If the native activation already owns the ventricle, the spike sits on top of an unchanged QRS (pseudofusion); if both wavefronts share the ventricle, the QRS becomes a hybrid (fusion). Both are normal behavior, not malfunction. Software often labels them as noncapture, so the technician checks the QRS shape and the timing before reporting a device problem, which avoids an unneeded interrogation or a false alarm for the physician.

Sources: CanadiEM: Interpreting a Pacemaker ECG (2016)

Question 8 · Abnormal Rhythms

At a telemetry central station, the arrhythmia software labels a regular narrow-complex tachycardia 'SVT' on the lead II channel of a 72-year-old patient after hip surgery. At 25 mm/s the R-R interval measures 10 small boxes and stays the same for 20 minutes. Then two R-R intervals measure 20 small boxes each, and inside those gaps the baseline shows identical negative sawtooth waves every 5 small boxes with no flat segment. Which rhythm does this strip show?

  1. Focal atrial tachycardia
  2. Atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction
  3. AV nodal reentrant tachycardia
  4. Sinus tachycardia

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Atrial tachycardia can also conduct 2:1 and then show more P waves in a pause, but its P waves are discrete with an isoelectric baseline. Continuous sawtooth waves with no flat segment are flutter.
  • B. Correct. 1500 ÷ 10 gives 150/min and 1500 ÷ 5 gives atrial waves at 300/min; when two intervals briefly conduct 4:1 (exactly double the R-R), the sawtooth waves hidden at 2:1 come into view.
  • C. A regular narrow rhythm at 150/min fits the AVNRT range, which is why the software calls it SVT. But AVNRT has no continuous atrial waves to expose, and a long pause in its circuit would usually end it rather than show sawtooth waves.
  • D. A longer R-R in sinus tachycardia would show ordinary, separate P waves with flat baseline between them, not continuous negative sawtooth waves at twice the ventricular rate.

LITFL's rule of thumb is to suspect flutter in any narrow-complex tachycardia at about 150/min (range 130 to 170), because flutter waves at about 300/min are often concealed in the QRS and T waves when 2:1 block is present. Typical flutter waves are negative and best seen in II, III and aVF, and the ratios are fixed numbers: 2:1 gives about 150/min, 3:1 about 100/min and 4:1 about 75/min. An R-R that suddenly lengthens to exactly twice the usual interval is the same atrial rhythm conducted at a higher ratio. The technician prints the unmasked section for the nurse and physician and corrects the software label.

Question 9 · Abnormal Rhythms

At a telemetry central station that also watches the ICU, a technician sees regular, sawtooth-like deflections at about 290/min appear in leads II and III of a 61-year-old patient on continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Narrow QRS complexes continue underneath at a regular 72/min, the same rate as before. The nurse reports that the CRRT pump was stopped for 3 minutes for a bag change; the deflections vanished for exactly those 3 minutes, and upright P waves at a constant PR showed then. Which classification BEST fits the deflections?

  1. Shivering artifact from blood cooled in the circuit
  2. Artifact from the CRRT pump
  3. Atrial flutter with 4:1 conduction
  4. Self-ending bursts of atrial flutter

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. CRRT can cool a patient and shivering does cause artifact, but shivering makes coarse, irregular baseline noise that follows muscle activity, not regular sawtooth waves that stop exactly with the pump.
  • B. Correct. A roller pump's rhythmic motion can build static charge in the circuit and add regular deflections to the ECG; waves that come and go with the pump while the QRS timing never changes come from the machine, not the atria.
  • C. Tempting because 290/min is a flutter rate and divides evenly into the QRS rate of 72/min, but flutter does not stop the moment a pump is switched off and return when it restarts.
  • D. Paroxysmal flutter can start and stop on its own, but its timing would not match the pump's stop and restart, and the switch to and from sinus rhythm would usually shift the QRS timing; here the R-R interval never changed.

Equipment with rhythmic moving parts, such as the roller pumps of CRRT and cardiopulmonary bypass circuits, can create static-electricity artifact that the monitor and the eye read as flutter, supraventricular tachycardia or even ventricular tachycardia. The test is timing: the deflections came and went with the pump, the QRS complexes kept the same regular R-R interval throughout, and sinus P waves showed whenever the pump was off. A deflection rate that happens to divide evenly into the QRS rate is the lure. The technician documents the event as equipment artifact, tells the nurse which device is responsible, and keeps watching the QRS rhythm, because artifact can hide a real change.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 10 · Abnormal Rhythms

In a cardiology clinic, a technician reviews a resting 12-lead EKG on a 33-year-old patient referred for brief spells of a racing heart. The computer statement reads 'normal sinus rhythm, normal ECG.' The technician measures a PR of 0.10 s and a QRS of 0.11 s, and in leads I, aVL, V5 and V6 each QRS starts with a short, slurred upstroke. Which interpretation BEST fits the tracing?

  1. Subtle ventricular pre-excitation
  2. Lown-Ganong-Levine pattern
  3. Incomplete left bundle branch block
  4. A normal tracing, as the computer states

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A short PR with a slurred QRS onset is the mark of an accessory pathway even when the QRS has not reached 0.12 s. LITFL notes that pre-excitation can be subtle, so the technician flags the tracing for the reading physician.
  • B. LGL describes a short PR with a normal QRS that starts sharply, with no delta wave. The slurred upstroke here is a delta wave.
  • C. Incomplete LBBB slows left ventricular activation and can slur the lateral QRS upstroke, which makes this tempting, but it does not shorten the PR interval.
  • D. A QRS of 0.11 s is within normal width, which is why the program passed it, but a PR of 0.10 s is below the 0.12 s lower limit, and a slurred QRS onset is not a normal finding.

LITFL lists the sinus-rhythm features of WPW as a PR under 120 ms, a delta wave slurring the start of the QRS and a QRS usually longer than 110 ms, and it warns that the features may be subtle or present only at times. When the AV node conducts quickly, most of the ventricle is still activated normally and the delta wave stays small, so the QRS can sit at the edge of normal width and a computer that relies on width thresholds may call the tracing normal. In a patient with racing spells, the technician measures the PR and looks at the QRS onset, then notes the possible pre-excitation for the reading physician.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 11 · Abnormal Rhythms

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a recording from an 84-year-old patient with dizziness. A 3-hour segment shows sinus P waves at 90/min and a wide escape rhythm at 36/min with PR intervals that vary at random. Twice in the segment a narrow QRS arrives earlier than the next escape beat is due, 0.18 s after a P wave, and the escape rhythm then resumes. How should the technician classify the segment?

  1. Mobitz I second-degree AV block with grouped beats
  2. Accelerated idioventricular rhythm with sinus capture
  3. High-grade AV block with occasional conducted beats
  4. Third-degree AV block with premature junctional beats

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Wenckebach conducts most P waves with a PR that lengthens before each drop; here most P waves are blocked for long stretches under a slow wide escape.
  • B. Capture beats occur in accelerated idioventricular rhythm, but that rhythm runs about 50 to 110/min; a wide rhythm at 36/min under blocked P waves is an escape rhythm.
  • C. Correct. The early narrow beats are capture beats: a sinus impulse reached the ventricles through the normal pathway, so AV conduction is not entirely absent, although most P waves are blocked.
  • D. Early narrow beats can be junctional, which makes this tempting, but both follow a P wave at the same normal PR; complete block by definition conducts no atrial impulse.

Third-degree AV block is the complete absence of AV conduction, with no supraventricular impulse reaching the ventricles. A capture beat is a narrow, normally conducted QRS that appears when a sinus impulse arrives at a moment the conduction system can carry it, in the midst of AV dissociation. Its presence shows that some conduction remains, so the segment is high-grade (advanced) AV block, in which two or more P waves in a row fail to conduct. It is usually infranodal and just as dangerous, so the technician reports it urgently with the longest pause and the slowest rate.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 12 · Abnormal Rhythms

In a Holter scanning lab, the software labels a stretch at 02:40 'sinus rhythm with pauses' on the recording of a 38-year-old patient. Sinus P waves march at 72/min. The PR measures 0.20 s, then 0.28 s, then 0.32 s, and the next P wave has no QRS after it; this group repeats for 10 minutes, and every QRS is narrow. Which ventricular rate and rhythm should the technician report?

  1. 54/min, sinus arrhythmia with pauses
  2. 54/min, Mobitz II with 4:3 conduction
  3. 72/min, Mobitz I with 4:3 conduction
  4. 54/min, Mobitz I with 4:3 conduction

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The software label makes this tempting, but sinus arrhythmia keeps a QRS after every P wave; an on-time P wave with no QRS after it is AV block.
  • B. The rate is right, but Mobitz II keeps the PR constant before the dropped beat; here the PR lengthens from 0.20 to 0.32 s.
  • C. The label is right, but 72/min is the atrial rate; with one P wave in every four blocked, the ventricular rate is lower.
  • D. Correct. Each group holds 4 P waves and 3 QRS complexes, so the ventricles beat 3/4 as often as the atria: 72 × 3 ÷ 4 = 54/min, and the PR lengthens before the drop, which is Mobitz I.

Wenckebach groups are described by counting P waves and QRS complexes: 4 P waves with 3 conducted beats is 4:3 conduction. The ventricular rate is the atrial rate times the fraction conducted, 72 × 3 ÷ 4 = 54/min, and counting QRS complexes over a full minute gives the same answer. Progressive PR lengthening before the dropped P wave, with a narrow QRS and a nighttime onset, fits block in the AV node, which high vagal tone during sleep favors. The technician corrects the software label and reports the type, ratio, rates and times for the reading physician.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 13 · Abnormal Rhythms

In a cardiology clinic, a technician measures a resting 12-lead on a 71-year-old patient with long-standing hypertension. Data are: sinus rhythm 68/min; PR 0.18 s; QRS 0.09 s; frontal axis +105°; rS complexes in I and aVL; qR complexes in II, III and aVF; P wave upright in lead I; no dominant R wave in V1 and no tall P waves. Which interpretation BEST fits these findings?

  1. Left posterior fascicular block
  2. Left arm and right arm electrode reversal
  3. Right ventricular hypertrophy with strain
  4. Left anterior fascicular block

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Right axis deviation with rS in I and aVL and qR in II, III and aVF, a normal QRS width, and no sign of RVH or electrode reversal fit LPFB.
  • B. Swapped arm electrodes also make lead I negative and shift the axis right, but they invert the P wave and T wave in lead I as well. Here the P wave in lead I is upright.
  • C. RVH is a leading cause of right axis deviation, which is why LPFB is called only after excluding it, but RVH would show a dominant R wave in V1, often tall right-atrial P waves and, with strain, right precordial ST-T changes. None of these is present.
  • D. LAFB has the mirror-image pattern: left axis deviation with qR in I and aVL and rS in the inferior leads.

LITFL defines left posterior fascicular block by right axis deviation over +90°, rS complexes in I and aVL, qR complexes in II, III and aVF, and a prolonged R-wave peak time in aVF, with a QRS of about 80 to 110 ms. It may be diagnosed only after other causes of a right axis are excluded, such as right ventricular hypertrophy, pulmonary embolism, lateral infarction and limb lead reversal. LPFB is far less common than LAFB and is rare in isolation; it usually appears with right bundle branch block as a bifascicular block, which makes a new finding worth flagging.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Ramadurai et al., Frequency of ECG Lead Reversal (2025)

Question 14 · Abnormal Rhythms

On a telemetry unit, a technician measures the admission 12-lead of an 80-year-old patient admitted after a fall with a brief loss of consciousness. At 25 mm/s the PR spans 7 small boxes, and each P wave is followed by a QRS. The QRS is 0.14 s, with an rSR′ in V1 and V2 and a broad, slurred S wave in V5 and V6. Lead I is upright, and the inferior leads are mainly negative, giving a frontal axis of −70°. Which interpretation BEST fits this tracing?

  1. True trifascicular block
  2. RBBB and LAFB with normal AV conduction
  3. RBBB with LPFB and first-degree AV block
  4. Bifascicular block with first-degree AV block

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. True trifascicular block means bifascicular block with third-degree AV block. Here each P wave conducts to a QRS, so the AV block is first-degree.
  • B. The two blocks are read correctly, but 7 small boxes is a PR of 0.28 s, well past the 0.20 s upper limit, so AV conduction is not normal.
  • C. The RBBB and the PR are read correctly, but posterior fascicular block gives a right axis with rS in I. An upright lead I with an axis of −70° is the anterior fascicle.
  • D. Correct. RBBB plus left anterior fascicular block (axis −70°, inferior leads negative) is bifascicular block, and a PR of 7 × 0.04 = 0.28 s adds first-degree AV block.

LITFL describes bifascicular block as RBBB with either left anterior or left posterior fascicular block, and calls bifascicular block with first-degree AV block the pattern long known as 'incomplete trifascicular block'; the AHA/ACCF/HRS advise against that term because the long PR usually comes from the AV node. True trifascicular block is bifascicular block with third-degree AV block and needs a pacemaker. Progression is slow, about 1% a year, but bifascicular block with syncope or near-syncope warrants monitoring and likely pacing, so the technician reports this tracing promptly and watches for higher-degree block.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library

Question 15 · Processing Test Findings

On an orthopedic unit at 3 AM, a technician records an ordered ECG on a 74-year-old woman two days after hip surgery. She says her heart has been racing for an hour and she feels lightheaded, and the machine reads a heart rate of 148/min. The technician finds her nurse in the hallway. Which report is BEST?

  1. Room 12's ECG is done and has been placed in the chart for the morning read
  2. Room 12 seems a little off tonight, so look in on her when you have a minute
  3. Room 12 is in atrial fibrillation and will need a rate-control drug started soon
  4. Room 12: new rate of 148, racing and lightheaded for an hour; please see her now

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because the ordered task is finished, but it hides a new fast rate with symptoms in a chart that may not be read for hours.
  • B. Tempting because it is polite, but it gives no data and no urgency, so the nurse cannot judge how fast to come.
  • C. Tempting because it sounds expert, but naming a diagnosis and suggesting a drug are outside the technician's role, and the report leaves out her symptoms.
  • D. Correct. It identifies the patient, gives the measured rate and her symptoms with their timing, and makes a clear request, without the technician interpreting the rhythm.

Structured hand-off tools such as SBAR or ISBAR (identify, situation, background, assessment, request) help staff pass on critical information that needs action. For a technician, the assessment part is what was measured and observed, not a diagnosis: a new heart rate of 148/min, a racing heart for an hour and lightheadedness two days after surgery. The report ends with a clear request, here to see the patient now. Having the nurse repeat the key points back closes the loop, and the technician hands over the tracing and documents to whom and when the report was made.

Sources: Aaberg et al., TeamSTEPPS in a Surgical Ward (2021); AHRQ TeamSTEPPS Pocket Guide
