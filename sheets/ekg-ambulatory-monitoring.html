Hookup

  1. Check the order (device, wear time, reason) and identify the patient as policy requires. Ask about tape or adhesive allergy.
  2. Clear the last patient from the recorder (only after that download is confirmed), enter this patient's ID, set the clock to the correct time and check the battery and memory.
  3. Prep each site: clip or shave hair if needed, clean with alcohol and let it dry, then rub off dead skin lightly.
  4. Use fresh electrodes from a sealed, in-date pack. Place them over bone (sternum, ribs), not muscle, following the recorder's diagram.
  5. Snap the wires, tape the wires down so they cannot tug the electrodes, and secure the recorder in its pouch or belt.
  6. Watch every channel on the screen before the patient leaves; fix a flat or noisy channel now.
Safety A monitor is not for an emergency. If the patient has chest pain, nearly faints or looks unwell at the hookup, stay with the patient and get the nurse or provider at once.

Try one

Holter, Event and Patch Monitors practice question

At a Holter hookup, a technician gives a 58-year-old woman a diary card for her 24-hour recording. She says she will fill it in tonight from memory and list any times her heart acted up. Which instruction BEST makes the diary useful to the reader?

  1. Write the time, her activity and how she feels as each symptom happens
  2. Write down serious symptoms and leave out the minor ones to keep the card clear
  3. Fill it in tonight as she planned, adding the time of each meal and walk
  4. Press the recorder's marker button at each symptom rather than writing it down

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Entries made at the moment give the reader a clock time and a context to match against the rhythm.
  • B. A clear card sounds helpful, but the patient is a poor judge of which symptoms matter, and a minor feeling can line up with a significant rhythm.
  • C. Extra context sounds useful, but times recalled in the evening are often off by many minutes, which weakens the match.
  • D. The button stamps a time, which helps, but it cannot say what she was doing or feeling, so it supports the diary instead of replacing it.

The reader of a Holter report matches symptoms to the rhythm at the same minute, so the diary needs the clock time, what the patient was doing and how she felt. Entries written later from memory are less accurate, and poor compliance with symptom logs is a recognized limitation of Holter monitoring. The marker button stamps a time but cannot record the activity or the feeling, so it supports the diary and does not replace it.

Scanning and reporting

  • Confirm every software label against the strip. A true event shows in all channels at once; noise is usually worse in one (but a disconnection can flatten all of them).
  • PVC burden = PVCs ÷ total beats × 100. AF burden = time in AF ÷ monitored time × 100, reported with the monitored time.
  • Pauses: give the longest R-R in seconds, the clock time, sleep or awake, and whether P waves appear in the gap (see the Rhythm Gallery).
  • List each diary entry with the rhythm at that minute, even sinus rhythm; list each arrhythmia with its time and any symptom.

In depth

How each monitor records

A Holter recorder is a small battery-powered box (about 200–300 g) worn in a pocket or pouch and wired to standard gel electrodes. It records every beat, usually in 2 or 3 channels and sometimes in 12, for 24–48 hours; some newer recorders run for up to 30 days. The patient takes part by keeping a diary and pressing the marker button, and the recording is analyzed afterward on a scanning workstation. Its strengths are multichannel signal quality and wide familiarity; its weak points are diaries and markers that patients do not keep, electrodes that come off, wire and skin artifact, and no real-time analysis.

A patch monitor has its electrodes built into one adhesive patch, usually placed over the left pectoral area. It records 1 or 2 leads continuously for up to 14 days with no wires, and the patient presses the patch to mark a symptom. Patients accept it well and it is water resistant. The cost is the signal: closely spaced electrodes give a limited, low-voltage tracing with no information on direction, so it cannot localize where an arrhythmia starts, and signal quality varies with body type.

An external loop recorder is worn continuously, attached by wired electrodes, and records into a rolling memory that is overwritten. A button press, or an auto-trigger when the device detects a pause, a slow rate or a tachycardia, saves the strip from a set time before the event to a set time after it. That is why a press just after a short spell still captures it. It saves only those strips, so it cannot document the rhythm the rest of the time.

A post-event (symptom) recorder is not worn. The patient carries it and, when symptoms start, holds it to the chest (or between the hands) and starts a short single-lead recording. It records only from that moment, so it suits spells long enough to apply it in a patient who can recognize them and stays alert. Smartphone ECG cases work the same way (patient-activated, no loop memory).

Mobile cardiac telemetry (MCT) is worn continuously in a patch, pendant, belt or wired electrodes. It analyzes the rhythm as it records and sends events (or a continuous stream) over a cellular or Wi-Fi link to a reading center, where trained technicians review them and pass alarms to the patient's physicians. It needs no action from the patient to send data. Wired MCT systems need the electrodes changed daily, which lowers patient acceptance over long periods.

An implantable loop recorder (ILR) is a small monitor placed under the skin of the chest through a small incision under local anesthetic. It records up to about 3 years, saves auto-triggered and patient-activated events, and needs no electrodes, so it does not limit bathing. The risks are a small chance of infection or pain at the site. It is used when spells are too rare for external monitors.

ISHNE-HRS states the rule plainly: the frequency of symptoms should dictate the type of recording, and correlating symptoms with the rhythm (or showing no arrhythmia during them) is the key result. A 24–48 hour Holter fits palpitations that happen every day or that the patient can reproduce, such as palpitations with exertion or a change in position. Loop and event recorders fit infrequent, unpredictable spells, provided the patient can activate them in time. A usual sequence is continuous monitoring for 24 hours to 7 days, then a loop recorder for weeks to months, then an ILR for patients still undiagnosed. MedlinePlus describes the same order: a 1–2 day Holter first when symptoms are frequent, and an event monitor if no diagnosis is reached or symptoms occur weekly to monthly.

Longer recording raises the yield. ISHNE-HRS reports a trial in which the chance of matching a symptom with a rhythm rose from 22% with a 48-hour Holter to 56% with a month of loop recording, and another in which loop recording reached a symptom-rhythm match in 87% of patients by one month (median 16 days). After a stroke of unknown cause, 30 days of monitoring found AF lasting more than 30 seconds in 16.1% of patients against 3.2% with a 24–48 hour Holter.

Syncope is the special case: loss of consciousness prevents the patient from triggering a recording, so a monitor that records continuously or triggers itself (MCT, an auto-triggered loop recorder, an ILR) is used. Most children under 5 cannot trigger reliably either; parents, caregivers or teachers can activate for them. Monitoring is also ordered without symptoms: to look for silent AF after a stroke, to see how a new heart medicine affects the rate and rhythm, to count PVCs, or after a heart attack. A patient without symptoms still keeps the activity diary (and notes sleep and waking), so that rates can be read in context.

The technician's part in this choice is to notice a mismatch, for example a 24-hour Holter ordered for faints months apart, or an order with no wear time, and to take it to the ordering provider before the patient leaves with a recorder that cannot answer the question.

Hookup, skin and lead systems

An ambulatory recording has to survive days of movement, sweat and sleep with no one there to fix it, so the skin-electrode contact matters more than on a resting ECG. ISHNE-HRS lists four steps: shave the skin if necessary, remove dead skin by rubbing with rough paper or cloth, use electrodes from airtight packages, and check the expiration date. ANMCO adds that Holter electrodes go over prominent bones after the skin is cleaned with alcohol, are secured with tape, and that the patient-cable wires are fixed so they cannot move and create muscle-tremor artifact. Bone moves less than muscle and carries less muscle noise.

Abrasion is the most common skin treatment, and it can irritate the skin. In a 24-hour study of gel electrodes on healthy volunteers' upper arms (Murphy 2021), abrasion gave by far the lowest impedance at the start and stayed significantly lowest at 8 and 24 hours. Untreated skin improved over the day as sweat hydrated it, and by 24 hours every group was below 20 kΩ, so the gap was widest in the first hours of the recording. The authors conclude that removing the outer skin layer is what gives a low, stable contact. Keep the skin free of oils, creams and, as far as possible, sweat (MedlinePlus).

Lead systems vary by recorder; follow the maker's diagram. Wired single-channel set-ups commonly use a chest-modified V5 (CM5), a chest-modified V3 (CM3) or a modified inferior lead, and a lead can be chosen to suit the question (for ischemia, the leads that showed the most ST change on exercise testing). A 12-lead Holter uses the Mason-Likar system, with the arm electrodes below the clavicles and the leg electrode on the lower left torso. EASI derives 12 leads from 5 electrodes without limb electrodes or intercostal counting. Recordings with the limb electrodes on the torso are not equivalent to a standard 12-lead ECG and should not be used for serial comparison with one.

Before the patient leaves, look at each channel. Modern recorders with three or more channels let the technician pick the channel that gives a waveform fit for interpretation; if none does, move the electrodes until one does (ANMCO). A missing channel cannot be recovered after the recording. Check that the patient's ID is in the recorder, that the clock is right (the diary is matched to it minute by minute) and that the battery and memory will last the ordered time. For a transmitting monitor, have the patient send a practice recording while you watch; in one series of 495 children, more than half never sent a legible ECG during symptoms.

Teaching that makes the recording useful

The diary is what turns a recording into an answer. MedlinePlus stresses that symptoms and activities must be recorded accurately so the provider can match them with the findings, and ISHNE-HRS names frequent noncompliance with symptom logs and event markers as a main limit of Holter monitoring. Entries written later from memory are less useful than entries made as each symptom happens. The marker button stamps a time but not the activity or the feeling, so it supports the diary rather than replacing it.

  • Keep to the usual routine; MedlinePlus notes the patient may be asked to exercise if symptoms have come with exercise. Wires that bounce are a signal problem to secure, not a reason to skip the activity.
  • Keep the monitor close to the body day and night; overnight hours are part of the record.
  • Holter: bathe or shower before the hookup; the recorder must not get wet. Patch: water resistant and can stay on in the shower and during exercise (ISHNE-HRS), within the maker's instructions.
  • Avoid lists vary by device. MedlinePlus (Holter): electric blankets, high-voltage areas, magnets and metal detectors. MedlinePlus (event monitors) adds cell phones and electric toothbrushes. NHLBI adds microwave ovens and electric razors and advises keeping phones and tablets away from the monitor.
  • If an electrode loosens, follow the replacement steps taught at the hookup. If the monitor turns off early, or the skin reacts, contact the office that placed it.
  • Event monitors and MCT: the monitoring station explains how to send data, and the center or the ordering provider may call about a concerning rhythm.
  • Return the device and the diary as scheduled. An ILR stays in until the physician removes it.

Skin irritation is the most common problem with these devices (MedlinePlus); it usually settles once the patches are removed. A patient who reports tape or adhesive allergy is reported to the provider before anything is placed. Tell the patient plainly not to count on the monitor to get help (even a transmitting monitor is read after the event reaches the center): NHLBI advises calling 911 for possible heart attack symptoms even when unsure, and not driving or being driven to the hospital.

Artifact and false events

ISHNE-HRS sorts ambulatory artifact into two groups. Body movement, brief loss of skin contact, loose connections, faulty leads, skeletal muscle potentials and ambient noise draw deflections that imitate atrial flutter or fibrillation (pseudo-atrial arrhythmias) or ventricular tachycardia. Intermittent loss of contact, and recorder faults in older systems, create pseudo-pauses that imitate sinus arrest, high-grade AV block or pacemaker malfunction. Most artifact can be recognized by comparing the simultaneous channels: noise is usually worse in one channel, while a true event appears in all of them.

  • A 'pause' in one channel while another keeps beating is signal loss in that channel.
  • A flat line in every channel that starts and ends with a sharp jump, with no P waves and a diary that says 'felt fine', is a disconnection. In true AV block the P waves continue.
  • A low-amplitude channel can make the software miss R waves and report a long R-R; ANMCO shows a 4-second 'pause' that measured about 860 ms in another channel.
  • Noise that copies flutter or VT usually rides over sinus beats that keep their timing and has no T wave of its own.
  • Noise that comes with an activity in the diary (gardening, vacuuming, a car ride) and disappears during sleep points to artifact.

Missing a real arrhythmia is the more serious error. ISHNE-HRS describes errors of commission (calling artifact an arrhythmia, which can lead to unneeded procedures or devices) and errors of omission (missing a serious event). The technician relabels confirmed artifact so the counts are right, and calls something artifact only for a reason seen on the strip (one channel only, sinus beats marching through, a sharp jump at each end of a flat line); when in doubt, it goes to the physician as a possible event.

Scanning, burden and the report

Scanning software detects and classifies beats and quantifies the 'arrhythmic burden', but its algorithms are mostly proprietary with little published accuracy data, so every label the report relies on is checked against the strip. A summary usually covers total beats, minimum, mean and maximum rates with their times, ventricular and supraventricular ectopy counts and runs, the longest pause, AF episodes, and each diary entry with the rhythm at that time. Rhythm names and criteria are on the Rhythm Gallery; this section covers what is measured.

MeasureHow it is reported
PVC burdenPVCs ÷ total beats × 100. Example: 8,400 PVCs in 96,000 beats is 8.75%. Fewer than 10,000 a day, about 10% of beats, is described as a low burden (Scorza 2026).
PVC detailCount, couplets and runs, and whether the shapes are one or several; 12-channel recordings show PVC origin more reliably than 3-channel ones
AF burdenTime in AF ÷ monitored time × 100, always with the monitored time. Example: 18 h of AF in a 168 h (7-day) patch is about 10.7%.
AF detailNumber of episodes, shortest and longest, rate during AF, how episodes start and stop
PausesLongest R-R in seconds with clock time, sleep or awake, symptom, and what lies in the gap
SymptomsEach diary entry or button press with the rhythm at that minute

Only continuous recorders give a true AF burden. ISHNE-HRS notes that auto-triggered event recorders detect when AF starts but are not designed to record when it ends, so they cannot give a consistent burden, while patches and MCT give an accurate burden for the recording period. Grigoriou 2026 adds that burden from different monitoring methods cannot be compared directly and that the monitored time must be reported with it. The commonly used minimum duration for an AF episode is 30 seconds; ISHNE-HRS notes that this cutoff is not based on outcome data.

For a pause, measure from the last QRS before the gap to the first after it (at 25 mm/s each large box is 0.20 s), then march out the atrial activity through the gap. No P waves points to the sinus node; a gap that is an exact multiple of the P-P interval fits sinoatrial exit block (LITFL); an on-time P wave with no QRS is AV block. Name the level, because the physician's decision depends on it.

Symptom correlation works in both directions. A symptom during sinus rhythm is a real result: it points away from an arrhythmic cause, and ISHNE-HRS counts showing no arrhythmia during recurrent symptoms as one of the two aims of monitoring for syncope. In one short-term Holter study it cites, symptoms matched an arrhythmia in 4% of patients and occurred without one in 17%. An arrhythmia with no symptom logged is listed as its own finding. The technician compiles and flags; the physician interprets.

Notification and escalation

Monitors that transmit by cellular link send data around the clock, so a practice must be able to handle incoming data 24 hours a day, every day of the year. Vendors' default criteria tend to overstate the significance of findings and generate needless calls to physicians and EMS, so ISHNE-HRS recommends that each practice set its own. Its Table 6 is one example, built to limit unnecessary calls while catching life-threatening events.

A Holter lab sees events after the fact, which makes prompt reporting more pressing, not less: a serious finding on a returned recording goes to the reading physician under the lab's urgent-findings protocol. At a remote center, follow the escalation steps in order and document each attempt. When the patient is in front of you and unwell, the order changes: stay with the patient and call for the nurse, the provider or the code team as your setting directs, and start CPR or use an AED if you are trained and the patient is unresponsive without normal breathing.

Hospital telemetry and central monitoring

In hospital, telemetry sends the ECG wirelessly from ambulatory patients on a ward to a central station, usually with 3, 5 or 6 leads. ISHNE-HRS makes skin preparation, electrode placement and connections the first step: electrodes were misplaced in 26% of patients in one study, and education improved it. Between 72% and 99% of hospital alarms have no immediate clinical relevance, and the resulting alarm fatigue has caused missed events and deaths. Better skin preparation with fine sandpaper and daily electrode replacement cut alarms by 46%.

Who recognizes and escalates an arrhythmia varies between hospitals: nurses, monitoring technicians and sometimes nonclinical staff. In one central monitoring model ISHNE-HRS describes, a technician watched up to 48 patients, lead technicians helped with complex rhythms, and a dedicated crisis phone reached the unit in an emergency; monitoring technicians there commonly held the CCT or CRAT credential. The technician's job is the same as in an ambulatory lab: tell artifact from a true event, check the patient's own nurse sees it, and escalate by protocol when the nurse cannot be reached.

Documentation

  • Hookup: identifiers checked against the order; device type and serial number; ID and clock set in the recorder; start date and time; ordered wear time; lead set-up and any nonstandard electrode sites; skin condition, preparation and any allergy; teaching given and the patient's repeat-back; diary given; return date; practice transmission sent.
  • Return or removal: date and time removed; reason if early (device stopped, skin reaction, patient choice); skin condition; diary returned; download complete, opened and filed under the right patient before the recorder is cleared.
  • Events and escalation: the time the event occurred and the time it was found; the rhythm and its measurements; how it was triggered and any symptom; who was told, when, and what they said.
  • Report: monitored time, the burden figures, the longest pause and each diary correlation. ISHNE-HRS describes reports issued as PDFs (per event, daily, weekly or end of monitoring) and filed in the electronic record.
