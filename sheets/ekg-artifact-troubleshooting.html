Artifact and Lead Problems

Tremor, 60-cycle interference, wandering baseline, loose electrodes, lead-off and cable faults, artifact that mimics VT, VF or a pause, and limb and chest lead reversals

The patient first

Safety Before you fix any tracing, look at the patient. Alert and talking: it is not VF or asystole, so look for the cause. Unresponsive and not breathing normally: call for help and the code team, and start CPR and use the AED if you are trained. Never silence or ignore an alarm because the last one was artifact.
  1. Check the patient, then a second signal: pulse, SpO₂ or arterial waveform.
  2. Look at the baseline: fuzz, regular sawtooth, slow drift, jumps or a flat line.
  3. Find which leads carry it (table below), then check those electrodes and wires.
  4. Check the cable, the machine settings and the room.
  5. Fix the cause, record again, and label anything you changed.

Try one

Artifact and Lead Problems practice question

In a Holter scanning lab, the software labels long daytime stretches 'atrial flutter, 4:1' for a 71-year-old patient with essential tremor. Regular sawtooth-like waves at about 300/min fill both channels, and the narrow QRS complexes are regular at 75/min. From 23:00 to 06:00, while the diary says the patient slept, the waves are gone and an upright P wave precedes each QRS at the same rate. Which finding MOST strongly supports artifact rather than flutter?

  1. The waves disappear during sleep
  2. The wave rate is near 300/min
  3. The R-R intervals stay regular
  4. No flat baseline lies between the waves

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Artifact follows the activity that causes it; a tremor that settles in sleep explains waves that disappear overnight while the ventricular rate stays at 75/min.
  • B. About 300/min is the typical atrial rate of flutter itself, so this rate fits flutter at least as well as tremor.
  • C. Regular R-R intervals argue against atrial fibrillation, which makes this tempting, but flutter with a fixed 4:1 ratio is also regular.
  • D. A continuous, undulating baseline can look like noise, but typical flutter also lacks an isoelectric segment between its waves.

Tremor at 4 to 6 per second can draw regular waves that copy the 300/min sawtooth of flutter, so rate, regularity and shape do not separate them. Timing does: artifact follows the activity that causes it. Essential tremor is an action tremor that is less noticeable at rest and settles while the patient sleeps, whereas a flutter circuit does not stop for sleep and restart each morning at the same ventricular rate. Upright P waves at the same rate overnight show sinus rhythm was present throughout, so the technician relabels the daytime stretches as tremor artifact and notes it for the reading physician.

Chest lead problems

  • R waves should grow and S waves shrink from V1 toward V5. One lead out of step with its neighbors: suspect two chest wires swapped.
  • V1 and V2 too high (2nd or 3rd space) can fake incomplete RBBB, T inversion, septal Q waves or ST elevation. Count down to the 4th space.
  • A limb wire swapped with a chest wire makes the tracing look bizarre: limb leads take on chest-lead size and most leads change.
  • Machine statements often miss misplaced electrodes. Your check comes first.
Exam tip Exams test: 12 tiny regular spikes per large box = 60-cycle; irregular fuzz = tremor; slow drift = wandering baseline. Clean lead I = left leg. Flat limb lead = neutral (RL) wire swapped. Inverted lead I with normal chest leads = arm reversal. 'VT' with QRS marching through and a steady pleth = artifact. Fix the cause before using a filter, and label any filter.

In depth

The one-page summary above is what the PDF prints. These sections go further.

Why artifact matters

Artifact is any signal on the tracing that did not come from the heart. It costs in two directions. Read as an arrhythmia, it can lead to drugs, procedures or devices the patient did not need: in one case report, runs of artifact called VT were treated before review showed QRS complexes inside them (DeLeon 2023). Dismissed as artifact, a real event is missed. On monitors false alarms, artifact among their causes, drive alarm fatigue. In a month of monitoring in five adult ICUs, 88.8% of 12,671 annotated arrhythmia alarms were false, including 86.8% of VT alarms, 67.0% of asystole alarms and 87.7% of pause alarms (Drew 2014).

The NHA CET test plan names three artifacts by name (wandering baseline, somatic tremor and AC interference) and asks the technician to identify, troubleshoot and resolve them, and to know lead placement and troubleshooting. Misplaced electrodes belong to the same job: they produce tracings that look like disease.

How the leads are built, and why it locates the problem

Each standard limb lead records between two electrodes: lead I between the right and left arms, lead II between the right arm and left leg, lead III between the left arm and left leg. The augmented leads (aVR, aVL, aVF) and the chest leads are measured against combinations of the same three limb electrodes (the central terminal). The right leg electrode is the neutral or ground; it is not part of any lead.

So a bad limb electrode spoils every lead that uses it, and the limb lead that stays clean names the culprit. Gursoy 2021 sets it out: if only lead I is normal, the left leg electrode is at fault; if only lead II is normal, the left arm; with the right arm electrode, lead III is minimally affected or no limb lead is fully clean. Because the chest leads use the central terminal, a bad limb electrode often shows faintly in all six chest leads too. A problem in one chest lead only points to that electrode. A problem in every lead at once points to the cable, the machine or the room (Mondal 2026), with one exception: a whole-body tremor or shivering moves every electrode.

Somatic tremor and muscle artifact

Skeletal muscle produces electrical potentials far larger than the heart's; Mondal 2026 gives up to 10 times larger. Any muscle work near an electrode therefore shows: a cold or shivering patient, clenched fists, stiff arms, moving fingers, talking, anxiety or pain. On the tracing it is an irregular, jagged baseline, fine or coarse, that can hide P waves and imitate atrial fibrillation, flutter or VT.

A true tremor is more regular. Mondal gives 4 to 10 Hz for tremor and about 5 Hz for a Parkinson rest tremor. Five waves a second is 300 a minute, the rate of flutter waves, so a tremor can draw a convincing false flutter with a normal ventricular rate underneath. Clues that it is tremor: sinus P waves show through when the limb is still, the waves are worst in the leads that use the shaking limb, and they come and go with the tremor (for example, an action tremor fades in sleep on a Holter).

  • Warm the room and the patient; let a shivering patient settle before recording.
  • Position about 45 degrees with the limbs supported by the bed (SCST); the patient's limbs should look still and relaxed before you press record.
  • For tremor, SCST suggests asking the patient to tuck both hands gently behind the legs.
  • Significant tremor is one of the few reasons SCST accepts moving limb electrodes up the limbs; label the tracing with the positions used. Limb electrodes do not go on the torso for a diagnostic 12-lead, because it changes wave amplitudes.
  • If relaxing and supporting the patient fails, SCST allows the 40 Hz low-pass filter on a repeat recording, clearly labeled. It smooths muscle noise but also distorts the ECG, and filtering can distort pacing spikes (Mondal 2026), so a filtered tracing is never the first attempt.

AC (60-cycle) interference

Mains electricity alternates at 60 Hz in the United States (50 Hz in many other countries). Pickup from power cords and plugged-in equipment draws a thick baseline of tiny, identical, perfectly regular oscillations at that frequency, usually across all leads (Mondal 2026). At 25 mm/s a large box is 0.2 seconds, so 60 Hz is 12 oscillations per large box. That regularity separates it from muscle artifact, which is irregular.

  • Route the patient cable away from power cords; do not drape lead wires over other devices.
  • Switch off electrical equipment that is not needed during the recording (Mondal 2026); never equipment the patient depends on, and ask the nurse before touching anything connected to the patient.
  • Check electrode contact: fresh electrodes on clean, dry skin.
  • Filters: SCST presets the mains filter off and wants all filters off for the first recording. Mondal lists the notch filter as a remedy. Remove the source first; use a notch filter only if your lab's procedure allows it, and label it.

Other equipment draws its own patterns. Electrocautery gives high-frequency noise that can look like flutter or VT. Ventilators, infusion pumps and dialysis machines can give rhythmic oscillations that follow the device. Neurostimulators give periodic pulses, and an LVAD's continuous rotation gives persistent high-frequency noise that can hide the underlying rhythm (Mondal 2026). The technician does not stop such equipment: record the best tracing possible, note the device on it, and tell the nurse or provider if the noise hides the waveforms.

Wandering baseline

The isoelectric line should be stable (SCST). In wandering baseline it slowly rises and falls, often with breathing, because breathing changes chest impedance; electrode movement and poor skin contact add drift (Mondal 2026). It matters because a baseline that moves can make the ST segment look raised or depressed. Fix it at the skin: clean and dry the sites, lightly abrade, place fresh electrodes, keep the patient relaxed and still, and repeat. The machine's low-frequency filter is part of the diagnostic preset (SCST: 0.67 Hz in auto mode, 0.05 Hz in manual mode); raising it to hide wander distorts the ST segment.

Electrodes, skin and lead-off

Most contact problems start with the electrode or the skin. SCST asks the technician to check the use-by date, to confirm that the gel core of a wet-gel electrode has not dried, and to keep electrodes in their sealed foil pouch until use. ISHNE-HRS adds airtight packages, attention to expiry dates, shaving where needed and light abrasion of dead skin. Dried gel raises impedance and noise, and the skin must be clean and dry; sweat spoils contact (Mondal 2026).

A poorly attached electrode gives sudden baseline jumps, spikes or brief flat lines in the leads that use it. A lifted electrode or a wire off its snap gives a flat line or a lead-fault message in that lead. Do not rely on the machine statement to flag lead problems (in Ramadurai 2025 it recognized none of 15 misplacements), so check every lead before you send the tracing.

Cables: SCST asks for a visual check before use for fractures, faults and damaged insulation. Fractured lead wires and loose connectors give sudden spikes, dropouts or continuous signal loss; Mondal advises inspecting leads and cables for cracks, fractures or stretching, securing them so they are not tugged, and replacing any that look suspect. Wires pulled tight from a cart parked too far away tug the electrodes with every movement. Park close, let the wires lie slack, and swap in another cable when artifact persists in every lead with a still patient and fresh electrodes.

Pulse tapping: a limb electrode over a pulsing artery moves with each beat and draws a wave at a fixed delay after every QRS. It can mimic ST elevation or a long QT. The wave does not line up with the J point and may start before or after the QRS onset, and one limb lead is spared, which names the electrode (Gursoy 2021, Mondal 2026). Move that electrode off the pulse and repeat.

Artifact that mimics VT, VF or a pause

Motion, loose electrodes, tremor and electrical interference can produce fast, wide, irregular deflections that the monitor calls VT or VF, and dropouts that it calls asystole, a pause or AV block. For what the real rhythms look like, see the Rhythm Gallery. The tests below separate them.

  • The patient. Mondal 2026 makes assessing the patient, before the tracing, the first step. A patient who is awake and talking, with a steady SpO₂ or arterial waveform, is not in VF.
  • Native QRS complexes. Normal QRS complexes that keep appearing on time inside the burst are the strongest tracing clue. In DeLeon 2023, subtle QRS complexes were found buried in what had been read as VT.
  • The other leads. Artifact often fills some leads and spares others; true VF and asystole show in every lead. In Drew 2014, QRS complexes were visible in at least one lead in 91% of false asystole alarms and 94% of false pause alarms. ISHNE-HRS: most Holter artifact can be recognized from the simultaneous channels.
  • A second signal. An SpO₂ or arterial waveform that keeps pulsing at the old rate argues for artifact (Drew 2014 counts an SpO₂ waveform pulsing at the underlying rate as proof of a false alarm); in fast VT the pulse waveform often shrinks or vanishes. In DeLeon's second case the monitor read 199 while the oximeter read 60.
  • Rate. Apparent rates of 400 to 600 a minute are beyond human limits; VT rarely exceeds 250 to 300 (Mondal 2026).
  • Timing. Artifact starts and stops with its cause: brushing teeth, scratching, shivering, a pump, getting dressed. A pause with a sharp jump at each end, flat in every channel, with no P waves and a patient who felt fine, points to signal loss; report it with the diary entry rather than dismissing it.

Pseudo-pauses come from intermittent loss of electrode contact or recorder faults and can simulate sinus arrest, pacemaker malfunction or high-grade AV block (ISHNE-HRS). Low-amplitude QRS complexes cause the same false alarms on monitors: in Drew 2014 one patient with small limb-lead complexes produced 45.2% of all annotated alarms while a V lead showed good complexes. Drew recommends analyzing a lead with adequate QRS amplitude; changing the analyzed lead is done under unit policy, with the nurse informed.

Safety Judge each alarm on its own tracing. A patient with three tremor alarms today can still have real VT on the fourth: wide complexes in every lead, no native QRS between them and a flat pleth mean alert the nurse now and follow the emergency protocol.

Limb lead reversals in detail

Batchvarov 2007 puts incorrect cable connections at about 0.4% to 4% of 12-lead ECGs, highest in intensive care. In Ramadurai 2025, 15 of 1,000 consecutive ECGs (1.5%) had misplaced electrodes confirmed on a repeat: 11 limb reversals (8 LA/LL, 3 RA/LA) and 4 chest-lead exchanges. The machine's automated interpretation recognized none of them. Swapping two limb electrodes turns the frontal-plane leads upside down or exchanges them; swapping with the neutral electrode makes one lead flat (LITFL).

  • LA and RA is the familiar one, and it can look like dextrocardia. The chest leads separate them: with arm reversal R waves still grow from V1 toward V5; in true dextrocardia R-wave progression is poor or reversed (SCST, LITFL).
  • LA and LL is the hardest to see, because the tracing still looks plausible. The clue is a P wave larger in lead I than in lead II, the reverse of normal, with lead III inverted. It was the most common reversal in Ramadurai 2025, and Batchvarov reports one detection algorithm caught only 17.9% of LA/LL swaps.
  • Neutral electrode swaps also distort the central terminal, so chest-lead voltages can change slightly (LITFL, Batchvarov).
  • A limb lead that is a flat line (no P, QRS or T at all) on a still patient with every electrode attached points to a neutral-wire swap: both of its electrodes are now on the legs, which sit at nearly the same potential (LITFL, Batchvarov).
  • If no single swap explains the tracing, more than two wires may have been moved in a circle (LITFL). Recheck every label against its limb.

Known dextrocardia: SCST records a second tracing with V3 to V6 on the right chest, labeled V3R to V6R, with V1 and V2 in their usual places. SCST's alternative is to swap the arm wires to normalize the limb leads; if that approach is used, the tracing must be very clearly annotated so the dextrocardia is not overlooked. When you have confirmed the wires are correct and lead I is still inverted, do not change wires on your own to make it look normal: send it as recorded with a note that the wires were checked, and follow the lab's procedure or the provider's order for the right-sided repeat.

Chest lead swaps and misplacement

From V1 to V6 the R wave grows and the S wave shrinks, and P and T waves change in a predictable way. Batchvarov 2007 recognizes a chest-cable interchange by a break in that pattern: an absent normal R-wave progression or a disordered S-wave progression. Infarction and hypertrophy can do the same, which is why the wires are checked before the tracing is read. Examples: a V1/V3 swap gives a smaller R and deeper S in V3 than in V1, or a T wave inverted in V3 alone; an R wave taller in V6 than in V5 can be hypertrophy or a V5/V6 swap. In Ramadurai 2025 the four chest errors were V2/V3, V2/V4, V3/V5 and a V2-V5 shuffle.

  • Chest wire swapped with the neutral (RL) wire: that chest lead records a leg signal and looks like an unaugmented aVF; the other leads look unchanged (Batchvarov).
  • Limb wire swapped with a chest wire: the central terminal is distorted, most leads change shape and at most one of I, II and III stays normal; the tracing looks bizarre (Batchvarov).
  • V1 and V2 placed too high, in the 2nd or 3rd intercostal space, are a common error and can produce incomplete RBBB, anterior T-wave inversion, septal Q waves or ST elevation (Gregory 2021, quoting Walsh 2018). In that study only 3 of 52 paramedics placed every chest electrode accurately, and correct V1 placement went with better placement of the rest.
  • V2 amplitude varies between recordings with small placement changes, so an amplitude difference alone is not proof of a swap (Batchvarov).

Documenting and reporting

  • Label every change from standard on the tracing: electrode positions moved (upper arms, right-sided or posterior leads), filter on, gain changed (SCST allows 5 mm/mV when complexes overlap), patient position, wheelchair (SCST); a device that adds noise, such as an LVAD.
  • If the tracing cannot be improved, annotate why it is suboptimal (SCST), for example a tremor that would not settle.
  • Note symptoms present at the time of recording, such as chest pain, palpitations or dizziness (SCST).
  • A reversed tracing that was already sent: repeat it correctly and, per policy, tell the reader about the first one.
  • Telemetry: routing is set by unit policy. In Mitchell 2026 technical alerts (low battery, leads off, many artifacts) go to tele-runners, while loss of signal, abnormal waveforms, lethal arrhythmias and patient deterioration go to the bedside nurse, and changes are reported immediately. Technicians collect and report; they do not diagnose.
Exam tip Exam answers favor the fix at the source (warm, support, re-prep, new electrode, slack wires, cable away from cords) over gain, speed or filters, and favor checking the patient over printing or silencing.
