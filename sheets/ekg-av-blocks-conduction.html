Measure before you name

  • At 25 mm/s one small box is 0.04 s. Normal PR 0.12–0.20 s (3–5 small boxes); a QRS of 0.12 s (3 small boxes) or more is wide.
  • The PR runs from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS, a small q wave included.
  • March out the P waves with calipers: does every P have a QRS, and is the PR the same each time?
  • Name a bundle branch block from V1 and from I or V6, not from lead II. Drawn strips of each AV block: see the Rhythm Gallery.

Try one

AV Blocks, Bundle Branch Blocks and Pre-excitation practice question

In a Holter scanning lab, a technician reviews a recording from an 80-year-old patient with dizzy spells and a known left bundle branch block. Daytime sinus beats show an upright P wave, a PR of 0.18 s and a QRS of 0.14 s. From 01:50 to 02:15 the P waves disappear, and a regular rhythm at 44/min follows, with QRS complexes 0.14 s wide and identical in shape to the daytime beats. How should the technician label the overnight rhythm?

  1. Junctional escape rhythm
  2. Accelerated idioventricular rhythm
  3. Ventricular escape rhythm
  4. Sinus bradycardia with left bundle branch block

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The rate sits in the junctional escape range of 40 to 60/min, and a QRS identical to the conducted beats shows the impulses travel down the same bundle-branch pathway, so the width comes from the old block.
  • B. AIVR is also wide without P waves, but it runs at about 50 to 110/min and starts in the ventricles, so its QRS would differ from the conducted beats.
  • C. The wide QRS and missing P waves fit a ventricular escape, which makes this tempting, but a ventricular focus would change the QRS shape and usually fires at 20 to 40/min.
  • D. The QRS matches the patient's bundle branch block, but sinus bradycardia needs an upright P wave before each QRS, and none is seen in this stretch.

Width alone does not prove a ventricular origin. An impulse that starts in the AV junction travels down the bundle branches like a sinus beat, so in a patient with a bundle branch block it is drawn with the same wide QRS as the conducted daytime beats. A ventricular focus spreads from a new starting point, which changes the QRS shape, and it usually fires at 20 to 40/min. Comparing escape complexes with the patient's own conducted beats is therefore the key step. The technician labels the rhythm and reports the loss of sinus P waves with its times and the diary entries for the reading physician.

Measuring the PR and the pauses

AV block is diagnosed from the relationship between P waves and QRS complexes, so the P waves are measured first and on their own. Set the calipers on two consecutive P waves and walk them across the strip, through the pauses and through the T waves, where a P wave can hide. Then measure the PR on every conducted beat. The full method for measuring intervals is on the Paper, Settings and Measurement page.

The PR is rate-dependent. The Brazilian ECG guideline defines first-degree block as a PR over 200 ms at a heart rate of 50 to 90/min, and its exercise guideline notes that the PR normally shortens as the rate rises, to about 100 ms in healthy people at high exercise rates. A PR that is merely long at rest often shortens with exercise. A PR over 0.3 s may cause exercise intolerance (Merck); the exercise guideline gives the reason: the PR fails to shorten as the rate rises, so atrial contraction falls too close to the previous ventricular contraction. First-degree block is found in up to 7.8% of healthy people, and its usual site is the AV node (da Silva 2021).

Pauses sort themselves by what lies inside them. A pause that holds an on-time P wave with no QRS is AV block. A pause with an early, differently shaped P wave on the preceding T wave is a blocked PAC. A pause with no P wave at all is sinus arrest or SA exit block: LITFL notes that SA block shows the same patterns as AV block, but can be judged only from the P waves, because sinus node firing itself leaves no mark on the ECG.

Second-degree block: Mobitz I, Mobitz II and 2:1

In Mobitz I (Wenckebach) the PR lengthens with each beat until a P wave is not conducted. The PR is longest just before the dropped beat and shortest just after it. The biggest increase usually comes between the first and second beats, so the increments shrink and the R-R intervals shorten as the cycle goes on, while the P-P stays about constant (LITFL). The pause is less than twice the duration of any other R-R interval (Merck). Cycles are named by P waves to QRS complexes, such as 3:2, 4:3 or 5:4. Mobitz I with a narrow QRS is nearly always in the AV node; with a QRS of 0.12 s or more it is in the His-Purkinje system in 60–70% of patients (Merck, da Silva 2021). Mobitz I occurs in 1–2% of healthy young people, especially during sleep (da Silva 2021).

In Mobitz II the PR stays constant before and after a P wave that suddenly fails to conduct, and the R-R spanning the block equals twice the other R-R intervals (Merck): an exact multiple, double for one dropped P and triple for two (LITFL). The block lies below the AV node. LITFL gives a broad QRS in about 75% of cases, where the block is in the bundle branches, and a narrow QRS in about 25%, where it is within the His bundle. Mobitz II fails suddenly and unpredictably, often in a patient who already has LBBB or bifascicular block, and LITFL gives the risk of asystole as about 35% a year.

In 2:1 block, conducted and blocked P waves alternate with a regular P-P and a constant PR on the conducted beats. Because two conducted beats never come in a row, there is no way to see whether the PR would have lengthened, so the strip is named '2:1 AV block' and not Mobitz I or II. Clues to the level (da Silva 2021): a normal PR and QRS point to the AV node or within the His bundle; a bundle branch block points below the His bundle. The Brazilian 2022 guideline adds that most 2:1 blocks are intra- or infra-His, and its exercise guideline notes that 2:1 block can be preceded by a run of Mobitz I or II and that 2:1 block brought on by a faster rate is usually His-Purkinje disease. A longer recording that shows a Wenckebach cycle or a Mobitz II drop in the same patient helps the reader. A blocked PAC on every second beat can imitate 2:1 block; the P-P will not be regular.

Terms differ by source. Merck calls any block of every second P wave or more 'high-grade'; da Silva and the Brazilian guidelines keep 2:1 separate and reserve 'advanced' or 'high-grade' for 3:1 or higher, where fewer than half of the sinus P waves conduct. The bank follows the second usage.

High-grade and third-degree block

High-grade (advanced) block conducts fewer than half of the sinus P waves, in a 3:1, 4:1 or higher ratio. Conduction is recognized by the same PR on every beat that does conduct, and most of these blocks are intra- or infra-His (Brazilian 2022). Junctional escape beats may fill the long pauses; an escape beat comes late, at the end of a pause, not early.

In third-degree (complete) block no P wave reaches the ventricles. A pacemaker below the block takes over, the P waves and QRS complexes are unrelated, and the atrial rate is faster than the escape rate. Measure the atrial and the ventricular rates separately. The escape tells the level: a junctional escape above the bifurcation of the His bundle is narrow at about 35 to 55/min; a ventricular escape is wide at about 20 to 40/min and less reliable (Merck, da Silva 2021). A supra-Hisian escape looks like the patient's usual QRS (Brazilian 2022), so a patient with a known bundle branch block has a wide junctional escape; compare with the old tracing before calling it ventricular.

Causes, as background for the report: idiopathic fibrosis of the conduction system (about 40%) and ischemic heart disease (about 20%) lead; drugs (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin, amiodarone), high vagal tone, sarcoidosis and Lyme carditis also cause block. Block in inferior MI is usually nodal and often resolves within days; block in anterior MI reflects necrosis of the His-Purkinje system (Merck).

Safety Mobitz II, high-grade and third-degree block can progress to ventricular standstill without warning. Report at once, look at the patient, and be ready to call the code team and start CPR if the patient becomes unresponsive and pulseless.

AV dissociation that is not block

AV dissociation means only that the atria and ventricles are driven by separate pacemakers (LITFL). Complete heart block is one cause. Others need no block at all: the sinus rate falls below the rate of a lower pacemaker, or a junctional or ventricular pacemaker speeds up past the sinus rate, as in AIVR, junctional tachycardia or VT. The Brazilian 2022 guideline lists the mechanisms as replacement, interference, AV block and arrhythmia; it describes two dissociated rhythms, usually a regular sinus rhythm and a regular junctional or ventricular rhythm, and calls the form in which their rates are similar isorhythmic dissociation.

  • In complete block the atrial rate is the faster one. When the ventricular rate equals or exceeds the atrial rate, the dissociation is from a faster lower pacemaker, not from block.
  • Example: a trained athlete asleep whose sinus rate slows below the junction's rate, so the P waves drift into and out of a narrow QRS.
  • Example: AIVR after a coronary artery is reopened, wide complexes a little faster than the sinus P waves.
  • Capture beat: a sinus P wave that arrives when the AV node and ventricles are ready conducts, giving an early beat with a normal QRS. Fusion beat: a sinus and a ventricular impulse meet and make a hybrid complex (LITFL VT versus SVT). Both prove the AV node can conduct, so the rhythm is not complete block.
  • Interference dissociation with a junctional tachycardia has been reported after an MI (Ying 2023).

Right and left bundle branch block

When a bundle branch fails, that ventricle is activated late, muscle cell to muscle cell, so the QRS widens to 0.12 s or more and changes shape. Normal adult ventricular activation gives a QRS of 0.11 s or less (Merck). Abnormal depolarization brings abnormal repolarization: the ST segment and T wave point opposite the main or terminal QRS deflection. This 'appropriate discordance' is expected and is not ischemia.

Criteria above are Merck's and LITFL's; the Brazilian 2022 guideline accepts 120 ms as the minimum for LBBB but notes that classical LBBB is 130 ms or more in women and 140 ms or more in men, with an axis usually between −30° and +60°. Prevalence figures are da Silva 2021.

RBBB can appear without heart disease, but it is also caused by right ventricular strain (cor pulmonale, pulmonary embolism, where a transient RBBB signals significant right ventricular dysfunction), ischemic, rheumatic and congenital heart disease, myocarditis, cardiomyopathy and degenerative disease of the conduction system. Isolated RBBB without other conduction disease does not appear to raise future risk (Merck). The right bundle is usually supplied by perforating branches of the LAD, so a new RBBB with chest pain is highly concerning for an occluded artery (LITFL). Brugada syndrome can also draw an RSR′ in V1–V3. An rSR′ with a QRS under 0.12 s is incomplete RBBB (LITFL), which can be a normal variant; check that V1 and V2 were not placed too high, which can draw the same rSr′ pattern (Brazilian 2022).

LBBB is unusual without organic heart disease (LITFL): aortic stenosis, ischemic heart disease, hypertension, dilated cardiomyopathy, anterior MI, degenerative conduction disease, hyperkalemia and digoxin toxicity. Unlike RBBB, it carries a higher risk of future cardiac events and death (Merck), and it makes the two ventricles contract out of step, which can worsen heart failure. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement causes new LBBB in 14.0 to 45.2% of patients across studies (da Silva 2021).

LBBB hides an infarct. Its expected discordant ST elevation after the deep S waves of V1–V3 is generally under 25% of the depth of that S wave (LITFL). The findings a provider looks for (Sgarbossa, weighted 5, 3 and 2 points in the original score): ST elevation of 1 mm or more in the same direction as the QRS; ST depression of 1 mm or more in V1, V2 or V3; and discordant ST elevation of 5 mm or more (Brazilian 2022). The tech's job is to recognize concordant changes and get the tracing, with an old one, to the provider; the score is the provider's.

Bundle branch block can be intermittent or rate-related: a bundle that conducts at a slow rate fails at a faster one (acceleration-dependent block), more rarely the reverse, and an early beat in AF after a long cycle can be conducted with an RBBB shape (Ashman phenomenon; see the Rhythm Gallery). Da Silva 2021 lists acceleration- and deceleration-dependent block, the Ashman phenomenon, pre-excitation, drugs and electrolyte disturbances among the causes of intermittent conduction changes. A slow sinus rhythm with a bundle branch block is still sinus: check for a P wave before every wide QRS before accepting a monitor's 'ventricular' label.

Fascicular blocks, bifascicular and 'trifascicular' block

The left bundle divides into an anterior and a posterior fascicle. Block of one of them shifts the axis while the QRS stays under 0.12 s, because the other fascicle still reaches the left ventricle, about 20 ms late (LITFL).

LPFB is diagnosed only after other causes of right axis deviation are excluded: right ventricular hypertrophy, acute pulmonary embolism, tricyclic overdose and lateral STEMI (LITFL), and a slender build or old lateral infarct (Brazilian 2022). Left axis deviation has other causes too; LAFB needs an initial r wave in II, III and aVF, so QS complexes there with left axis point to something else, such as an old inferior infarct, and the differential diagnosis includes body build and pathologic Q waves (da Silva 2021).

Bifascicular block is RBBB with LAFB (left axis; up to 1.5% of adults) or with LPFB (right axis). Conduction then depends on the one remaining fascicle. Progression to complete block runs about 1–4% a year overall and about 1% a year without symptoms, but 17% a year in patients who present with syncope (LITFL), so a history of fainting goes in the report. LBBB itself is sometimes counted as bifascicular, because both left fascicles are involved (da Silva 2021).

True trifascicular block is bifascicular block with third-degree block. Bifascicular block with first-degree block is still often called 'trifascicular' (once 'incomplete trifascicular'), but LITFL, Merck and da Silva all call this inaccurate: the long PR usually comes from the AV node, not the third fascicle, and LITFL reports that the AHA/ACCF/HRS recommends against the term. First-degree block does not change the roughly 1% yearly progression in asymptomatic bifascicular block (LITFL). Alternating bundle branch block, RBBB on some beats and LBBB on others, or RBBB with alternating LAFB and LPFB, is trifascicular disease (Merck) and is reported at once.

Nonspecific intraventricular conduction delay

A wide QRS (Merck: over 0.12 s) that fits neither the RBBB nor the LBBB pattern is a nonspecific intraventricular conduction delay (IVCD). It can come from slow conduction through the muscle beyond the Purkinje fibers, as in severe hypertrophy and cardiomyopathy (Brazilian 2022 calls this parietal or focal block). Its risk of later cardiac events and death is similar to LBBB, so it prompts a search for structural heart disease (Merck). Before calling a wide QRS an IVCD, consider what else widens it: pre-excitation (look for the short PR and delta wave), ventricular pacing (look for spikes), hyperkalemia and sodium-channel-blocking drugs.

Pre-excitation and WPW

In pre-excitation an accessory pathway, a strand of muscle across the AV ring, carries the atrial impulse to part of the ventricles before the AV node does. That early activation is the delta wave, and it shortens the PR. The rest of the QRS is drawn by normal conduction through the AV node, which arrives and takes over (LITFL). Each QRS is therefore a fusion of two routes, and anything that slows the AV node, such as vagal tone during a Valsalva maneuver or AV-nodal blocking drugs, shifts more of the ventricle onto the pathway and makes the delta wave bigger (LITFL). By the same logic a premature atrial impulse, slowed in a partly recovered AV node, gives a wider pre-excited beat.

  • Criteria: PR under 120 ms in adults (under 90 ms in children, Brazilian 2022); delta wave; QRS wide by LITFL (over 110 ms), Merck (0.12 s or more) or the Brazilian guideline (over 120 ms), though a small delta wave can leave it near normal; ST-T changes opposite the delta wave and QRS.
  • Pattern and syndrome: the ECG findings are the WPW pattern; with episodes of tachycardia they are WPW syndrome. Manifest pre-excitation is found in about 1 to 3 per 1,000 people (Merck; 0.1–0.3%, Brazilian 2024).
  • Location (LITFL): type A, a left-sided pathway, has positive delta waves in all chest leads and R/S over 1 in V1, which can mimic a posterior MI; type B, a right-sided pathway, has negative delta waves in V1 and V2. Left lateral pathways are the most common, about 50% (Brazilian 2022).
  • Pseudo-infarction: negative delta waves can look like Q waves, and tall R waves in V1–V3 like a posterior MI, in up to 70% of patients (LITFL).
  • Mimics: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and storage diseases such as Fabry disease can look pre-excited (Brazilian 2022). A short PR with a normal QRS and no delta wave is the 'Lown-Ganong-Levine' pattern, whose existence as a syndrome is disputed (LITFL). Mahaim-type pathways may leave the resting ECG normal and produce a tachycardia with an LBBB shape.
  • Intermittent pre-excitation: a delta wave that comes and goes, or that disappears abruptly from one beat to the next as the rate climbs on a treadmill, means the pathway cannot conduct fast. Elendu 2025 calls this sudden loss the normal response and links it to a lower risk; pre-excitation that persists at high rates is the concern.

The tachycardias: in orthodromic AVRT, about 95% of AV reentrant tachycardias, the circuit runs down the AV node and back up the pathway, so the QRS is narrow (unless there is a bundle branch block), the rate is 120 to 250/min and regular, the delta wave disappears, and a negative P wave sits in the first half of the R-R in II, III and aVF (Merck). A concealed pathway, which conducts only backward, gives a normal resting ECG but the same orthodromic tachycardia. In antidromic AVRT the circuit runs down the pathway, so every beat is fully pre-excited and the tachycardia is regular and wide; LITFL notes it may be impossible to tell from VT without the history or an old tracing showing a delta wave.

Pre-excited AF: why it is urgent and what to measure

AF develops in up to 50% of patients with WPW syndrome (Schiavone 2024). The AV node normally limits how many fibrillatory impulses reach the ventricles; a pathway with a short refractory period does not, so the ventricular rate can reach 200 to 300/min, with R-R intervals as short as 160 ms, and the rhythm can degenerate to VF and sudden death (Merck). The tracing is fast, irregularly irregular and mostly wide, and because each beat is a different mix of pathway and AV node conduction, the QRS width and shape change from beat to beat, with an occasional narrower complex that does not follow a long pause (Merck). AF with a fixed bundle branch block, the main look-alike, keeps one QRS shape.

The shortest pre-excited R-R interval (SPERRI) estimates how fast the pathway can conduct: 250 ms or less marks a pathway linked to VF (Schiavone 2024, Merck). At 25 mm/s that is 6¼ small boxes; measure the shortest interval between two consecutive wide beats on the whole recording, not a typical one. Other markers named by Merck are younger age, exercise, earlier tachycardias and multiple pathways. Manifest WPW carries an estimated 1.5% yearly risk of AF and about 0.1% yearly risk of sudden death, and sudden death can be the first sign, especially in adolescents (Merck).

Why the drug history matters to the tracing: digoxin, adenosine, verapamil and diltiazem slow the AV node but not the pathway, may increase the ventricular rate in pre-excited AF and cause VF, and are contraindicated (Merck); Schiavone 2024 adds that amiodarone may enhance pathway conduction. A tech who sees an irregular, very fast, wide rhythm with changing QRS shapes, or who knows from an old tracing that the patient has a delta wave, says so when reporting, because that changes the provider's drug choice.

  1. Look at the patient: responsive, breathing, pulse?
  2. Alert the nurse or provider at once and say 'irregular wide-complex tachycardia, possible pre-excited AF'; mention a known WPW pattern or delta wave on an old ECG.
  3. Print and keep the strip; on a Holter or event recording, mark the episode and its shortest R-R.
  4. Stay with the patient. If the patient becomes unresponsive and pulseless, call the code team, start CPR and use the AED if trained.

Exam pearls

Exam tip PR of 7 small boxes (0.28 s), every P conducted: first-degree. Compare the PR just before and just after the dropped P: longer before is Mobitz I, the same is Mobitz II. One 2:1 strip: call it 2:1. Slow regular escape, faster P waves, PR changing at random: third-degree. RBBB: rSR′ in V1 and wide S in I and V6. LBBB: QS or rS in V1 and broad R with no q in I and V6. Left axis with qR in aVL and rS inferiorly: LAFB. Short PR with a delta wave: pre-excitation; a delta wave that vanishes in one beat as the treadmill rate climbs marks a lower-risk pathway. Fast, irregular, wide, changing shapes: pre-excited AF.
