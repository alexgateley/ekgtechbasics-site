Exam tip The classic errors: V1 and V2 too high (2nd or 3rd space) and V4 to V6 too low. High V1 and V2 can fake an rSr′ pattern, T-wave inversion or ST elevation. Getting V1 right makes the rest more likely to be right.

Try one

Electrode and Lead Placement practice question

In a hospital stress lab, a technician prepares a 62-year-old for a treadmill exercise stress test. For the resting 12-lead ECG done last month, the limb electrodes were on the wrists and ankles. Where should the four limb electrodes go for the treadmill test?

  1. Over the deltoids and over the outer hip muscles
  2. On the upper arms and on the fronts of the thighs
  3. Below the outer clavicles and on the torso above the iliac crests
  4. On the wrists and ankles, the standard sites at rest

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Electrodes over large working muscles pick up their electrical activity, so this placement adds muscle artifact during exercise.
  • B. Proximal limb sites move less than wrists and ankles at rest, but during walking or running the thighs and arms still swing, so artifact remains.
  • C. Correct. Moving the limb electrodes to the torso keeps them off the swinging arms and striding legs, which cuts motion artifact during exercise.
  • D. These are the standard resting sites, which makes them tempting, but the arms and legs move constantly on a treadmill and the tracing fills with motion artifact.

Exercise ECGs use torso placement of the limb electrodes, often called Mason-Likar placement: the arm electrodes go just below the outer ends of the clavicles and the leg electrodes on the lower torso above the iliac crests. This reduces motion artifact, but it shifts the QRS axis and ST levels slightly, so the resting baseline before exercise is recorded with the same placement and the lead system is noted on the tracing. The chest electrodes stay at their usual landmarks, and the skin is prepared carefully.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Finding the landmarks

Placement starts at the sternal angle (angle of Louis), the horizontal ridge where the manubrium meets the body of the sternum. The 2nd rib attaches beside it, so the space directly below that rib is the 2nd intercostal space. SCST's method is to slide the finger down and to the patient's right into the 2nd space, count down to the 4th, then slide toward the sternum until its edge is felt: the center of the V1 electrode goes there. Repeat the count on the left for V2. The rib spaces on the two sides can be offset, so V2 is not simply placed beside V1.

V4 goes next, in the 5th intercostal space in line with the midpoint of the clavicle, and V3 is then placed midway between V2 and V4. V5 (anterior axillary line) and V6 (midaxillary line) sit on a horizontal line drawn from V4. The ribs curve upward as they run toward the armpit, so following the 5th space laterally places V5 and V6 too high or too low; the horizontal line is the rule. Placing V4 before V3 avoids guessing V3's position. The center of the electrode's gel area, not its edge, is lined up with each landmark.

Position changes the tracing too. SCST recommends a semi-recumbent position of about 45° with the limbs supported; there is no evidence that angles between flat and 45° matter, but recordings with the patient upright or inclined 60° or more can differ, so any significant variation is written on the tracing.

Why misplacement matters

Misplaced chest electrodes are common. SCST cites studies showing V1 and V2 are often placed too high and V4 to V6 too low. In Gregory 2021, only 3 of 52 UK paramedics (5.8%) placed all six chest electrodes correctly on a young male model of medium build with easily found landmarks; V1 and V2 were usually too high (some in the 2nd intercostal space) and V4 tended to be too low and too far left. When V1 was right, the others were more likely to be right. That paper also summarizes Rajaganeshan 2008, in which V1 was placed correctly by 90% of cardiac technicians but by fewer than half of nurses and physicians.

The consequences are false findings. V1 and V2 placed high can produce an incomplete right bundle branch block pattern, anterior T-wave inversion, septal Q waves or ST elevation (Walsh 2018, cited by Gregory). Serial tracings recorded at different sites can show changes that are not in the heart, which is why ANMCO stresses using the same standard positions for every follow-up ECG. When a tracing shows an unexpected change, placement is checked; if the electrodes are wrong they are moved, the ECG is repeated and the nurse or provider is told which tracing is valid. A tracing that may need urgent attention is never held back while this is done.

Limb sites and the Mason-Likar torso layout

The 1975 AHA recommendation allowed the four limb electrodes anywhere on the arms and legs distal to the shoulders and hips, not only at the wrists and ankles (Tung 2021). Sites are not all equal, though: voltages differ along the limb, more along the left arm than the right because the left arm is closer to the heart (Pahlm 1992), and upper-arm sites alter limb-lead voltages and durations. SCST therefore keeps limb electrodes just above the wrists and ankles and moves them only for amputation or significant tremor. ANMCO advises placing limb electrodes over bone rather than muscle to reduce muscle noise.

Mason and Likar moved the limb electrodes to the torso so that arm and leg movement would not swamp the exercise ECG. As quoted by ISHNE-HRS and Tung, the arm electrodes sit in the infraclavicular fossae medial to the deltoid insertions and the left leg electrode midway between the costal margin and the iliac crest in the left anterior axillary line; Carluccio 2026 places the leg electrodes above the iliac crests, and some monitoring versions put the arm electrodes over the outer clavicles and the right leg (reference) electrode on the sternum. The chest electrodes stay at their standard sites.

For stress testing the layout is accepted because the comparison is within the same test: Morales-Arteaga 2026 advises a short resting tracing in the same layout before exercise, used consistently through the protocol, so that changes during exercise reflect the heart rather than the electrodes. ISHNE-HRS adds that ambulatory ECGs with torso limb sites should not be used interchangeably with standard ECGs for serial comparison. The Lund system, a different set of proximal limb electrode sites, matched the standard ECG's Q waves and axis better than Mason-Likar in 75 exercise patients (Pahlm and Wagner 2008, abstract). Whatever the system, the tracing is labeled with it.

Telemetry and Holter monitoring

Monitoring electrodes go on the chest wall, roughly equidistant from the heart, rather than on the limbs. A 3-electrode set (RA, LA, LL) displays the bipolar leads I, II and III; ANMCO prefers lead II because it runs parallel to the heart's main axis and shows clear P waves. A 5-electrode set adds RL and one chest electrode, so the monitor can also show one unipolar V lead, named for wherever the chest electrode sits (V1 to V6). ANMCO notes that P waves are often seen best in V1. Follow the diagram for the monitor in use: exact torso sites differ a little between makers and units.

Monitoring electrodes stay on for hours or days, so preparation matters more than for a resting ECG. ANMCO advises washing the skin first and placing electrodes over bone rather than muscle, where they move less. ISHNE-HRS reports incorrect placement in 26% of monitored patients in one study and cites a 46% fall in alarms from better skin preparation (fine abrasion) and daily electrode changes. For Holter recorders, ISHNE-HRS lists shaving if needed, light abrasion, electrodes from airtight packages and in date; ANMCO adds securing the electrodes and the cable so that movement does not tug on them. To identify what the rhythm on the screen is, see the Rhythm Gallery.

Some systems avoid intercostal counting altogether. EASI uses five electrodes (lower sternum, left and right midaxillary lines at the same level, the manubrium, and a reference anywhere on the torso) and derives a 12-lead ECG mathematically; it avoids the breast and the limbs. A derived or torso-recorded 12-lead is still not a standard 12-lead, so it is labeled as such (ISHNE-HRS).

Right-sided, posterior and 15-lead recordings

Right-sided leads look at the right ventricle. LITFL describes two methods: place all six chest electrodes in mirror image on the right chest (Morales-Arteaga notes that V1R then sits where V2 normally is, and V2R where V1 is), or, more simply, leave V1 and V2 in place and move V3 to V6 to the right as V3R to V6R. V4R, in the 5th right intercostal space at the midclavicular line, is the most useful single lead; LITFL quotes a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 78% for ST elevation in V4R in right ventricular infarction. That ST elevation is transient, lasting less than 10 hours in half of patients, which is why a provider who asks for V4R wants it now, not with the next routine ECG. Every moved channel is relabeled with its R name.

Posterior leads look at the back of the left ventricle, which the standard 12 leads see only in mirror image (ST depression and tall R waves in V1 to V3). V7 (left posterior axillary line), V8 (left midscapular line; LITFL says the tip of the scapula) and V9 (left paraspinal area, at the spinal border) all sit on the horizontal plane of V4 to V6. On a cart that cannot record 15 leads, SCST says to record the standard 12-lead first, then move V4, V5 and V6 to V7, V8 and V9 and record a second tracing, clearly annotated. ST changes in these leads are small (LITFL treats 0.5 mm of ST elevation as significant), so exact placement and labeling matter. Right posterior leads V7R to V9R use the same landmarks on the right.

A 15-lead ECG adds three of these leads to the standard 12. Morales-Arteaga 2026, citing the 2025 ACC/AHA acute coronary syndrome guideline, describes adding V4R with V8 and V9 in inferior STEMI, for little extra time. Other combinations are in use, so the technician follows the order or the unit's protocol and labels each moved lead.

Dextrocardia and arm-lead reversal

In dextrocardia the heart lies in the right chest. A standard tracing then shows negative P waves and QRS complexes in lead I and poor R-wave progression across V1 to V6. Reversed arm electrodes also invert lead I, but the chest leads progress normally, which is the way to tell them apart (LITFL). Check the arm wires first. If they are correct, SCST recommends a second tracing with V3 to V6 moved to the right chest at the same landmarks (V3R to V6R) and V1 and V2 left in place; the limb leads stay inverted, which shows the heart's position, and the right chest leads regain normal R-wave progression. Annotate the moved leads.

Some providers prefer the arm connections swapped as well, which makes the limb leads look normal. SCST calls very clear annotation imperative in that case, because a normal-looking tracing could let the dextrocardia be overlooked. An arm swap made by mistake on any other patient is a recording error: repeat the ECG with the wires correct, and if a wrong tracing has already been sent, tell the nurse or provider so it is not used.

Children and infants

SCST says the standard method for children is the same as for adults: limb electrodes just above the wrists and ankles and chest electrodes at the adult landmarks. A patient, gentle approach keeps the child still; record semi-reclined if possible, or seated if that prevents restlessness or distress. Extra leads such as V4R, V5R or V7 are recorded on a clinician's request or under local policy. It is common to record V4R and omit V3 in infants up to 1 year old: the V3 electrode goes at the V4R site, the mirror image of V4, and the channel is relabeled. Children's ECGs need a wider frequency range (high-frequency cutoff no lower than 250 Hz), so carts are set up for it.

Limb problems, breasts and other special cases

Amputation: SCST allows moving limb electrodes up the limb when a limb has been amputated. Place the electrode on the residual limb and the electrode on the opposite limb at the same level, because sites along a limb record different voltages. When both legs end at different heights, the shorter residual limb sets the level for both. If no limb site is usable, torso sites are used and labeled (Morales-Arteaga lists amputations, dressings and burns among the reasons for proximal sites). Write the sites on the tracing and reuse them for later ECGs, so a difference between tracings is more likely to be real.

Damaged skin: ANMCO says not to apply electrodes to skin with redness, lesions or wounds. Move to the nearest intact skin and note the change. SCST's standard site is anywhere on the forearm or lower leg above the wrist or ankle, so a short move that stays there needs nothing else; a move higher up the limb is matched on the opposite limb, as for an amputation. The right leg electrode is the neutral (ground) one: Paul and Jacob note that even swapping it with the left leg electrode leaves the tracing essentially unchanged, so when only the right leg is blocked it goes to the nearest free skin and the left leg electrode stays above the ankle. Hair is removed only where it stops the electrode sticking, preferably with clippers rather than a blade (ANMCO; SCST allows a single-use razor). Metal such as a watch or bracelet near an electrode disturbs the baseline and should be removed (ANMCO).

Breasts: by convention V4 to V6 go on the chest wall beneath the left breast when breast tissue covers the landmarks (SCST). Explain why, ask permission, and either let the patient lift the breast or lift it with the back of the hand to limit contact; offer a chaperone and cover the chest once the cables are on. Macfarlane 2003 (abstract) found less variability with electrodes at the true anatomical positions over the breast, and SCST notes evidence that over-breast placement may not reduce the signal, but neither changes the convention yet. Breast implants make placement harder and may alter the ECG; SCST cites a small study (Bun 2019) in which 42–46% of ECGs from women with implants and normal hearts were judged abnormal. In ambulatory patients, ANMCO notes that large breasts or prostheses can tug on electrodes, so choose sites close to bone.

Tremor and tension: muscle activity adds fine, irregular noise. Warm the room and settle the patient first. SCST suggests asking the patient to tuck the hands behind the legs (ANMCO: under the buttocks). Limb electrodes are moved up the limbs only for a significant tremor that persists. The muscle filter (0.67–40 Hz) is a last resort because it also distorts the ECG; SCST records the first tracing with the filter off and labels any tracing made with it on.

Seated and wheelchair recordings: for a patient who cannot lie back because of breathlessness, pain or frailty, Morales-Arteaga describes a seated ECG with V1 to V6 at their usual sites and limb electrodes at the wrists and ankles or, if needed, symmetric proximal sites. SCST allows recording in a wheelchair. In both, write the position on the tracing, because posture can change rate, intervals and T waves.

Pacemakers and ICDs: electrodes go at the standard sites. ANMCO warns that filters can hide pacing spikes; keep the filter off and note the device on the tracing so the reader expects paced complexes. For what paced rhythms look like, see the Rhythm Gallery.

What the record must show

SCST lists what every stored ECG should carry: the patient's full name, date of birth and a unique number, the referrer, the person recording, the date and time, the institution, and any alteration from standard lead positions, in both the printed and the electronically stored copy. Jowett 2005 and Sevilla 1989 both conclude that a tracing with torso limb electrodes must say so on its face ('torso-positioned limb leads' or 'nonstandard'), so that a reader does not take a change caused by the electrodes for disease. The same applies to right-sided and posterior leads recorded on standard channels: if V4 to V6 now sit on the back, the printout still says V4 to V6 unless the technician relabels it.

Changes that are not placement are written down too: a seated or upright position, a filter or gain setting other than standard, poor quality from tremor or movement, and any symptoms during the recording. Findings that may need urgent attention go to the nurse or provider at once (SCST).
