The pacemaker code (NBG)

LetterMeansChoices
1stChamber pacedA atrium, V ventricle, D both
2ndChamber sensedA, V, D, O
3rdResponse to a sensed beatI inhibit, T trigger, D both, O none
4thRate responseR raises the rate with activity, O none
5thMultisite pacingFor example both ventricles; O none

VVIR: paces and senses the ventricle, holds back when it senses a beat, speeds up with activity. DDD is the most common mode. AOO, VOO and DOO pace at a fixed rate and sense nothing (asynchronous).

Try one

Pacemakers and Implanted Devices on the ECG practice question

On a cardiac surgery step-down unit, a monitor technician reviews a lead II strip from a 71-year-old patient on day 2 after valve surgery, whose temporary epicardial pacemaker is set to VVI at 60/min. The patient's own narrow beats run at about 82/min. At 25 mm/s the pacing spikes are 25 small boxes apart all the way through the strip. Spikes that land on a native QRS, ST segment or T wave produce no new complex, and the one spike that falls late after a native beat is followed by a wide QRS. Which classification BEST fits the strip for the report to the nurse?

  1. Normal demand pacing at the lower rate
  2. Failure to capture
  3. Undersensing of the native beats
  4. Oversensing of the T waves

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A VVI pacemaker set at 60/min should be inhibited when the patient's own rhythm runs at 82/min; spikes firing on a steady schedule through that rhythm are not normal demand behavior.
  • B. Tempting because several spikes have no QRS after them, but those spikes fall while the ventricle is still refractory from a native beat, and the one spike that arrives after recovery does capture, so the output is working.
  • C. Correct. Spikes 25 small boxes (1.0 s) apart fire at the set rate of 60/min with no regard for native beats at 82/min, so the device is not sensing them; spikes can then fall on T waves.
  • D. Oversensing makes the pacemaker see signals that are not there and withhold output, so it causes missing spikes and pauses, the opposite of the extra spikes on this strip.

At 25 mm/s, 25 small boxes is 1.0 s, so the spikes fire at exactly the programmed 60/min while the patient's own rhythm runs faster. A demand pacemaker that sensed those beats would stay silent, so spikes marching through the native rhythm mean undersensing (failure to sense). Spikes without a QRS here do not prove failure to capture, because they fall in the refractory period after native beats, and the late spike that produces a wide paced QRS shows the output still captures. The finding is reported to the nurse promptly, because an undersensed spike that lands on the T wave can trigger ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation.

Sources: CanadiEM: Interpreting a Pacemaker ECG (2016)

Safety A spike on a T wave (R on T) can trigger VT, torsades or VF: report it promptly even if the patient feels fine. Pauses, lost capture or dizziness: stay with the patient and tell the nurse now. Unresponsive and not breathing normally: call the code team and start CPR.

Magnets and ICDs

DeviceMagnet on (stops when it is lifted)
Most pacemakersFixed-rate pacing at the maker's magnet rate; spikes march through the patient's own beats
Transvenous ICDShocks and antitachycardia pacing suspended; no fixed-rate pacing
Micra leadless pacemakerNo magnet response
  • Placing a magnet is the provider's decision; record and report what the strip shows.
  • An ICD also paces, so its spikes look like a pacemaker's. Antitachycardia pacing is a short burst of fast paced beats that can stop VT without a shock.
  • Shock, patient awake: stay, tell the nurse, save the strip and the time. Several shocks or symptoms: urgent.
  • Shock, patient unresponsive: code team, CPR, AED with the pad off the device. Touching a patient during an ICD shock is not harmful.

Filters, monitors and look-alikes

  • Record first at the diagnostic setting (to 150 Hz); a 40 Hz filter can blunt, hide or distort spikes. Label any filter.
  • Many monitors find spikes only with their pacer setting on and then draw their own marker; with it off, paced beats can alarm as a ventricular rhythm.
  • Fake spikes: static from clothing, loose or broken wires, nerve stimulators (fixed-rate spikes unrelated to the P or QRS).

Exam pearls

Exam tip Spike, no QRS: failure to capture. Spikes among the patient's own beats: undersensing. Pause with no spike: oversensing or failure to pace. Two spikes per beat: dual-chamber. Wide LBBB-like QRS after each spike: RV pacing. No spikes with a normal rhythm: demand pacing, not failure. Magnet: fixed-rate pacing, expected. Paced 'LBBB' on the computer read: note the device.

Spikes: why they are small, missing or fake

A pacing stimulus is very brief, so on paper it is a thin vertical line, usually about 2 ms wide (LITFL). Its height depends on where the lead sits and what kind of lead it is: bipolar leads make much smaller spikes than unipolar leads, and epicardial leads make smaller spikes than leads inside the heart. A spike that is obvious in one lead may be invisible in the next, which is why CanadiEM advises checking every lead before deciding a beat is not paced.

Machines have to work to see them. In one study of electrocardiographs, pacer spike detection from the ordinary 500-samples-per-second data stream found 77.5% of spikes, while a detector sampling at 32,000 per second found 99.5% (Luo 2008). The same study notes that a slow data stream can show only one spike when two ventricular leads fire close together, as in biventricular pacing. Stored ECGs are usually compressed, and SCST warns that compression changes short, sharp signals such as pacemaker spikes more than it changes the ST segment, so an archived copy may not match the original.

Many bedside monitors use a separate pacer detection mode with a higher sampling rate. In Drew's ICU study, when it detected a spike the monitor painted its own marker on the display, so the spike you see may be the monitor's mark, not the real signal (Drew 2014). In that study only one third of patients with pacemakers had the mode switched on, and intermittent ventricular pacing without it produced false 'accelerated ventricular rhythm' alarms. The technician confirms the pacemaker in the chart and asks the nurse to turn the setting on, or turns it on where unit policy allows.

Filter or settingEffect on spikesWhat to do
Diagnostic band, 0.05 or 0.67 to 150 Hz (SCST)Keeps short, sharp signalsUse it for the first recording

Analog-type monitorsDrop expected spikes more often than diagnostic ECGs (Mondal 2026)Confirm on a 12-lead or paper strip before calling a malfunction

Things that make spike-like marks: static electricity, for example from clothing (Luo's test set included spikes from clothing static and a static gun); fractured wires and loose connectors; and other implanted stimulators. Mondal describes neurostimulators (deep brain, sacral nerve and gastric) sending periodic pulses, and deep brain stimulators causing high-frequency artifact, usually 60 to 200 Hz. A stimulator's spikes run at their own fixed rate with no link to the P waves or QRS complexes, and re-prepping the skin does not remove them; ask about implanted devices and tell the nurse or provider. Mondal's rule of thumb: spikes that occur at predictable intervals in relation to atrial or ventricular events are almost always real pacing. Pseudopacing artifacts that no pacing algorithm can explain also occur, and artifact can simulate pacemaker malfunction (ISHNE-HRS), so read every suspected malfunction in context before it is reported as one.

Modes and how a dual-chamber pacemaker behaves

The code follows the NASPE/BPEG (NBG) system, revised in 2002; LITFL notes that many textbooks still print the 1987 version, in which the fourth letter also covered programmability and the fifth covered antitachycardia functions. Positions I to III are the same in both. Merck's table gives position V as O, A, V or D, where multisite pacing means both atria, both ventricles or more than one lead in one chamber; a code can have three to five letters.

ModeWhat it doesWhat the strip shows
AAIPaces and senses the atrium; holds back for the patient's own P waveAtrial spikes only when the sinus rate falls below the set rate
VVI(R)Paces and senses the ventricle; one of the two most used modesVentricular spikes only when the patient's own QRS is late
DDD(R)Senses and paces both chambers; the most common modeNo spikes, atrial spike only, ventricular spike only, or both
AOO, VOO, DOOPaces at a fixed rate and senses nothing; what most pacemakers do under a magnetSpikes march on regardless of the patient's own beats

A DDD pacemaker watches the atrium first. If no P wave comes by the time the lower rate is due, it paces the atrium; then it waits a set delay for the patient's own QRS and paces the ventricle only if none comes (Fernando 2026). That is why LITFL says dual-chamber spikes may precede only the P wave, only the QRS, or both. A P wave with a ventricular spike after it means the device is following the patient's own atrial rate; two spikes per beat means both chambers are paced; no spikes means the patient's own rhythm is in charge.

Atrial pacing can unmask a slow AV node. LITFL shows atrially paced patients with first-degree block or Wenckebach conduction that their slower native rhythm did not show, and calls it clinically unimportant if cardiac output holds. Each atrial spike followed by a P wave proves atrial capture; the dropped beats are still reported to the nurse.

Rate response (R) uses a sensor to speed up pacing with activity. Merck notes that such devices can also respond to vibration and muscle activity, so a bumpy car ride can raise the paced rate; a diary entry beside the faster pacing helps the device clinic. Mode switching protects dual-chamber patients from fast atrial rhythms: in atrial fibrillation, for example, a DDDR device can switch itself to VVIR and pace the ventricle at a programmed rate instead of following the atria. The device is working, but new AF is still a rhythm change to report.

Reading a paced strip step by step

  1. Read the strip as usual, then add four checks (CanadiEM): rate, capture, pacing, sensing.
  2. Rate: measure spike to spike. At 25 mm/s, 1500 divided by the small boxes between spikes is the paced rate (25 small boxes = 60/min). Compare it with the programmed rate in the chart or on the device card.
  3. Capture: is every spike followed by its P wave (atrial) or QRS (ventricular)? A spike that lands just after the patient's own beat finds the heart refractory, so no complex follows; that alone does not prove failure to capture. Look for spikes that fall where the heart could respond.
  4. Pacing: are spikes there whenever the patient's own rate drops below the programmed rate? A pause longer than the pacing interval with no spike is failure to pace, from oversensing or a hardware problem (CanadiEM).
  5. Sensing: do spikes appear when the patient's own beats are already on time? That is undersensing.
  6. Record the device, the findings and the time, and report as below. Leave interrogation, reprogramming and treatment to the nurse, provider and device team.

Fusion and pseudofusion are normal behaviour, not malfunction. When the patient's own rate is close to the programmed rate, the device's timer can run out just as a native beat begins. If both wavefronts share the ventricle, the QRS is a hybrid of the paced and native shapes, often narrower than a fully paced beat (fusion, LITFL and CanadiEM). If the native beat already owns the ventricle, the spike sits on a QRS that it did not change (pseudofusion). Check the QRS shape and timing before calling either one a problem.

FindingHow fast to report
Failure to capture or to pace, pauses, or any finding with dizziness, weakness or faintingNow: stay with the patient and tell the nurse
Undersensing, especially a spike on a T wavePromptly, even without symptoms
Paced rate faster or slower than programmed; new AF in a paced patientPromptly, with the strip
Normal paced rhythm, fusion, or no spikes with a normal native rhythmDocument the device; routine

Malfunctions in more depth

Capture means the stimulus is strong enough to depolarize the heart. CanadiEM groups the causes of failure to capture as hardware (a broken lead or one that has lost contact, battery problems), exit block (a rise in the stimulation threshold where the lead meets the heart), and patient factors (electrolyte problems, drugs, myocardial infarction). Each uncaptured spike is a lost beat, so the true heart rate falls below the programmed rate, which explains weakness or lightheadedness.

Sensing is set by a sensitivity number: a lower number makes the device see smaller signals. Undersensing means the device misses the patient's own beats and paces when it should not, so spikes appear anywhere, from a lead fracture, poor contact, an insulation problem or low-voltage native signals. A spike near the peak of the T wave can cause an R-on-T event, which CanadiEM links to VT or torsades.

Oversensing means the device mistakes something else for a heartbeat and holds back its output: T waves, signals from the wrong chamber, muscle potentials (myopotentials), or electromagnetic interference. The strip shows pauses with no spikes, for example during arm work with muscle noise on the baseline, which separates it from failure to capture (spikes with nothing after them). ISHNE-HRS lists myopotential inhibition, cross-talk and pacemaker-mediated tachycardia as the main abnormalities seen on Holter recordings in pacemaker patients, though they are uncommon, and notes that in one report pacemaker dysfunction explained syncope in only 4.9% of pacemaker patients.

ProblemMechanism (Merck, CanadiEM)On the strip
Cross-talkThe ventricular channel senses the atrial pacing pulse and withholds ventricular pacingAtrial spike and P wave, then no ventricular spike and a pause
Pacemaker-mediated tachycardia (PMT)A PVC or paced beat conducts back to the atria; the atrial lead senses that P wave and the device paces the ventricle again, over and overSudden ventricular-paced tachycardia at or near the upper rate, often after a PVC, with a retrograde P after each QRS
Sensor-driven tachycardiaThe rate sensor responds to vibration or muscle activityPaced rate rises without exertion; match it to the diary
Pacemaker syndromeVentricular pacing that loses the normal timing between atria and ventriclesPaced rhythm that looks normal; the patient has vague dizziness, neck or breathing symptoms. Report them.

PMT needs an atrial lead to sense the retrograde P wave, so it occurs only with dual-chamber devices; the device team can stop and prevent it, so the technician reports it promptly. A patient whose own heart produces little or no escape rhythm is called pacing dependent; pauses from oversensing or cross-talk matter most in these patients.

Biventricular pacing (CRT)

Cardiac resynchronization therapy adds a lead that paces the left ventricle, usually from a coronary vein, so both ventricles are paced together. Pacing from the right ventricular apex alone gives a wide QRS that is negative in V1 (recorded at the 4th intercostal space) with, usually, a left superior axis. During biventricular pacing the QRS most often has a dominant R wave in V1 and V2, and the frontal axis most often points to the right superior quadrant, though other axes do not by themselves mean a problem (van Stipdonk 2015).

A negative QRS in V1 during CRT warrants evaluation. Causes include a V1 electrode placed in the wrong space, slow conduction away from the left ventricular lead (which leaves a picture dominated by right ventricular pacing) and a dislodged left ventricular lead. The technician's first job is to confirm that V1 sits in the 4th intercostal space at the right sternal edge, then to report a change in the paced QRS shape compared with earlier tracings.

CRT helps only when it paces nearly every beat. ISHNE-HRS says the benefit depends mostly on effective biventricular pacing, and that programming should aim for the maximum, above 98%. The 2023 HRS/EHRA/APHRS/LAHRS remote device clinic consensus notes that studies have used cut-offs from above 80% to above 98% and names above 95% as the target. PVCs, AF and fusion or pseudofusion beats all lower the true share. In one small study of CRT patients in AF whose devices counted over 90% biventricular pacing, a 12-lead Holter found fusion or pseudofusion beats in 53% of the patients, so a high device count can overstate real pacing. A high PVC or AF burden on a Holter is a finding the device clinic needs.

Magnet response in more depth

Most pacemakers switch to asynchronous pacing (AOO, VOO or DOO, depending on the leads) while a magnet is over them, at a rate set by the maker. Sensing is off, so spikes compete with the patient's own beats, and spikes that land just after a native beat find the ventricle refractory; that is expected while the magnet is on. LITFL notes that asynchronous ventricular pacing carries a risk of pacemaker-induced VT, so a spike on a T wave is still reported. The magnet rate is lower once the battery reaches its elective replacement point (ERI), by about 20% (Fernando 2026).

Exceptions exist, and magnet mode can be programmed off. Biotronik devices respond for 8 hours unless the magnet is lifted and replaced, and in their default auto mode give 10 asynchronous beats and then return to normal settings.

  • Removing the magnet restores the device's programmed behaviour.
  • Magnets and MRI are outside the technician's decisions: if a patient with a device is booked for MRI, or a magnet is being used, record what the strip shows and report it.

ICDs: pacing, antitachycardia pacing and shocks

A transvenous ICD has leads in the right ventricle and sometimes the right atrium (a CRT-D adds a left ventricular lead). It cardioverts or defibrillates VT and VF, and tiered-therapy ICDs also pace for slow rhythms and use antitachycardia pacing (ATP) on tachycardias that respond to it; they store intracardiac electrograms of each event (Merck). Its bradycardia pacing looks the same on the ECG as a pacemaker's.

ATP delivers a burst of stimuli slightly faster than the tachycardia; in Okubo's cases the burst interval was 88% of the VT cycle length. On a monitor or Holter it appears as a short run of rapid paced beats interrupting the VT, followed by the return of the patient's rhythm if it worked. It is painless, but it fails more often in fast VT (success as low as 50% when the cycle length is under 300 ms), and it can accelerate VT in up to 10% of therapies, after which the device may shock.

Merck lists the causes of inappropriate shocks or pacing: sinus rhythm, supraventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation or flutter, and nonphysiologic signals such as those from a fractured lead. Missed therapy can come from lead or generator migration, undersensing, a rise in the pacing threshold or a depleted battery. A recording made at the time of a shock helps show which it was, so save the strip, mark the time and note what the patient was doing.

After a shockWhat happens next (NHLBI, Merck)
One shock, patient feels well afterwardProvider or device clinic check that day or the next
Several shocks in a short time, or chest pain, shortness of breath, faintingUrgent: tell the nurse or provider at once; outside a hospital, the emergency department
Patient unresponsive and not breathing normallyCode team, CPR and the AED as trained

NHLBI tells patients that a high-energy shock lasts under a second and can be strong or painful, and that a partner touching them usually feels nothing or a tingle that is not harmful. CPR is not delayed for fear of an ICD shock. A patient wearing a defibrillator vest is different: it sounds a warning before it shocks, and anyone touching the patient moves away as soon as it warns.

Around the device: the technician's job

  • Before recording, ask about a pacemaker, ICD or other implanted stimulator; look for the scar and bulge below a collarbone.
  • Use the diagnostic filter settings for the first recording, and label any filter you add.
  • Note the device on the tracing or requisition and tell the reader. If you learn of it after the tracing is filed, add a dated addendum; never change the original entry.
  • On telemetry, confirm the monitor's pacer detection is on for every paced patient.
  • Spikes that do not fit any pacing pattern: think artifact or a nerve stimulator before malfunction, but report anything you cannot explain.
  • AED: do not put a pad over the device. Singapore 2026 advises about four finger breadths away, and notes devices implanted under the right collarbone, where the upper pad usually goes.
  • Report, do not decide: magnets, MRI, interrogation and reprogramming belong to the nurse, provider and device team.
Exam tip The NHA CET plan asks you to recognize pacemaker spikes and the meaning of atrial-paced, ventricular-paced and dual-chamber-paced tracings; the CCI CRAT outline asks you to identify paced rhythms. Expect strips, not device programming.
