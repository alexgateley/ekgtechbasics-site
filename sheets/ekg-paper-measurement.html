Exam tip The calibration pulse is the ruler for height. Read its height before any amplitude: a 10 mm R wave is 1 mV at standard gain, 2 mV at half standard and 0.5 mV at double standard. Paper speed never changes height, and gain never changes width.

Try one

Paper, Settings and Measurement practice question

In a stress lab, a technician prints a resting 12-lead and then sees that the header reads 50 mm/s and 5 mm/mV, settings left by the previous user. Compared with a tracing of the same patient at standard settings, how do the complexes on this printout look?

  1. Twice as wide and twice as tall
  2. Twice as wide and half as tall
  3. Half as wide and half as tall
  4. Half as wide and twice as tall

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The speed effect is right, but reading 5 mm/mV as a higher sensitivity reverses the gain: fewer millimeters per millivolt means shorter waves, not taller ones.
  • B. Correct. Double paper speed spreads each wave over twice the distance, and half the standard gain prints each millivolt as 5 mm instead of 10 mm.
  • C. The gain effect is right, but faster paper stretches the waves; narrower waves would come from a slower speed such as 12.5 mm/s.
  • D. This reverses both settings and describes a slower paper speed with a higher gain, the opposite of what the header shows.

Paper speed sets the horizontal scale and gain sets the vertical scale, and each works on its own. At the standard 25 mm/s a small box is 0.04 s; at 50 mm/s the same interval covers twice as many millimeters, so PR, QRS and R-R distances all look twice as long, and a rate counted with the standard box rules comes out at half the true rate. At the standard 10 mm/mV a 1 mV signal prints 10 mm tall; at 5 mm/mV it prints 5 mm, so every wave looks half its usual height and normal voltage can be misread as low. The technician resets both settings to standard, repeats the tracing and confirms a 10 mm calibration mark before passing it on.

Sources: LITFL ECG Library; Green, 12-Lead EKG Confidence, 3rd ed.

Measuring the QT and QTc

  1. Use lead II or V5–V6, whichever shows the longest QT; measure several beats.
  2. End of T: draw a line down the steepest part of the T downslope; where it meets the baseline is the end.
  3. Leave out a U wave that is separate from the T; if T and U merge, use the tangent.
  4. Bazett: QTc = QT ÷ √RR (seconds). Adequate at 60–100/min; it overcorrects above 100 and undercorrects below 60.
  5. Outside 60–100/min, Fridericia (QT ÷ ∛RR) or Framingham (QT + 0.154 × (1 − RR)) is preferred; Hodges (QT + 0.00175 × (HR − 60)) is another linear option.
Exam tip Worked example at 120/min (RR 0.50 s), QT 0.32 s: Bazett 453 ms looks long, but Fridericia gives 403 ms and Framingham 397 ms. A long Bazett QTc at a fast rate may be the formula, not the patient. Report the QT, the rate and the formula used.

U waves

A small wave after the T, same direction as the T, usually about 0.5 mm and under a quarter of the T's height, best seen in V2–V3 and at slow rates. Prominent U waves (over 1–2 mm): bradycardia and low potassium most often; also low magnesium or calcium, and drugs such as digoxin, sotalol and amiodarone. An inverted U wave where the T is upright is abnormal: report it.

Why the grid works

ECG paper is a graph. At the standard speed of 25 mm per second, each 1 mm small box is 0.04 s and each 5 mm large box is 0.20 s, so 5 large boxes make 1 second, 300 large boxes make 1 minute, and 1,500 small boxes make 1 minute. That is where the 300 and 1,500 rate constants come from: 300 ÷ large boxes between R waves, or 1,500 ÷ small boxes, gives beats per minute. At the standard gain of 10 mm per millivolt, each small box is 0.1 mV and each large box 0.5 mV. Modern carts print and save PDFs on the same scale.

Electronic calipers report milliseconds. Converting is division: 1,250 ms ÷ 40 ms per small box = 31.25 small boxes, and 1,500 ÷ 31.25 = 48/min; or 60,000 ÷ 1,250 ms = 48/min directly.

At 50 mm/s the paper moves twice as fast, so every wave is twice as wide: a small box is 0.02 s and a large box 0.10 s, and a 10-second paper width holds only 5 seconds. The 300 and 1,500 shortcuts then give half the real rate unless they are doubled to 600 and 3,000. A provider may ask for 50 mm/s to separate closely spaced waves, for example to look for flutter waves or P waves in a fast rhythm. It is not used routinely, because the stretched low-amplitude waves, including U waves, are harder to measure. Height is unchanged by speed: a 2 mm ST deviation is 0.2 mV at either speed if the gain is standard. (At 10 mm/s a small box is 0.1 s and a large box 0.5 s.)

Gain and the calibration pulse in practice

Most tracings carry a rectangular calibration pulse of 1 mV, 0.2 s wide, usually at the left edge of the tracing or of each row. Its height is the gain actually used: 10 mm at the standard 10 mm/mV, 5 mm at half standard (5 mm/mV) and 20 mm at double standard (20 mm/mV). Half gain fits very tall complexes on the page; double gain enlarges very small ones. Either way, every amplitude on that tracing is read against the pulse, not the grid alone. Voltage criteria such as low QRS voltage (all limb-lead QRS under 5 mm, or all precordial QRS under 10 mm) and the voltage criteria for ventricular hypertrophy assume standard gain.

  • Before recording: check the settings screen for 25 mm/s, 10 mm/mV and the diagnostic filter. Settings left by the previous user are a common cause of a misleading tracing.
  • After recording: confirm the pulse height matches the gain in the header. A cart that prints a 7 mm pulse at a displayed 10 mm/mV is out of calibration: take it out of use per policy, record on another cart and report it to biomedical engineering.
  • Comparing tracings: if today's complexes are half the size of yesterday's, check both pulses before calling it a change in the patient.
  • Scanned or faxed copies: if the calibration pulse, speed and gain were cropped off, the reader cannot trust any amplitude; rescan or reprint the full page.
  • Mark any non-standard speed or gain clearly so the reader does not measure it as standard.

Filters: what they remove and what they distort

The skin and electrodes produce a steady offset of up to about 200 mV, far larger than the 3–4 mV heart signal, so every ECG amplifier needs a high-pass (low-frequency cutoff) filter. The question is where to set it. Breathing moves the baseline slowly, at about 0.25 Hz for 15 breaths per minute, and a 0.5 Hz cutoff steadies the baseline. But in a simple real-time filter, a 0.5 Hz cutoff shifts the timing of the low-frequency parts of the ECG, and the error lands where the QRS meets the ST segment. In Buendía-Fuentes 2012 (45 patients), real-time recording at 0.5 Hz produced clinically significant ST shifts in 93%: false elevation in leads with a dominant S wave (V1–V4, inferior leads) and false depression in leads with a dominant R wave (I, aVL, V4–V6), with the J point moved 1.5 to 9 mm (average 3 mm). Wide QRS complexes, such as left bundle branch block and paced beats, were affected most. At 0.05 Hz there was no distortion.

AHA recommendations since 1990 therefore keep 0.05 Hz for traditional filters and allow up to 0.67 Hz only for digital filters that do not shift timing (bidirectional, or linear-phase, filters). Those filters work on stored data, so the computer-processed 12-lead printed a few seconds later can be clean while a real-time printout or monitor at 0.5 Hz is not. That is why ST levels on monitors, telemetry and real-time strips are not used to diagnose ischemia.

The low-pass (high-frequency cutoff) filter removes fast noise: muscle tremor and 50–60 Hz AC interference. Diagnostic adult tracings need at least 150 Hz, and children 250 Hz. A 40 Hz 'muscle' or monitoring filter lowers QRS amplitude and smooths notches and sharp deflections; in one 2026 study, heavy filtering (0.05–25 Hz) cut the QRS notches and slurs seen by 22% and turned about half the notches into slurs, enough for readers to miss a true left bundle branch block pattern. Cutoffs below 150 Hz can also lose pacemaker spikes, so a paced rhythm can be missed. A 40 Hz filter is sometimes used on purpose, for example to clear the interference a left ventricular assist device pump causes, but then only for rhythm, not for detailed QRS or ST-T review.

Heart rate methods in detail

  • 300 method: count large boxes between two R waves and divide 300. The sequence 300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50 is 300 ÷ 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. An R wave that falls between lines makes this an estimate: 3.6 large boxes is 83/min, not 75 or 100.
  • 1,500 method: count small boxes and divide 1,500. More precise, and the method of choice for fast regular rhythms, where one box changes the answer by a lot (10 small boxes is 150/min, 9 is 167/min).
  • 6-second method: count QRS complexes in 6 seconds (30 large boxes) and multiply by 10, or count a full 10-second strip and multiply by 6. It gives the average rate and is the only one of the three that is valid when the rhythm is irregular.
  • Slow rhythms: the count methods are coarse (one complex more or less changes the answer by 6 or 10), so count the whole 10 seconds or measure R to R if the rhythm is regular.
  • Two rates: when P waves and QRS complexes are not linked one to one, calculate the atrial rate from P to P and the ventricular rate from R to R and report both.
  • Machine rate: usually right, but check it against the strip when T waves are tall, the QRS is small, or there is artifact.

Measuring PR, QRS, ST and QT

MeasureLandmarksPitfalls
PR intervalStart of the P wave to the start of the QRS (Q or R, whichever comes first)Under 0.12 s: possible pre-excitation or junctional origin. Over 0.20 s: first-degree AV block. A slurred QRS start (delta wave) with a short PR needs reporting
QRS durationFirst deflection of the QRS to the J point (where the QRS ends and the ST begins)0.10–0.12 s is abnormal but below bundle branch block width; 0.12 s or more: bundle branch block, pre-excitation, paced or ventricular beats, high potassium, sodium-channel drugs
ST levelAt the J point, against the baseline set by the PR (or TP) segmentOnly on a diagnostic-filter tracing at standard gain
QT intervalStart of the QRS to the end of the TFlat T tail, merged U wave, P wave on the T at fast rates, irregular R-R
  • Lead choice for the QT: lead II or V5–V6, using whichever is longest. Bazett used lead II, and the U wave is usually smallest there; mid-chest leads can overestimate. Use the same lead each time when the QT is being trended.
  • Several beats: QT varies by more than 20 ms within one 10-second tracing, and it is longer during sleep.
  • Irregular rhythms such as atrial fibrillation or marked sinus arrhythmia: measure several beats and average them.
  • Premature beats: do not measure the beat right after a premature beat, because its repolarization is altered.
  • Flat or long T tail: use the tangent method; following the tail to the baseline overmeasures the QT.
  • U waves and the QT: a small U wave, or one separate from the T, is always left out. Sources differ on a large U wave fused to the T: LITFL includes a fused U wave over 1 mm in the QT, while Lester 2019 and the Brazilian guideline draw the tangent down the T downslope, which ends the measurement before the U. Follow the lab's rule and say which was used. In low potassium, the Brazilian guideline suggests measuring in aVL, where the U wave tends to be flattest.
  • Wide QRS: a bundle branch block or paced rhythm lengthens the QT without a true repolarization problem. Correction methods exist (for example subtracting the extra QRS width); record the QRS width beside the QT so the reader can judge.
  • Quick screen: at normal rates a QT under half the preceding R-R is normal; over half means calculate the QTc.

QTc formulas compared

The QT shortens as the rate rises, so it is corrected to what it would be at 60/min (RR 1.0 s). Every formula agrees at exactly 60/min, where QTc equals QT. RR = 60 ÷ heart rate, in seconds.

Arithmetic examples. At the fast rate Bazett gives the longest value (overcorrection); at the slow rate it gives the shortest (undercorrection).
FormulaEquation (seconds)At 120/min, QT 0.32 sAt 50/min, QT 0.46 s
Bazett (1920)QT ÷ √RR453 ms420 ms
Fridericia (1920)QT ÷ ∛RR403 ms433 ms
FraminghamQT + 0.154 × (1 − RR)397 ms429 ms
HodgesQT + 0.00175 × (HR − 60)425 ms443 ms

Bazett is built into most carts because it is simple, and it is adequate between 60 and 100/min (Brazilian guidance puts the upper limit at 90). Outside that range Fridericia or Framingham is recommended, and the FDA no longer routinely asks for Bazett values in drug trials because Fridericia performs better. In one comparison, Bazett detected 54% of prolonged QT intervals and Fridericia 100%. No formula removes the effect of rate entirely, so a QTc from a tachycardic patient should be read with the rate beside it, and repeated when the rate settles if the question matters.

All agree that over 500 ms carries a high risk of torsades de pointes. A short QTc is under about 340–350 ms.

Children

A child's ECG is read with the same steps but different numbers. Heart rate is higher and falls with age; LITFL lists 60–100/min from age 6, and the Brazilian guideline says rates reach adult values around age 12. The PR is shorter and the QRS narrower than an adult's, and both lengthen with age, so a QRS that is normal for an adult can be wide for an infant. Use the age-specific table on the cart printout or in the lab's reference, not adult cutoffs.

  • Pre-excitation in a child: a short PR for age with a delta wave; the QRS may be normal or wide for age. The signs can be subtle, sometimes seen only in V3–V4, and can come and go in infants and children.
  • QT in children: measure in II, V5 and V6 and use the longest. At fast rates the next P wave can sit on the T and hide its end. Children often have large U waves: leave out a separate U, and use the tangent when the U merges with the T or is more than half its height. Average several beats in marked sinus arrhythmia.
  • QTc in children: up to 440 ms is normal, 440–460 ms borderline, over 460 ms prolonged. Bazett can overcorrect at high pediatric heart rates, although one study in children under 2 found it the most consistent formula; borderline values are confirmed on repeat tracings.
  • Filters: the diagnostic band reaches 250 Hz for children.
  • Computer interpretation is less accurate in children; the tracing is read by the provider.
  • T waves negative in V1–V4 are normal until about age 12.
