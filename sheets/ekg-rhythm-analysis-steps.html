A fixed order for every strip, regularity, narrow vs wide fast rhythms, early vs escape beats, describing what you cannot name, and what to report at once

Patient and tracing first

Look at the patient, then check the tracing: right lead, 25 mm/s, standard gain, clean baseline. Read a 10-second lead II strip and confirm in other leads. For individual rhythms, see the Rhythm Gallery.

Safety A rhythm that does not fit the patient is a tracing problem until shown otherwise: a patient talking to you is not in VF. But an unresponsive patient who is not breathing normally needs help now: call for help or the code team, start CPR and use the AED if you are trained.

Try one

Analyzing a Rhythm Strip Step by Step practice question

On a nephrology unit, a 66-year-old with kidney failure has two 12-lead EKGs 30 minutes apart while the team waits for lab results. The first shows tall, peaked T waves with a QRS of 0.09 seconds. Which change on the second tracing should the technician report to the provider as MOST urgent?

  1. QRS widened to 0.16 seconds, and the P waves barely visible
  2. Peaked T waves now in the limb leads as well as in V2 to V4
  3. Heart rate down from 92 to 84, with the PR interval unchanged
  4. T waves now 1 mm taller in V2 to V4 than on the first tracing

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Loss of P waves and a QRS that has nearly doubled in width mean the potassium effect has moved past the T waves to slowed conduction, the stage before a sine-wave pattern.
  • B. Spread of peaked T waves to more leads is worth noting, but it is still the first stage of the sequence, while a widening QRS is a later, more dangerous one.
  • C. Slow rhythms do occur with high potassium, but a rate of 84 is normal, and an unchanged PR interval shows no new conduction delay.
  • D. Peaked T waves are the hallmark of high potassium, which makes this tempting, but a small rise in their height is an early-stage change, less urgent than conduction slowing.

Hyperkalemia changes the tracing in a rough sequence: peaked T waves first, then flattening and loss of P waves with PR prolongation, then QRS widening and bizarre complexes, and finally a sine-wave pattern that can degenerate into ventricular fibrillation, asystole or a pulseless wide-complex rhythm. A QRS that has widened from 0.09 to 0.16 seconds while the P waves fade means conduction through the heart is slowing, so the patient is moving toward that pre-arrest stage. The technician reports it to the provider at once and stays nearby, because LITFL notes that arrest can come with little warning and the potassium value may not match the tracing. Taller or more widespread T waves and a rate still in the normal range are earlier, less urgent findings.

Irregularly irregularNo pattern at allAF, flutter with variable block, MAT
Exam tip Grouped beats: measure the PR of each beat in the group next. If the pulse felt irregular but the 10-second strip is regular, record a longer strip.

Early or late: premature vs escape beats

Premature (ectopic) beatEscape beat
TimingBefore the next expected beatAfter a pause, later than the next beat was due
WhyAn irritable spot fires earlyThe faster pacemaker slowed, stopped or was blocked; a backup took over
ExamplesPAC, PJC (narrow); PVC (wide, no P before it)Junctional, 40–60/min (narrow); ventricular, 20–40/min (wide)

Exam tip Exams test timing: early means premature, late means escape. An escape beat keeps the heart going; the problem is the pause before it.

Fast rhythms: narrow or wide, regular or not

Rate over 100RegularIrregular
Narrow (under 0.12 s)Sinus tachycardia, atrial tachycardia, SVT, flutter with a fixed ratio (for example 2:1)AF, flutter with variable block, MAT
Wide (0.12 s or more)VT until proved otherwise; SVT with a bundle branch block; paced; high potassiumAF with a bundle branch block or WPW; polymorphic VT (torsades)
Safety A regular wide-complex tachycardia is VT until proved otherwise. Being awake with a pulse does not rule out VT: tell the nurse at once and stay with the patient.

When you cannot name it, describe it

  • Rate (ventricular, and atrial if different) and regularity, with the pattern.
  • P waves: present or absent, shape, and how they relate to the QRS (ratio, or no fixed relation).
  • PR, QRS and QT in seconds; narrow or wide.
  • Extra beats (early or late, narrow or wide, how many, pattern) and pauses (length in seconds).
  • Time, lead, how it started and stopped, and the patient's symptoms.

Example: 'Rate 38/min, regular, QRS 0.14 s; P waves regular at 80/min with no fixed relation to the QRS; patient dizzy.' A precise description gets the right response before anyone names the rhythm.

What to report, and how fast

Local protocol sets the thresholds and who calls the rapid response or code team.

In depth

Measuring well

  • Paper: at 25 mm/s a small box is 0.04 s and a large box 0.20 s, so a 10-second strip is 50 large boxes. At 50 mm/s the boxes are worth half (0.02 s and 0.10 s) and complexes look wider; check the speed before calling a rate slow or a QRS wide.
  • Rate: the 300 and 1,500 methods divide into one R-to-R interval, so they are exact only when the rhythm is regular; 1,500 is the more precise. For irregular or slow rhythms, count complexes over a known time. Count the atrial rate separately when P waves and QRS complexes differ. The machine can miscount tall T waves or odd QRS shapes, so check its number.
  • PR: from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS. Normal 0.12–0.20 s. Over 0.20 s is first-degree block; under 0.12 s suggests pre-excitation or a junctional origin.
  • QRS: from the first deflection to the end of the QRS. LITFL gives a normal of 70–100 ms (110 ms is sometimes seen in healthy people); a bundle branch block or a ventricular rhythm needs 120 ms or more.
  • QT: from the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave, in the lead with the longest QT (usually II or V5–V6), over several beats. Find the end of the T where a line along its steepest downslope meets the baseline. Include a U wave over 1 mm that is fused to the T; leave out a small or separate one. A QT under half the preceding R-to-R interval is normal.
  • QTc: the QT corrected for rate. Long is over 440 ms in men and 460 ms in women (LITFL) or over 0.45 s and 0.47 s (Merck); over 500 ms raises the risk of torsades. Under 350 ms is abnormally short. The usual (Bazett) correction overcorrects above 100/min and undercorrects below 60/min, so a QTc from a fast or slow strip is an estimate.

Regularity in practice

  • Mark two R waves on a paper edge or set the calipers, then walk the mark along the strip. Do the same for P waves: P waves can be regular while the QRS complexes are not, and the reverse.
  • Sinus arrhythmia: the P-to-P interval varies by more than 120 ms, usually speeding with breathing in and slowing with breathing out. It is normal, most common in the young; when it is not tied to breathing it is more often a sign of disease.
  • Groups of beats separated by pauses mean a pattern: measure the PR of each beat in the group (a lengthening PR before a dropped beat is Wenckebach), and look for early beats.
  • Muscle tremor or electrical interference can look like the wavy baseline of AF, but under it the QRS complexes stay regular.

P waves and how they relate to the QRS

  • Sinus P waves are upright in II and look alike. An early P wave of a different shape is a PAC; at least 3 different P shapes is MAT over 100/min or wandering atrial pacemaker at 100 or less.
  • P waves inverted in II, III and aVF, just before, inside or just after the QRS (within about 0.1 s), point to a junctional origin. P waves after the QRS can also be retrograde from a ventricular beat.
  • P waves hide. Look for a hump or notch on a T wave that is not on the others: a PAC buried in the T wave, or a P wave of a block marching through.
  • No P waves: think AF (a wavy baseline) or sinus arrest with an escape rhythm. Sawtooth waves are flutter.
  • More P waves than QRS complexes means some P waves are not conducted (block). P waves and QRS complexes that are each regular but keep no fixed PR are dissociated; in third-degree block the P rate is faster than the QRS rate. An occasional P wave that does capture is a clue to the mechanism: note it.

Premature beats, pauses and escape

FeaturePACPVC
P waveEarly, different shape (may hide in the T)None before it; sometimes inverted after it
QRSUsually narrow; can be wide (aberrant, often RBBB-shaped) or absent (blocked)0.12 s or more, abnormal shape, T wave opposite the QRS
Pause afterUsually less than compensatory: the sinus node is resetUsually fully compensatory: the beats either side are two R-to-R intervals apart
  • Patterns: bigeminy (every other beat), trigeminy (every third), quadrigeminy (every fourth), couplet (two in a row). Three or more ventricular beats in a row over 100/min is VT: nonsustained if it stops on its own within 30 s, sustained if it lasts longer or causes compromise.
  • Unifocal PVCs share one shape; multifocal PVCs have two or more shapes. More than 5 PVCs a minute on a routine EKG counts as frequent.
  • PVCs increase with caffeine, alcohol, stimulant drugs, low oxygen and electrolyte problems; PVCs that increase with exercise can mean ischemia, so report them during a stress test.
  • Holter burden is a share of all beats: PVC count ÷ total beats × 100 (15,450 ÷ 103,000 = 15%).
  • A blocked PAC is an early P wave with no QRS after it, often hidden in the T wave before a pause. Before calling a pause sinus arrest or AV block, compare that T wave with the others for a hidden early P.
  • Escape: when beats from above slow or stop, a lower pacemaker takes over. Merck gives junctional escape as 35–50/min (LITFL 40–60), narrow, and fairly reliable; ventricular escape is 20–40/min, wide and unreliable. Causes include severe sinus slowing, sinus arrest, high-grade or complete AV block, high potassium, and beta-blocker, calcium channel blocker or digoxin toxicity.
Safety A slow, wide escape rhythm is unreliable and can stop. Report it at once with the rate and how the patient looks.

Sorting a fast rhythm

  • A wide-complex tachycardia is a rate of 100/min or more with a QRS of 120 ms or more. The causes are VT, a supraventricular rhythm with a bundle branch block or rate-related aberrancy, conduction down an accessory pathway (WPW), fast ventricular pacing, and QRS widening from high potassium or drug toxicity.
  • Clues to VT a technician can see and report: P waves marching through at their own slower rate (AV dissociation), capture or fusion beats, a very wide QRS (over 160 ms), and all chest leads pointing the same way. Patient history matters too: age over 35, prior heart attack or structural heart disease.
  • The published criteria miss many VTs (sensitivity about 20–50% for some), so a wide tachycardia without these clues can still be VT. If in doubt, it is VT.
  • Narrow and regular: sinus tachycardia speeds up and slows down gradually; SVT usually starts and stops suddenly. Note how the rhythm began and ended, which is why a captured onset is worth saving.
  • AF with a wide QRS may mean WPW: the strip is fast, irregular and wide. Report it at once.
  • Torsades: a fast polymorphic VT whose QRS axis twists around the baseline, usually on a long QT and often started by a short-long-short sequence of beats.

Reporting and documenting

  • Say who and where (patient, room or monitor), what (your description, and the rhythm name only if you are sure), when (time it started and whether it is still going) and how the patient is (symptoms, or 'patient not yet seen').
  • Ask the nurse to repeat the finding back. Record the finding, the time of the call and the name of the person who took it; save or print the strip.
  • In a telemetry room, monitor technicians watch the central station and alert the nurse to meaningful events; they do not give bedside care. One study of monitor watchers tested pauses over 2 s, second- or third-degree block or junctional rhythm under 40/min, runs of VT, AF or flutter, SVT and pacemaker failure.
  • Ambulatory monitors (Holter, event, mobile telemetry): the lab's protocol sets who is told and how fast. In the ISHNE-HRS example criteria, a sustained regular wide tachycardia at 160/min or more or prolonged asystole goes to the physician at any hour; Mobitz II or third-degree block, syncope, pacemaker malfunction, and a pause of 4 s or more or a sustained rate of 30/min or less with symptoms go within 24 hours; new AF or flutter lasting 30 s or more goes during office hours. Note whether the patient pressed the button or wrote a symptom in the diary at that time; matching symptoms to the rhythm is the point of the test.
  • Stay in scope: technicians record, measure, recognize and report. Decisions about treatment belong to the nurse or provider.

Exam pearls

  • Use the same order on every strip; most wrong answers skip regularity or the P-to-QRS step.
  • 300 or 1,500 methods only for regular rhythms; count complexes for irregular ones.
  • Early beat: premature. Late beat after a pause: escape.
  • Equal P-to-P and equal R-to-R intervals with no fixed PR: dissociation, not sinus rhythm.
  • Regular, wide and fast: VT until proved otherwise.
  • Patient first: a rhythm that does not fit the patient is checked at the bedside, and an unresponsive patient gets help and CPR before the strip is studied.
