Try one

Safety, Compliance and Patient Care practice question

During a Holter hookup, a patient says he has had chest pressure on the stairs this week. He asks the technician not to tell the provider because he fears losing work time. What should the technician do?

  1. Tell the front-desk staff so they can book a sooner follow-up visit
  2. Promise to keep it private and encourage him to raise it himself at his next visit
  3. Remind him to record the pressure in his symptom diary and let the monitor results speak for themselves
  4. Explain that the care team needs to know, and tell the nurse before he leaves

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Front-desk staff do not need the clinical detail to schedule a visit, so this shares more than the minimum necessary with people outside the care team.
  • B. Confidentiality matters, but it permits sharing among the care team, and a promise of silence would leave new chest symptoms unreviewed.
  • C. The diary is useful, but it will not be read until the monitor is returned days later, and it is not a way of reporting symptoms that need prompt attention.
  • D. Correct. Information needed for his care can be shared with the nurse and provider, and the technician should say so honestly rather than promise secrecy.

Patients can trust that their information is protected, but sharing with the nurse and provider for treatment does not require a separate consent, while staff who only schedule visits should get no more than they need. New chest pressure with exertion is information the care team must act on. Keeping secrets with a patient is a boundary red flag, so the technician does not promise silence; she reports to the nurse and tells the patient plainly who will be told and why.

Sources: AMA Code of Medical Ethics Opinion 3.2.1: Confidentiality; HIPAA Privacy and Breach Rules, 45 CFR 164; NCSBN: A Nurse's Guide to Professional Boundaries

Privacy (HIPAA)

  • Share with the care team for treatment. Employers and coaches need the patient's written authorization; coworkers with no part in the care get nothing.
  • Family at the bedside: only if the patient agrees or does not object when asked.
  • Minimum necessary: never open charts of patients you are not caring for.
  • Curtain closed, voice low, no talk in elevators; screens locked; printouts in confidential shred bins.
  • Your own login only; no tracing photos on a personal phone.
  • Misdirected fax or wrong-chart tracing: report it at once to your supervisor or privacy officer.

Electrical safety

  • Inspect before use: frayed cord, cracked case, exposed wire, bent or missing ground prong. Take it out of service and report it.
  • Plug and unplug with dry hands; keep fluids off the cart.
  • A patient with pacing wires or a central line can fibrillate from a tiny current. Never touch exposed wire ends.
  • A tingle or shock from a device: stop using it and report it.

Unresponsive: CPR and AED at your scope

  1. No response and no normal breathing (gasping is not breathing): shout for help, call the code team, send for the AED.
  2. If trained, compress: center of the chest, about 5 cm, 100–120/min, full recoil, pauses under 10 s.
  3. AED: turn it on and follow the prompts; pads on a bare, dry chest. Nobody touches the patient during analysis or a shock; compress again right after.
  4. Then take the role you are given: compressor, recorder or runner.

Moving patients and loads

  • Lift device or more staff for a patient who cannot help; about 35 lb (16 kg) is the most to lift of a patient by hand.
  • Care in bed: bed raised to spare your back. Transfer: bed lowered so the feet touch the floor; brakes locked; wheelchair beside the bed, footrests away.
  • Bend the knees, back straight, load close, between knees and shoulders; turn with your feet.
  • Gait belt for a pivot transfer; the patient never holds your neck.
  • A patient starts to fall: lower them to the floor, then get the nurse.

Patient identification

A tracing filed under the wrong name harms two patients: one loses a finding and the other gains a false one. Identification is therefore done at the bedside, every time, before any electrode goes on. Ask for at least two identifiers, usually full name and date of birth, sometimes the medical record number, and ask them as open questions so the patient supplies the answer (Chang 2025). A nod to 'Maria?' proves nothing: an anxious, drowsy or hard-of-hearing patient may agree to any name. Compare the answers with the order and, for inpatients, the wristband.

  • Two patients with the same or similar names on one unit, or siblings at a pediatric visit: check both identifiers on each order; a similar surname is a known trap.
  • Wristband and order disagree, or the patient's answer matches only one: do not pick a source. Hold the test and tell the nurse so registration can correct the band or the order.
  • Child, sedated, confused or nonverbal patient: check the band against the order, or ask the parent or caregiver the same open questions (Chang 2025). A resident with dementia and no band: the nurse verifies identity and replaces the band before you record.
  • Unidentified emergency patient: use the temporary name and number registration assigns, exactly as on the band.
  • Labeling: enter or confirm the identifiers on the machine with the patient present, and finish one patient before starting the next. A tracing with no name in the header is not filed under a guess; tell the nurse and repeat it.
  • A wrong-chart tracing found later: report it so it is moved by the record's correction process, and file an event report.

Consent, refusal and communication

Consent rests on a patient's right to information and to choose (AMA Code 2.1.1). For routine, low-risk tests it is implied by the patient's conduct after an explanation; more invasive or risky interventions always need express informed consent, and in an emergency consent is presumed (Merck). The 2026 NHA test plan asks technicians to obtain and document implied or informed (verbal or written) consent. Which tests need a signature is set by facility policy; exercise stress testing commonly does.

  • Explain before you touch: what the test is, how long it takes, what you will uncover, that it does not hurt, and what the patient must do (lie still, breathe normally, do not talk).
  • Refusal is a right, not a sign of incapacity (Merck). Ask what worries the patient, answer what you can, and accept the answer. Tell the nurse so the provider learns the test was not done, and chart the refusal in the patient's words.
  • Agreeing at the start does not oblige the patient to finish. If the patient says stop, stop, remove the electrodes and report.
  • Patient without capacity: the surrogate (health care power of attorney or next of kin) decides whether the test is done, but the patient still takes part as far as possible. A frightened patient who resists is paused and calmed, and the nurse is told; nobody holds the patient down.
  • Minors: parents or guardians give permission and the child's assent is sought, more strongly as the child matures (AMA Code 2.2.1). A grandparent or other adult saying the parents agreed by phone: hold the test and ask the nurse who may consent; state law varies.
  • Witnessing a signature: you witness that this patient signed. If the patient says he does not understand or is unsure, do not witness; tell the nurse so the provider can explain again.
  • Verify understanding with teach-back ('Tell me how you will press the event button'), not 'Do you understand?'.

HIPAA for technicians

Protected health information (PHI) is individually identifiable health information in any form: a tracing with a name, a schedule listing patients and reasons for tests, a spoken remark about room 412 (45 CFR 160.103). Technicians are part of the covered entity's workforce, paid or not, and must be trained on its privacy policies and follow them; workforce members who do not are sanctioned (164.530(b), (e)).

Teaching images go through the facility's approved process, never a personal phone or a group chat. Incidental disclosure (a name overheard in a waiting room despite reasonable care) is allowed; carelessness is not. Calling a child back by first name only, rather than full name and reason for the test, is an example of reasonable care.

Infection control in detail

Standard precautions apply to every patient, whatever the diagnosis: hand hygiene, PPE chosen for the expected exposure, respiratory hygiene, careful handling of sharps, and cleaning of equipment and surfaces (CDC). Clean hands immediately before touching a patient, when moving from a soiled body site to a clean one, after touching the patient or the surroundings, after contact with blood or body fluids, and immediately after removing gloves. Gloves are not a substitute for hand hygiene. Change gloves when they are soiled or torn, between patients and before leaving the room.

  • Alcohol-based rub: enough to cover all surfaces, rub until dry, about 20 seconds. Soap and water: rub vigorously for at least 15 seconds. Soap and water when hands are visibly dirty, before eating, after the restroom, and during outbreaks of C. difficile or norovirus (alcohol does not kill C. difficile spores, though CDC still prefers rub outside outbreaks).
  • Nails: natural tips no longer than the fingertip; no artificial nails or extensions for direct contact with high-risk patients such as ICU and operating room patients.
  • Contact precautions: gown and gloves for any contact with the patient or the room, put on as you enter and removed before you leave. Use the room's dedicated BP cuff where provided; disinfect anything shared before it touches another patient.
  • Droplet precautions: facemask on entering. A coughing patient in a waiting room is given a mask and tissues and seated apart from others.
  • Airborne precautions (TB, measles, chickenpox, disseminated zoster): fit-tested N95 or higher respirator, airborne infection isolation room with the door closed; staff who are not immune do not enter for measles or chickenpox if immune staff are available.
  • Blood on equipment (a bleeding razor nick, a scraped knuckle on a treadmill rail): gloves, remove the visible blood, then disinfect with the facility's approved product.

Electrical safety in detail

Electrical injury depends on how much current flows and where it goes. Through the skin, about 1 mA tingles, 5 mA hurts, 15 mA causes sustained muscle contraction (tetany, so a person may be unable to let go), 50 mA can paralyze the breathing muscles and about 100 mA can cause ventricular fibrillation (macroshock). If the current reaches the heart directly, through temporary pacing wires, a guidewire or a fluid-filled central line, as little as 0.05–0.1 mA can cause fibrillation (microshock) (LITFL Part One). That is why patient-connected equipment is built to leak almost no current, and why grounding and inspection matter.

  • Hazards OSHA names in hospitals: plugs with bent blades or a missing ground prong, cords damaged by door edges, staples or age, frayed cords or cracked housings that expose live parts, and plugging or unplugging with wet hands.
  • Damaged or defective portable equipment, extension cords included, is removed from service until it has been repaired and tested (OSHA). Tag it and report it through biomedical engineering as your facility directs.
  • Outlets near water must be properly grounded, and OSHA calls for ground-fault circuit interrupters (GFCIs) on standard 120-volt receptacles; do not set fluids on the ECG machine or cart.
  • Use the three-prong grounded cord supplied with the machine; use only power strips and cords the facility has approved.
  • Never touch the exposed ends of pacing wires, and do not let them touch metal; report an uncovered wire to the nurse.
  • If a patient or staff member feels a tingle or shock from a device, stop using it, unplug it if safe, check the patient and report it.

Electrical interference on the tracing (60-cycle artifact) is a recording problem, not a shock hazard by itself; for fixes see Artifact and Lead Problems.

  • Pulse: index and middle fingers, not the thumb; never press both sides of the neck at once. A regular pulse may be counted for 30 s and doubled; count a full 60 s when it is irregular. Let the patient rest at least 10 minutes for a true resting rate. A faint pulse that comes and goes under light pressure needs a better site or another method, not a guess.
  • Respirations: one rise and fall is one breath. Note rate and effort: neck muscles working, nostrils flaring or inability to finish sentences are reported even when the rate seems normal.
  • Blood pressure preparation: empty bladder; no caffeine, tobacco, food or exercise for 30 minutes; seated at least 5 minutes with back supported, legs uncrossed, feet flat; bare upper arm supported at heart level; no talking; at least two readings 1 minute apart (MedlinePlus, CDC).
  • Cuff and arm errors: a regular cuff on an arm that needs a large or extra-large cuff read 4.8 and 19.5 mm Hg higher systolic; on an arm that needs a small cuff it read 3.6 lower (Cuff(SZ) trial). An arm hanging at the side read about 6.5 mm Hg higher than one supported on a desk (ARMS trial).
  • Adult BP categories: normal under 120 and under 80; elevated 120–129 and under 80; stage 1 130–139 or 80–89; stage 2 140 or higher or 90 or higher.
  • Orthostatic vital signs: BP and pulse after 5 minutes supine, then after 3 minutes standing. A fall of 20 mm Hg systolic or 10 diastolic within 3 minutes is orthostatic hypotension; dizziness on standing is reported and the patient sits or lies down (AAFP 2022).
  • Temperature averages 37 °C (98.6 °F), with a range of about 36.1–37.2 °C (97–99 °F); over 38 °C (100.4 °F) usually means fever. After a hot or cold drink, wait and recheck an oral reading.
  • SpO₂ 95–100% is normal; 92% or lower is reported, 88% or lower is urgent (MedlinePlus); some patients with COPD run lower targets set by the provider. Readings can be 2–4% off; dark nail polish, artificial nails, cold skin, poor circulation, tobacco use and darker skin pigmentation can affect accuracy.

Recognizing patient compromise

The 2026 NHA plan asks technicians to report signs needing further evaluation: cardiopulmonary compromise, altered mental status, diaphoresis, cyanosis, confusion, dyspnea and chest tightness. The technician's job is to notice, stay, call and hand over what has been recorded; diagnosis and treatment belong to the nurse and provider.

  • Heart attack: chest pain, heaviness or discomfort in the center or left chest; discomfort in the arms, back, shoulders, neck, jaw or upper abdomen; shortness of breath; sweating; unusual fatigue; nausea; light-headedness; a racing or irregular heartbeat. Women more often have unusual fatigue, older adults shortness of breath, and older adults and people with diabetes may have few symptoms (NHLBI). A patient who reports a few minutes of chest tightness that has passed is still reported.
  • A patient wearing an ambulatory monitor who phones with crushing chest pressure is told to call 9-1-1 and not to drive in (NHLBI: never delay calling).
  • Stroke (BE FAST): balance, eyes, face droop, arm drift, speech, time. Call for help immediately and note the time the signs first appeared, because treatment depends on it (CDC).
  • Fainting: a brief loss of consciousness from reduced blood flow to the brain. Lower the patient to lying, check breathing, loosen tight clothing at the neck, raise the feet if there is no injury, and call the nurse. Fainting with chest pain, a pounding or irregular heartbeat, an injury, or slow recovery needs urgent care (MedlinePlus).
  • Breathing trouble: breathlessness at rest, confusion or agitation, neck and shoulder muscles working, and chest pain are red flags (Merck); Merck adds that a saturation under 90% means a serious condition, though a normal reading does not rule one out. A patient who becomes breathless when laid flat for an ECG is sat up and the nurse is told; record in a semi-upright position if the provider accepts it and chart the position.
  • Allergic reaction to electrodes or tape: hives alone are reported; hives with throat tightness, a hoarse or whispered voice, swelling of the face or tongue, wheeze or dizziness can be anaphylaxis, which develops within minutes. Call for emergency help, stay, and start CPR if the patient stops breathing normally.
  • Low cardiac output or poor perfusion: pallor or cyanosis, sweating, new confusion, fainting or near-fainting, or a BP that falls; report it with the vital signs and the time.

CPR and the AED: the technician's part

This summary is not a course and does not replace the BLS training your employer requires; it lists what exam items test at technician level.

  • Recognize arrest: unresponsive and not breathing normally. Gasping is not normal breathing, and if in doubt treat it as arrest. Take no more than 10 seconds to check (Singapore 2026).
  • Get help first: shout, use the code button or number, and send someone for the AED. Do not start compressions on a patient who is breathing normally, coughing or moving.
  • Compressions: heel of one hand in the center of the chest with the other on top, about 5 cm (2 in) deep, 100–120 per minute, letting the chest rise fully each time, pauses under 10 seconds (MedlinePlus). Keep the arms straight and the shoulders over the chest; a bed lowered and put in CPR mode makes that possible.
  • Two or more rescuers swap compressors about every 2 minutes with as little interruption as possible (Singapore 2026).
  • AED: turn it on as soon as it arrives and follow the voice prompts. Pads go on a bare, dry chest where pictured; wipe a wet chest, shave hair at the pad sites if needed, remove a medicine patch and wipe off what it leaves, and place a pad about four finger-breadths away from an implanted pacemaker or defibrillator. Everyone stands clear during analysis and the shock, then compressions restart at once; the AED reanalyzes about every 2 minutes (Singapore 2026).
  • If the patient starts breathing normally or moving, stop compressions, leave the pads on, and watch breathing until the team takes over.
  • Choking adult who cannot speak or cough: call for help and give the choking relief taught in your BLS course; if the patient becomes unresponsive, lower them to the floor and start CPR.
  • Team role: compressor, AED operator, runner or recorder. A recorder writes every team action and its time as it happens, not only their own.
Safety In a stress lab or cardiac rehabilitation gym, know where the defibrillator and emergency cart are and check them as the lab requires. Drugs and shock settings are for the code team, not the technician.

Body mechanics and safe transfers

Repeated manual patient handling causes overexertion injuries in health care workers, and OSHA's safe patient handling guidance relies on lift and transfer equipment, not technique alone. Applied to patient handling, the revised NIOSH lifting equation gives about 35 lb (16 kg) as the most a worker should lift by hand, and only for a cooperative patient unlikely to move suddenly; above that, use assistive devices (Waters 2007).

  • Lifting boxes and cases: plan the route and the landing spot; get close, bend the knees, keep the spine straight, grip with both hands, tighten the abdomen, lift smoothly, keep the load close and centered, and keep the body pointed at the load. The safest zone is from mid-thigh to mid-chest; move a heavy case to a waist-high shelf rather than above the shoulders (CCOHS).
  • Turning a patient for posterior leads: raise the bed to a height that spares your back, lay it flat, lower the rail on the side you turn toward, one foot ahead of the other, one hand on the shoulder and one on the hip, then rail up (MedlinePlus). A patient who cannot help needs a draw sheet and a second person.
  • Patient slid down the bed: raise the bed, flatten it and use a draw sheet with help; do not drag the patient by the arms.
  • Bed or table to wheelchair: only for a patient who can stand on at least one leg; otherwise use a lift. Lower the bed so the feet touch the floor, chair beside the bed, brakes on, footrests away, nonslip footwear, gait belt, pivot toward the chair, and the patient never wraps arms around your head or neck (MedlinePlus).
  • After lying down, have the patient sit at the edge for a few moments in case of dizziness before standing (MedlinePlus); allow longer, and ask about dizziness, after days in bed or a pharmacologic stress test.
  • A falling patient: lower them to the nearest flat surface, usually the floor, protecting the head; do not try to hold them up. Do not get them up alone; call the nurse to assess first.
  • Several staff moving a heavy patient between stretcher and bed: match the surfaces' heights, lock both, use a transfer board or slide sheet, and move on one person's count.

Scope, ethics and documentation

NHA describes the CET's work as setting up and administering EKGs and stress tests, preparing patients for Holter or ambulatory monitoring, editing and delivering results to physicians for analysis, scheduling, and transcribing physicians' interpretations. Anything outside your job description and training, such as giving medicines, drawing blood where that is not part of your role, or recording special leads you have not been trained to place, is declined politely with an offer to help within your role. A patient who calls you 'the nurse' is told your role.

  • The NHA Code of Ethics asks certified technicians to be honest in all professional interactions, keep learning, act in the public's best interest, and protect the dignity and privacy of every patient.
  • A patient's question about the tracing ('Is my rhythm normal?') is answered honestly about your role: the provider reads it. Report anything that worried you to the nurse.
  • Boundaries: no personal phone numbers or social media contact with patients, no gifts beyond what policy allows (a ring pressed on you by a confused resident goes back, and the nurse is told), and ask a colleague to take a patient you know personally.
  • Do not copy yesterday's tracing as today's, change a time, or chart a test before it is done, even when asked. Each tracing is recorded fresh when ordered.
  • Suspected abuse or neglect (unexplained bruises, a companion who answers every question): tell the nurse, provider or supervisor privately, before the patient leaves, and follow facility policy.

Errors and near misses, such as a band that almost let the wrong patient be tested, are reported through the facility's event reporting system. Frontline staff who were involved usually file the first report, and these systems are generally confidential (AHRQ PSNet). Reporting a near miss is how the next one is prevented; it is not an admission of misconduct.
