Before the test

An exercise test records a 12-lead ECG, blood pressure and symptoms as the workload rises in steps. A drug replaces exercise when the patient cannot exercise adequately.

Carluccio 2026, adapted from the AHA exercise standards; BP figure from Merck.
Absolute: the test does not startRelative: the clinician decides
Infarction within 2 days; high-risk unstable anginaKnown left main stenosis; moderate aortic stenosis
Uncontrolled arrhythmia with hemodynamic compromiseUncontrolled-rate tachyarrhythmia; acquired complete heart block
Symptomatic severe aortic stenosis; decompensated heart failureObstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with a severe resting gradient
Acute PE, myocarditis, pericarditis, aortic dissection or pneumothorax; active endocarditisResting BP over about 200/110 mm Hg; recent stroke or TIA
A disability that makes testing unsafeMarked anemia, electrolyte imbalance, hyperthyroidism; limited cooperation
Safety The technician does not weigh contraindications. Hold the test and show the clinician: new chest pain, a new ECG change or block, resting BP still over about 200/110 on a repeat reading, or missing consent.
  • Verify the order, two identifiers and consent. Explain: it starts at a walk, gets faster and steeper about every 3 minutes, and symptoms are reported at once.
  • No food, tobacco, caffeine or alcohol for about 3 hours, often no caffeine for 24; walking shoes. Vasodilator scans: no caffeine for at least 12 hours (label; Merck says 24). Follow the lab's own times.
  • Medicines only as the provider directs (beta blockers blunt the rate rise). Report sildenafil, tadalafil or vardenafil taken in the past 24–48 hours: nitrates for chest pain could then drop the BP dangerously.
  • Clip, clean and lightly abrade the skin. Mason-Likar: arm electrodes just below the outer clavicles, leg electrodes above the iliac crests, chest leads as usual (see Electrode and Lead Placement). Record and label the resting baseline in this layout.
  • Defibrillator and crash cart checked and at hand.

Try one

Exercise and Pharmacologic Stress Testing practice question

In a stress lab, a technician phones a patient the day before a treadmill test. The patient says, 'I'll have my usual coffee and a full breakfast an hour before, and wear my work slacks and dress shoes.' Which instruction should the technician give?

  1. Skip food and caffeine for the time the lab sets, and wear loose clothes and walking shoes
  2. Skip food and caffeine, and stop your regular medicines that morning
  3. Keep your coffee but skip breakfast, and wear loose clothes and walking shoes
  4. Eat a light breakfast an hour before, skip coffee, and wear loose clothes and walking shoes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. This matches the usual preparation for an exercise stress test: no food or caffeine for the stated period, with loose clothing and comfortable shoes.
  • B. The diet advice is right, but the technician cannot tell a patient to stop medicines. Whether to take them that day is a question for the provider.
  • C. Tempting because it fixes the food and the clothing. Caffeine is also avoided before the test, usually for 24 hours, so the coffee remains a problem.
  • D. Close, but eating within an hour of exercise is not what the lab asks. Food is usually avoided for about 3 hours or more before the test.

MedlinePlus lists the usual preparation for an exercise stress test: no eating, smoking, or caffeine or alcohol for 3 hours or more beforehand, avoiding caffeine for 24 hours in most cases, and comfortable shoes and loose clothing for exercise. Patients ask the provider whether to take regular medicines that day and do not stop one without talking to the provider. A technician gives the lab's written instructions and its own times, so that the patient hears one consistent plan, and passes medicine questions to the nurse because only the provider can answer them.

  • Modified Bruce: two 3-minute stages at 1.7 mph, 0% then 5%, then Bruce stage 1; for older or deconditioned patients.
  • Naughton: small stages of about 1 MET, for low capacity or heart failure. Cycle protocols step up in watts.
  • Aim for 8–12 minutes of exercise; repeat tests on the same protocol.

Monitoring through recovery

  • ECG continuously; a 12-lead at rest (supine and standing), at the end of each stage, at peak, at 1, 2, 4 and 6 minutes of recovery, and at any change.
  • BP at rest, each stage (or every 2 minutes), peak and recovery: by hand on a treadmill; confirm any abnormal automatic reading manually.
  • Symptoms and Borg each stage; watch gait, color and sweating.
  • Recovery: slow the belt gradually, then seat the patient for at least 6 minutes or until near baseline (patients are told to expect 10–15 minutes); lie a symptomatic patient down. ST depression only in recovery still counts.
Safety Collapse, no response or no normal breathing: call the code team, start CPR, and use the AED as trained.

When to stop

Exam tip Exams return to three absolutes: the patient's request (honor it, even below target), a tracing or BP that cannot be monitored (sweat-loosened electrodes end the test), and ST elevation.

Reading the ST response

Finding (80 ms after the J point)Reads as
Horizontal or downsloping depression of 1 mm or more in 3 beats in a rowPositive (ischemic)

Measure from the PR baseline; above 130/min read at 60 ms. LBBB, ventricular pacing, pre-excitation or resting ST depression of 1 mm or more make ST changes unreadable, and so does digoxin therapy; these patients go to imaging, often with vasodilator stress.

Technician and clinician

The technician prepares the patient and room, runs the protocol, records ECG, BP, symptoms and Borg, reports changes at once, stops the treadmill for an absolute criterion or the patient's request per protocol, calls for help, and starts CPR and uses the AED when trained. The supervising clinician decides on contraindications and medicines and on relative criteria, gives and stops every stress drug, and interprets the test.

Borg anchors used on this page: 6 is no exertion and 20 is maximal; the scale was built so that the rating times 10 roughly tracks heart rate. In a study Carluccio cites (Sirico), most healthy subjects passed 85% of predicted at a rating of only about 14, a moderate effort, which is why Carluccio treats a rating over 17 as the sign of a maximal test. The CR10 version runs from 0 to 10.

What a stress test answers

QuestionWhat the test looks at
Is there inducible ischemia?ST response, angina, BP response; as a diagnostic test most useful when the likelihood of coronary disease is intermediate (Brazilian guideline); a normal result is most reassuring in lower-risk patients with a readable resting ECG (Merck)
How fit is the patient, and what is the prognosis?METs reached, heart-rate and BP responses, heart-rate recovery
Do symptoms or arrhythmias come with exertion?Ectopy, tachycardia, rate-related AV or bundle branch block, chronotropic incompetence
Specific conditionsAdrenergic arrhythmias such as CPVT, QTc in recovery for long QT, pre-excitation risk, pacemaker rate response, symptoms in aortic stenosis, mitral stenosis gradient (with echo)

The exercise ECG alone is a modest detector of coronary disease: Carluccio puts sensitivity and specificity at 60–77%, and Merck gives 58–66% and 61–62%, lower in women. Adding echo or perfusion imaging raises accuracy. Merck puts the risk of infarction or sudden death at up to 0.06% of tests, which is why the defibrillator and trained staff are part of every setup.

Pharmacologic stress is used when the patient cannot walk long enough to reach the target, for example from deconditioning, arthritis, obesity or peripheral arterial disease (Merck). The EACVI perfusion consensus also prefers drug stress for a patient with LBBB, a patient who depends on ventricular pacing, or uncontrolled AF, because conduction disease creates image artifacts that are less evident with a vasodilator, and it allows a submaximal exercise test to be converted to a drug protocol on the spot.

For dobutamine stress echo, the 2026 Brazilian stress echo guideline adds obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, a recent or mobile ventricular thrombus and resting BP over 200/110 mm Hg as absolute, and a thoracic or intracranial aneurysm over 40 mm as relative. Which list a lab follows is the medical director's decision; the technician's job is to notice the finding and report it before exercise or infusion begins.

Electrodes and the Mason-Likar tracing

Swinging arms and legs swamp a limb-lead ECG, so stress labs move the limb electrodes to the torso. Carluccio places the arm electrodes at the outer ends of the infraclavicular fossae and the leg electrodes on stable sites above the iliac crests; Morales-Arteaga describes the same layout as just below the clavicles and on the lower torso near the hips. The chest electrodes stay at their standard sites. Sweat is the usual reason electrodes fail mid-test, so clip hair, clean off oil and lightly abrade every site.

  • The torso layout changes the tracing: the axis shifts to the right, R waves shrink in I and aVL and grow in II, III and aVF, and chest-lead R waves change too (Papouchado 1987, abstract).
  • The inferior leads in this layout behave partly like anterior leads, one reason exercise ECGs miss some inferior ischemia (same abstract).
  • Record the resting baseline in the same layout before exercise and keep it through the test, so changes reflect the heart rather than the electrodes (Morales-Arteaga 2026).
  • Label the tracing as torso limb leads; it is not interchangeable with a standard resting 12-lead from the chart.

Protocol details

  • Bruce (1973) uses 3-minute stages so the patient reaches a steady state before each increase. It reaches a higher peak heart rate and rate-pressure product than a ramp, which helps detect ischemia; its drawbacks are the large jumps between stages, which can unbalance a sedentary patient, and running in the later stages.
  • Carluccio describes the modified Bruce warm-up at 2.4 km/h (about 1.5 mph); Kim 2023, Reed 2019 and the Brazilian table give 1.7 mph. Labs program their own; read the protocol on the cart.
  • Under 8 minutes underestimates peak oxygen uptake; over 12 minutes, the test tends to end for leg fatigue or joint pain rather than a cardiac end point.
  • The cart estimates METs from speed and grade. Holding the handrails tightly makes the estimate read high; the Brazilian guideline gives a separate correction for rail holding. Encourage a light touch for balance only, and record whether the patient held on.
  • Predicted capacity for age: about 18 − 0.15 × age METs in men and 14.7 − 0.13 × age in women (Carluccio).
  • Cycle ergometer: for orthopedic, vascular or neurologic limits on walking. Peak oxygen uptake is usually 5–20% lower than on a treadmill. A mechanically braked cycle needs a steady cadence; an electronically braked one holds the workload whatever the cadence. 1 W is about 6 kg·m/min.
  • The belt may be slowed or briefly stopped in exceptional cases to confirm a BP or replace an electrode (Brazilian guideline); note the time on the record.

Blood pressure in depth

  • Measure by auscultation on a treadmill; automatic cuffs validated for exercise are acceptable, especially on a cycle, but an abnormal value is confirmed manually. The Brazilian guideline also asks for a second reading to confirm an asymptomatic drop.
  • Normal: systolic rises about 5–10 mm Hg per MET, diastolic stays within about 10 mm Hg, and systolic returns to resting levels within about 6 minutes after peak.
  • High at peak: limits differ. Carluccio quotes the AHA at 210 mm Hg in men and 190 in women, the ESC at 220 and 200, and the ACSM at 225 for everyone. These describe an exaggerated response, not stop criteria.
  • Stop thresholds: Carluccio's table gives systolic over 250 or diastolic over 115 as absolute, with a note that athletes can exceed 250; the Brazilian guideline uses systolic over 250 or diastolic 120 or more (140 in known hypertension). Report either, after a manual check.
  • Falling with exercise: a drop of 20 mm Hg or more as workload rises is pathological; with angina or ST depression, especially at a low workload, it points to severe multivessel disease. It also occurs with cardiomyopathy, outflow obstruction, high vagal tone, low volume, antihypertensives and arrhythmia.
  • A low peak rate-pressure product (failure to raise either rate or pressure) signals poor cardiac reserve.

Stress drugs in depth

AgentLabeled or guideline facts the technician uses
AdenosineGiven as a continuous peripheral infusion with the tracer injected partway through. Contraindicated in second- or third-degree AV block or sinus node disease without a functioning pacemaker, and in bronchospastic lung disease. AV block in about 6% (first-degree 3%, second-degree 3%, third-degree 0.8%). New AF typically starts 1.5–3 minutes in and usually converts on its own. Common: flushing 44%, chest discomfort 40%, dyspnea 28%, headache 18%, throat, neck or jaw discomfort 15%. Dipyridamole increases its effect
RegadenosonInjected within 10 seconds into a peripheral IV of 22 gauge or larger, then a saline flush and the tracer 10–20 seconds later. Same AV block and sinus node contraindications. First-degree block in 3%; third-degree block and asystole within minutes reported after marketing. Heart rate over 100 in 22%; systolic fall over 35 mm Hg in 7%; BP can rise, especially when low-level exercise is added. Lowers the seizure threshold. Asthma and COPD are warnings, not contraindications. Dipyridamole is held at least 2 days when possible
DipyridamoleRaises the body's own adenosine; reactions such as nausea, headache or bronchospasm in about 10–20%. Merck lists asthma, acute infarction, unstable angina, critical aortic stenosis and systolic BP under 90 as contraindications
DobutamineBeta-1 effect raises rate and contractility; beta-2 effect dilates peripheral vessels. Starts working in 1–2 minutes, half-life about 2 minutes, raised in 3-minute steps. Used mainly when vasodilators are contraindicated (asthma, second-degree AV block) and with echo. About half of patients report a reaction (nausea, flushing, headache, a pounding in the neck or chest, tingling, urinary urgency, dyspnea, dry mouth); serious complications under 0.1%

Dobutamine end points in the 2026 stress echo guideline: reaching 85% of 220 − age, the end of the protocol or a limiting symptom, a new wall motion abnormality, sustained VT or complex arrhythmia, or a symptomatic fall in systolic BP (over 20 mm Hg) or in heart rate. New AF, SVT or complex ventricular ectopy and left ventricular dilatation are relative end points. When the rate will not climb, the physician may add atropine or handgrip exercise; metoprolol is kept on hand to reverse dobutamine. The technician's part is to time-stamp the dose stage, rhythm, BP and symptoms and to report a shortfall or a new rhythm.

Safety Both vasodilator labels warn that fatal cardiac events have occurred and that resuscitation must be ready before the drug is given (the regadenoson label names equipment and trained staff). Caffeine and other methylxanthines weaken the test (a false negative) and aminophylline, a methylxanthine, is what the clinician may give to end a severe reaction.

Documenting and reporting

  • Protocol, stage and time at stop, and total exercise time; the reason for stopping, in the patient's words when it is a symptom.
  • Resting and peak heart rate with the percentage of predicted; resting and peak BP; rate-pressure product; METs; Borg rating at peak.
  • Each ECG change with its leads, size in mm, shape (horizontal, downsloping, upsloping), the time and stage it began and when it resolved in recovery.
  • Arrhythmias and conduction changes with the time and workload; recovery values at each recorded minute.
  • Drug studies: the time of each injection or dose stage, reactions with their times, and what the clinician did.
  • Whom you told and when: a finding reported to the clinician is documented with the time of the report.
Exam tip Common exam calculations: 220 − age, then × 0.85, rounded to the nearest whole beat; METs = VO₂ ÷ 3.5; rate-pressure product = heart rate × systolic BP; percent of predicted = peak ÷ (220 − age).
