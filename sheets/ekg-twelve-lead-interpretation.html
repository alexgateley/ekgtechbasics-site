Try one

Reading a 12-Lead practice question

In a cardiology clinic, a technician records a follow-up 12-lead ECG on a 29-year-old who was diagnosed with acute pericarditis 4 weeks ago and is now free of chest pain. The admission tracing showed diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression. Today the ST segments are at baseline and the T waves are inverted in most leads, with no Q waves and normal R-wave progression. Which description BEST fits today's tracing?

  1. Recovery after an anterior infarction
  2. Wellens pattern of a critical LAD lesion
  3. Hypokalemia flattening the T waves
  4. An expected later stage of pericarditis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Inverted T waves follow infarction too, but anterior infarction usually leaves Q waves or poor R-wave progression in V1 to V4. Both are absent, and the original elevation was diffuse.
  • B. Wellens T waves are deep and symmetric, or biphasic, in V2 and V3 after an episode of chest pain. These inversions are spread across most leads and follow a known pericarditis.
  • C. Low potassium can flatten or invert T waves, but it brings ST depression and prominent U waves. Here the ST segments sit at baseline and no U waves are described.
  • D. Correct. After the early ST elevation settles, pericarditis can move on to flat and then inverted T waves from about the third week, before the tracing returns to normal.

Acute pericarditis evolves through up to four ECG stages: widespread concave ST elevation with PR depression in the first weeks, return of the ST segment to baseline with flattening of the T waves, T-wave inversion from about the third week, and finally a normal tracing weeks later. Fewer than half of patients show every stage, so a tracing caught in the inversion stage can look alarming on its own. Comparing it with the admission tracing and the history lets the technician describe it accurately before the reader confirms it.

Safety A mimic is the reader's call, not yours. ST elevation in a patient with symptoms is reported at once even when you suspect a mimic or the machine statement is normal; compare with the last tracing and say what changed.

Report immediately

  • ST elevation meeting the thresholds, or new LBBB, in a patient with chest pain, jaw or arm pain, shortness of breath, sweating or nausea: suspected STEMI.
  • ST depression in V1–V3 with tall R waves (possible posterior MI); widespread ST depression with ST elevation in aVR.
  • Deep symmetric or biphasic T waves in V2–V3 after recent chest pain, even when the pain has gone; report it before any stress test is started.
  • Peaked T waves, a coved V1–V3 pattern, or any life-threatening rhythm.

Hand the tracing to the provider or nurse in person, stay with the patient, leave the electrodes on for repeat or extra leads (V4R, V7–V9), and document the time and who you told. If the patient becomes unresponsive, call the code team and start CPR or use the AED if you are trained.

Exam tip Door-to-ECG: a patient with possible ACS should have a 12-lead obtained and read within 10 minutes of arrival. Atypical symptoms (women, older patients, dizziness, abdominal pain) are where the minutes are lost.

Working out the axis

The cardiac axis is the overall direction of ventricular depolarization in the frontal plane, read from the six limb leads. Each limb lead has a fixed angle on the hexaxial system: I 0°, II +60°, aVF +90°, III +120°, aVL −30° and aVR −150°. A lead records a mainly positive QRS when the axis points toward it and a mainly negative QRS when the axis points away. The usual range is −30° to +90°.

  1. Quadrant: look at lead I and aVF. Both positive is normal; both negative is extreme axis; I negative with aVF positive is right axis.
  2. Lead I positive and aVF negative: look at lead II. Positive or equiphasic, the axis is between 0° and −30° (physiological left axis); negative, it is past −30° (left axis deviation). Lead III can stand in for aVF in this three-lead check.
  3. Refine with the isoelectric lead: find the limb lead whose positive and negative deflections cancel (or the flattest). The axis lies at 90° to it, on the side of the leads with the tallest R waves. Example: aVL (−30°) equiphasic with tall R waves in II and aVF puts the axis at +60°.
  4. Record the value the department asks for (degrees or a category) and compare it with the last tracing: a new axis shift is worth pointing out to the reader.
AxisRecognized causes (LITFL)
Left axis deviationLeft ventricular hypertrophy; left bundle branch block; inferior MI; ventricular pacing or ectopy; WPW; primum ASD; left anterior fascicular block (a diagnosis of exclusion); horizontal heart in a short, wide build
Right axis deviationRight ventricular hypertrophy; acute right heart strain such as pulmonary embolism; COPD; lateral STEMI; hyperkalemia; sodium-channel blocker poisoning; WPW; dextrocardia; ventricular ectopy; secundum ASD; left posterior fascicular block (a diagnosis of exclusion); vertical heart in a tall, thin build
Extreme axisVentricular rhythms (VT, AIVR, ectopy); hyperkalemia; severe RVH. A positive QRS in aVR with negative complexes elsewhere is the clue

Children differ: at birth the axis lies between about +30° and +190° and moves left with age, so right axis can be normal on a pediatric ECG. In emphysema a vertical axis near +90° (lead I equiphasic, aVF positive) is common.

P waves and atrial enlargement

The normal P wave is under 0.12 s (3 small boxes), under 2.5 mm tall in the limb leads and under 1.5 mm in the chest leads. It is upright in lead II and biphasic in V1, with the positive and negative parts about equal. Look at lead II and V1 for atrial size.

LVH and RVH: criteria and their limits

No single ECG definition of LVH is accepted everywhere, and the ECG is a screening clue, not the diagnosis; echocardiography or cardiac MRI confirms it. The voltage criteria are highly specific but insensitive: many patients with LVH on echo have a near-normal ECG. In obesity both Sokolow-Lyon and Cornell miss most cases (Snelder 2020). In athletes the trade runs the other way: in 180 male athletes, Sokolow-Lyon was positive in most of those with hypertrophy (sensitivity 68–87%) but also in most of those without it (specificity 20–23%), while Cornell was the reverse (sensitivity 22–40%, specificity 89–91%) (Samesima 2017). Isolated high voltage in a fit young person is usually a normal training finding.

Sokolow-LyonS wave in V1 plus the taller R wave in V5 or V635 mm or more

Measure at standard gain (10 mm/mV). If the cart was set to 5 mm/mV because the chest complexes overlapped, every measured height is half its true size: double it before applying a criterion, and say on the tracing that the gain was changed. Supporting findings make LVH more likely: left atrial enlargement, left axis deviation, and the strain pattern (ST depression and T-wave inversion in the lateral leads I, aVL, V5–V6). Strain is not required for the diagnosis. LVH also raises the ST segment in V1–V3, in proportion to the deep S waves; this is expected and is one reason the STEMI thresholds do not apply in LVH. Severe LVH can look almost like LBBB; the clue is the very high voltage.

RVH criteria (LITFL): right axis of +110° or more; a dominant R wave in V1 (over 7 mm tall or R/S ratio over 1); a dominant S wave in V5 or V6 (over 7 mm deep or R/S ratio under 1); and a QRS under 0.12 s, so the tall R in V1 is not a right bundle branch block. Supporting findings: right atrial enlargement, right ventricular strain (ST depression and T inversion in V1–V4 and in II, III, aVF), an S1 S2 S3 pattern, and deep S waves in I, aVL, V5–V6. Causes include pulmonary hypertension, mitral stenosis, pulmonary embolism, chronic lung disease, congenital heart disease such as tetralogy of Fallot or pulmonary stenosis, and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy. With RBBB there are no accepted RVH voltage criteria.

Ischemia, injury and infarction on the tracing

Measure ST shifts at the J point, where the QRS ends and the ST segment begins, against the baseline. The Fifth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction (2026) gives the working diagnosis of STEMI as new J-point ST elevation in two contiguous leads at the thresholds on the one-page table, in the absence of LVH or bundle branch block. The higher, sex- and age-specific V2–V3 limits rest on population data and allow for the J-point elevation that healthy people, men more than women, often show in those leads.

Stage or findingWhat the tracing shows
HyperacuteBroad, tall T waves, out of proportion to the QRS, often before the ST rises
Acute injuryST elevation in the leads facing the wall, with reciprocal depression in the leads opposite
IschemiaHorizontal or downsloping ST depression of 0.5 mm or more (1 mm or more is more specific); new T-wave inversion of 1 mm or more. Upsloping depression is nonspecific
Evolving and old infarctionQ waves form (within an hour in one LITFL example); T waves invert. Late presenters more often show Q waves or inverted T waves than ST elevation. Q waves usually stay for life but can shrink over months to years
Persistent ST elevation weeks later with deep Q wavesSuggests a ventricular aneurysm after a large MI

Normal Q waves: small, narrow septal q waves are normal in I, aVL, V5 and V6, and a deeper Q can be normal in III and aVR. Q waves are not normally seen in V1–V3. LITFL calls a Q wave pathological when it is wider than 0.04 s (1 small box), deeper than 2 mm or deeper than 25% of the QRS, or present in V1–V3. Pathological Q waves usually mean a current or past infarction, but hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, infiltrative disease, marked rotation of the heart and misplaced electrodes can also produce them, and the septal q waves in V5–V6 disappear in LBBB.

Reciprocal change is the mirror image of injury seen from the opposite side. Look for it actively: a subtle ST elevation can hide behind an obvious depression. aVL is the lead most directly opposite the inferior wall and a sensitive marker of inferior infarction: LITFL cites a series in which 91% of subtle inferior occlusions that did not meet STEMI criteria showed ST depression in aVL. Localized depression in the inferior leads should send the eye to aVL for a small high lateral elevation.

Right ventricular infarction complicates up to 40% of inferior STEMIs, usually from the RCA. Clues on the standard 12-lead are ST elevation in V1 (especially with ST depression in V2) and ST elevation higher in III than in II. Confirm with right-sided leads: V4R goes in the 5th right intercostal space at the midclavicular line, a mirror of V4, and is the most useful of V3R–V6R. Record them promptly when asked, because the elevation is short-lived: in about half of patients it is gone within 10 hours. Label the tracing as right-sided.

Posterior infarction: no standard lead faces the back of the left ventricle, so V1–V3 see it from the front, reversed. Horizontal ST depression in V1–V3 stands for ST elevation, a tall, broad R wave (over 0.03 s, R/S over 1 in V2) stands for a Q wave, and an upright T wave stands for an inverted one; turning the strip upside down makes V2 look like an anterior STEMI. The T wave is not a reliable rule: Birnbaum 2014 notes that in the first hours the T waves in V1–V3 can be negative or biphasic and become upright later, so ST depression in V1–V3 in a patient with symptoms is reported whatever the T wave does. Posterior MI comes with 15–20% of STEMIs, usually inferior or lateral, and is isolated in 5–10%. Confirm with posterior leads at the level of V6: V7 at the left posterior axillary line, V8 at the tip of the left scapula and V9 beside the spine. These leads sit farther from the heart, so 0.5 mm of ST elevation is enough. Label the tracing as posterior.

In LBBB the QRS is 0.12 s or more, V1 has a dominant S wave, the lateral leads (I, aVL, V5–V6) have broad monophasic R waves with no Q waves, and the R wave in V5–V6 peaks late. The ST segment and T wave point opposite the main QRS deflection ('appropriate discordance'), so leads with deep S waves (V1–V3) show some ST elevation that does not mean injury, usually under 25% of the S wave. Any ST change in the same direction as the QRS is concerning. New LBBB with chest pain is no longer treated as a STEMI equivalent by itself, but the reader needs to see it now.

Telling the mimics apart

ST elevation is not specific to infarction. Besides the six mimics on the one-page table, sources list coronary spasm, ventricular aneurysm, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, raised intracranial pressure, pulmonary embolism, myocarditis, hypothermia (J waves), sodium-channel blocking drugs and the minutes after electrical cardioversion. The Fifth UDMI's rule of thumb: elevation in one coronary territory with reciprocal depression fits a blocked artery; diffuse elevation without reciprocal change fits pericarditis or early repolarization. Against pericarditis, convex (dome-up) or horizontal ST elevation points to infarction (LITFL), but shape is not proof either way: athletes can show convex elevation in V2, Brugada is coved, and infarction is often concave (24% of true STEMIs in one series cited by Birnbaum 2014), so a concave shape never rules infarction out.

Early repolarization in a patient over 50 with risk factors and chest pain deserves caution, and comparison with an old tracing is the most useful thing a technician can provide. Fewer than half of patients with pericarditis go through all four stages, and its PR depression may last only hours, so serial tracings help.

Brugada pattern: type 1 is coved ST elevation over 2 mm in more than one of V1–V3 falling into a negative T wave, and is the only potentially diagnostic type; type 2 (saddleback) and type 3 (under 2 mm) are not diagnostic. Fever, ischemia, some drugs (sodium-channel blockers among them), cocaine, alcohol, high or low potassium and hypothermia can unmask it. The pattern carries a risk of sudden death when it comes with syncope, documented VF or polymorphic VT, or a family history of sudden death under 45, so report it at once in a patient who fainted or has a fever.

Hyperkalemia: changes usually appear at potassium of 6.0 mmol/L or more and march along the complex: tall, peaked T waves first, then wide, flat P waves and a long PR, then bundle branch blocks and a wide, bizarre QRS, slow rhythms, and at the extreme a sine wave, VF or asystole. The tracing does not track the level closely, and a near-normal ECG does not exclude a dangerous potassium. Report peaked T waves promptly, and repeat the tracing when the nurse or provider asks.

Reporting a suspected STEMI

The NHA CET plan asks the technician to recognize patterns of ischemia, injury and infarction and report them according to protocol, and to check tracing quality and lead accuracy before escalating. The technician measures, recognizes and reports; the provider makes the diagnosis and decides on treatment.

  1. Check the tracing in seconds: correct patient, standard speed and gain, no lead reversal or artifact in the leads that show the change. Do not delay the report to re-prep electrodes when the change is consistent beat to beat in contiguous leads.
  2. Give the tracing directly to the provider (or to the nurse to give to the provider) following the department's STEMI or chest pain protocol. Never put it in the routine reading queue.
  3. Say what you see in plain terms: 'ST elevation in II, III and aVF with depression in aVL; the patient has chest pain.' Mention a prior tracing if there is one.
  4. Stay with the patient and leave the electrodes in place for serial tracings, right-sided leads or posterior leads. Label any nonstandard lead positions on the tracing.
  5. Document the time recorded, the time reported and the person told.
  6. If the patient becomes unresponsive or pulseless, call for help or the code team and start CPR, using the AED if you are trained.

Door-to-ECG: ACC/AHA guidance, as quoted in the studies below, is that a patient with symptoms suggesting acute coronary syndrome has a 12-lead obtained and interpreted within 10 minutes of arrival; ESC gives the same 10-minute target. Early ECGs matter because ischemic changes appear before the muscle dies, and a late ECG delays reperfusion. Studies keep finding the target missed: in one, only 49% of men and 32% of women had their ECG within 10 minutes (Zègre-Hemsey 2011). Delays cluster in patients without classic chest pain (women, older patients, dizziness, abdominal pain, cough), in 'priority delay' when registration and triage tasks come before the ECG, in crowded departments, and in finding a private space. A triage protocol that sends the technician as soon as symptoms are named is what shortens the time.

Exam pearls

Exam tip Lead I up, aVF down, II equiphasic: physiological left axis, not deviation. Equiphasic lead: the axis is 90° away. Sokolow-Lyon uses V1 with V5 or V6; Cornell uses aVL with V3 and differs for women. Inferior STEMI: look at aVL for the reciprocal change. ST depression with tall R waves in V1–V3: posterior leads. ST elevation III higher than II, or in V1: right-sided leads. Diffuse concave elevation with PR depression and aVR the only depressed lead: pericarditis. Wellens T waves: report before any stress test. ST elevation in a symptomatic patient: report first, whatever the machine statement.
